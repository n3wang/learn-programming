Technical skill gets you into the room. Behavioral rounds decide whether people want to work with you — and for junior hires, coachability and enthusiasm often outweigh a perfect SQL answer.

## Why behavioral rounds matter

Many candidates treat behavioral questions as fluff. Companies that hire carefully do not. Large tech orgs (Amazon leadership principles, Uber bar-raiser culture rounds) and smaller teams with explicit values all use past behavior as a proxy for future fit. Ignoring this slice of the process is an easy way to lose an otherwise strong loop.

Behavioral assessment is especially high-stakes early in a data career. A company investing in a junior hire is often betting on months of ramp. Soft signals — how you learn, communicate, and recover from mistakes — can tip the decision when seniors in the pipeline look stronger on paper.

## When they happen

You rarely get a calendar invite titled “Behavioral Interview.” The assessment shows up everywhere:

- Recruiter screens (fail here and you never reach technical rounds)
- The first 5–10 minutes of a technical interview (“tell me about a project…”)
- Lunch or hallway conversations
- Closing chats with a hiring manager (or their manager) after they already know you can do the work

Assume you are always on: icebreakers count.

## What interviewers are testing

Three lenses, roughly:

| Lens | Question behind the question |
|---|---|
| **Culture fit** | Do your stories match the team’s mission and values? |
| **Soft skills** | Can you communicate with stakeholders, collaborate, and handle setbacks with a growth mindset? |
| **Position fit** | Do you actually want *this* role and team — or just any paycheck? |

Technical interviews ask whether you *can* do the job. Behavioral interviews ask whether you *want* to, and whether others will want you on the team.

## “Tell me about yourself”

This is the most common opener. A strong answer is short (about one to two minutes) and structured:

1. **Who you are** (role / background in one beat)
2. **How you got here** (path + one or two concrete accomplishments)
3. **What you want now** (this role + this company, in their language)

Start at a relevant point (often college for early-career candidates). Weave in phrases from the job description and values page. Rehearse out loud so the pitch is crisp, not a life history.

## “Why data science?”

Closely related to your pitch. If you switched fields, treat that as evidence you are deliberate and fast-learning. Map prior domain skills onto data work — modeling is only one slice of the job; analysis, communication, and product judgment all count.

## “Tell me about a time…” — prepare stories, not scripts

After the opener, situational questions dominate: setbacks, hard coworkers, unpopular decisions, proud moments. Data roles often add twists:

- When data changed a business decision
- When results contradicted expectations
- When you had to decide without the ideal dataset
- How you validated a hypothesis
- When you disagreed with a PM or engineer

Do not invent answers on the spot. Build a small bank of **stories** you can remix.

### STAR

| Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **S**ituation | Specific challenge or opportunity |
| **T**ask | Your goal |
| **A**ction | What *you* did (first person) |
| **R**esult | Outcome + lesson (failures are fine if you learned) |

Where you can, echo the job’s language (roadmap influence, stakeholder communication, “startup mentality,” customer focus). Practice under two minutes with a timer; delivery and body language matter alongside content.

## Project walk-throughs

Sometimes the interviewer picks a resume bullet and digs in: data collection and cleaning, model choice, metrics and baselines, deployment, technical blockers, stakeholder conflict, and “what would you redo?” Treat these as STAR stories anchored to one project — they are also a credibility check that you did the work you claimed.

## “Do you have any questions for us?”

Never answer with “no.” Until you have an offer, keep selling fit: ask questions that show research on the product and genuine curiosity. Prefer topics that make the interviewer talk about their work and the company’s challenges over early compensation or PTO. Prepare at least three solid questions per interviewer; LinkedIn context about their background helps.

## After the interview

Send a short thank-you email within a few hours. Reference something specific you discussed so they remember which candidate you were.
