  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.1"
    company="Uber"
    minWords={40}
    keywords={['sample mean', 'normal']}
    prompt={`Explain the Central Limit Theorem (CLT). Why is it useful in practice?`}
    answer={`The CLT says that for i.i.d. random variables $X_1,\\dots,X_n$ with mean $\\mu$ and variance $\\sigma^2$, the sample mean $\\bar X$ becomes approximately normal as $n$ grows, regardless of the shape of the underlying distribution: $\\bar X \\approx N\\!\\left(\\mu, \\frac{\\sigma^2}{n}\\right)$. It's useful because it lets you apply normal-based tools (z-scores, confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) to almost any metric — bookings, click-through rate, latency — as long as you have a reasonably large sample, without needing to know or assume the metric's true distribution.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.2"
    company="Facebook"
    minWords={35}
    keywords={['estimate', 'uncertainty']}
    prompt={`How would you explain a confidence interval to a non-technical audience?`}
    answer={`A confidence interval is a range around your best estimate that accounts for the uncertainty of only having a sample instead of the whole population. Saying "average height is 68 inches, 95% CI [67, 69]" means: if you repeated this survey many times, about 95% of the intervals computed that way would contain the true average height. A narrower interval means a more precise estimate; a wider one means more uncertainty, usually from a smaller sample or more variable data.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.3"
    company="Twitter"
    minWords={40}
    prompt={`What are some common pitfalls encountered in A/B testing?`}
    answer={`(1) Unbalanced groups — randomization that doesn't actually balance confounders like device or geography, so a "significant" result may just reflect a confound. (2) Stopping early — peeking at results and stopping as soon as they look significant inflates the false-positive rate, which is why a pre-registered sample size or duration matters. (3) Ignoring seasonality — running a short test on a metric with weekly or daily cycles can bias the estimate. (4) Multiple comparisons — testing many metrics or variants at once raises the chance something looks significant purely by chance, unless corrected for (e.g., Bonferroni).`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.4"
    company="Lyft"
    minWords={40}
    keywords={['covariance', 'correlation']}
    prompt={`Explain both covariance and correlation formulaically, and compare and contrast them.`}
    answer={`Covariance measures the direction of a linear relationship: $\\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X-E[X])(Y-E[Y])] = E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]$. It can be any real number, and its scale depends on the units of $X$ and $Y$, which makes magnitudes hard to compare across variable pairs. Correlation normalizes covariance by both standard deviations: $\\rho(X,Y) = \\frac{\\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\\sqrt{\\mathrm{Var}(X)\\mathrm{Var}(Y)}}$. It's dimensionless and always in $[-1,1]$, which makes it comparable across different pairs of variables, unlike raw covariance.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.5"
    company="Facebook"
    decimals={4}
    prompt={`You flip a coin 10 times and observe only 1 heads. The null hypothesis is that the coin is fair ($H_0: p=0.5$) against the one-sided alternative that it's biased toward tails. What is the p-value — the probability, under $H_0$, of getting exactly 1 heads in 10 flips?`}
    answer={0.0098}
    why={`With $n=10$ too small for the CLT, compute the exact binomial probability: $P(\\text{exactly 1 heads}) = \\binom{10}{1}(0.5)^{10} = 10/1024 \\approx 0.0098$. That's below a typical significance level of 0.05, so you'd reject $H_0$ and conclude the coin is likely biased toward tails.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.6"
    company="Uber"
    minWords={40}
    keywords={['null', 'p-value']}
    prompt={`Describe hypothesis testing and p-values in layman's terms.`}
    answer={`Hypothesis testing starts by assuming nothing interesting is happening (the null hypothesis, e.g., "this new button doesn't change conversion") and asks: if that were true, how surprising is the data we actually saw? The p-value is that "how surprising" number — the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as ours if the null hypothesis were really true. A small p-value (conventionally < 0.05) means the data would be quite surprising under the null, so we reject it in favor of the alternative. It does not mean "5% chance the null is true" — it's a statement about the data given the null, not about the null given the data.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.7"
    company="Groupon"
    minWords={35}
    keywords={['Type I', 'Type II']}
    prompt={`Describe what Type I and Type II errors are, and the trade-offs between them.`}
    answer={`Type I error (false positive) is rejecting a true null hypothesis — e.g., concluding a change helped when it didn't. Type II error (false negative) is failing to reject a false null — e.g., missing a real effect. Type I error rate is controlled by the significance level $\\alpha$ (often 0.05); Type II error rate is $\\beta$, and $1-\\beta$ is the test's power. The two trade off: making the test stricter (lower $\\alpha$) to cut false positives raises $\\beta$ for a fixed sample size, and vice versa. The only way to lower both simultaneously is to collect more data.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.8"
    company="Microsoft"
    minWords={35}
    keywords={['power', 'sample size']}
    prompt={`Explain the statistical background behind power.`}
    answer={`Power is $1-\\beta$, the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when the alternative is actually true — i.e., of detecting a real effect. It depends on the significance level $\\alpha$, the true effect size, the variance of the data, and the sample size $n$: larger effects, less noise, a looser $\\alpha$, or more data all raise power. Before running an experiment, you typically fix a target power (commonly 0.8) and a minimum detectable effect, then solve for the sample size $n$ needed to hit that power — the standard "power analysis" used to size an A/B test in advance.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.9"
    company="Facebook"
    minWords={40}
    keywords={['standard deviation', 't-test']}
    prompt={`What is a Z-test and when would you use it versus a t-test?`}
    answer={`Both test statistics compare a sample mean to a hypothesized value, standardized by its standard error, but they use different reference distributions. A Z-test assumes the population standard deviation $\\sigma$ is known (or $n$ is large, $n>200$, so the sample estimate is essentially exact) and compares the statistic to a standard normal. A t-test is used when $\\sigma$ is unknown and estimated from the sample, which adds extra uncertainty captured by the heavier-tailed Student's t-distribution ($n-1$ degrees of freedom). Either is valid once the sample mean is approximately normal — from the population being normal, or from the CLT with $n \\gtrsim 30$. For a proportion, use a Z-test (not a t-test) as long as $np_0 \\ge 10$ and $n(1-p_0)\\ge 10$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.10"
    company="Amazon"
    minWords={35}
    keywords={['multiple', 'Bonferroni']}
    prompt={`Say you are testing hundreds of hypotheses, each with a t-test. What considerations would you take into account when doing this?`}
    answer={`Running many hypothesis tests inflates the family-wise false-positive rate: with $m=100$ independent tests at $\\alpha=0.05$, you'd expect about 5 "significant" results by chance alone even if nothing real is happening. The standard fix is a multiple-comparisons correction, e.g., the Bonferroni correction, which tests each hypothesis at $\\alpha/m$ (here $0.05/100=0.0005$) so the overall false-positive rate across all $m$ tests stays near $\\alpha$. Bonferroni is conservative — it raises the Type II error rate — so it's most appropriate when only a few of the many hypotheses are expected to be true; with many true effects, a less conservative correction (e.g., controlling the false discovery rate) is often preferred.`}
  />
