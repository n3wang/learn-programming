    title="7.12"
    company="Affirm"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['partial dependence']}
    prompt={`You're running a binary classification loan model, and rejected applicants must be told why they were rejected. Without digging into feature weights, how would you supply these reasons?`}
    answer={`Use partial dependence plots (also called response curves). After the model is fit, pick one feature at a time, sweep its value across a range while holding every other feature fixed, and plot the model's predicted approval probability against that sweep — this shows the feature's marginal effect on the output without ever inspecting a weight directly. Concretely: if two applicants are identical on every feature except FICO score, and the lower-FICO applicant is rejected while the higher-FICO one is approved, you can point to FICO as the "all-else-equal" reason for the rejection. In practice you'd compute this for each feature the applicant might plausibly want explained (income, debt, number of credit cards, FICO), and report whichever feature(s) show the applicant sitting in an unfavorable region of the curve relative to the approval threshold.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.13"
    company="Google"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['embeddings']}
    prompt={`Given a very large corpus of words, how would you identify synonyms?`}
    answer={`Learn word embeddings from the corpus first — word2vec is the classic approach, producing a dense vector per word based on the contexts it appears in, such that words used in similar contexts end up close together in vector space (typically measured by cosine similarity or Euclidean distance). Once you have embeddings, either cluster them (k-means) and treat words in the same cluster as candidate synonyms, or, given a target word, run k-nearest-neighbors in embedding space to pull its closest vectors. One caveat worth flagging to an interviewer: word2vec optimizes for contextual similarity, not meaning, so it can put antonyms right next to each other — "hot" and "cold" show up in nearly identical contexts despite opposite meanings — so a nearest-neighbors pass over word embeddings is a strong starting point but usually needs a filtering or verification step before being trusted as true synonym detection.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.14"
    company="Facebook"
    minWords={20}
    keywords={['bias', 'variance']}
    prompt={`What is the bias-variance trade-off? How is it expressed using an equation?`}
    answer={`Total expected model error decomposes as $\\text{Error} = \\text{Bias}^2 + \\text{Variance} + \\text{Irreducible error}$. Bias is the error from a model being too simple to capture the true relationship — e.g. fitting a line to a curved relationship — and shows up as systematic underfitting. Variance is the error from a model being too sensitive to the particular training sample — e.g. an overly deep tree that reacts to noise — and shows up as overfitting: great training performance, poor generalization. Irreducible error is noise in the data-generating process that no model can remove. Flexible models (deep trees, high-degree polynomials, large neural nets) tend toward low bias / high variance; rigid models (linear regression, shallow trees) tend toward high bias / low variance. You want both terms small simultaneously, which is why the trade-off matters: pushing complexity up to cut bias raises variance, and vice versa, so model selection is really about finding the complexity level that minimizes the *sum*, not either term alone.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.15"
    company="Uber"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['fold']}
    prompt={`Define the cross-validation process. What is the motivation behind using it?`}
    answer={`k-fold cross-validation estimates how a model performs on unseen data using only the training set. Shuffle the data and split it into $k$ equally sized folds; for each fold $i = 1,\\dots,k$, train the model on all data except fold $i$, then evaluate it on the held-out fold $i$; finally average the $k$ resulting validation scores into a single estimate of true out-of-sample performance. This accomplishes two things: it avoids evaluating on the same rows you trained on (which would understate error and hide overfitting), and it avoids permanently sacrificing a dedicated validation set that could otherwise be used for training — valuable when data is scarce or expensive to collect. The trade-off is compute: cross-validation costs roughly $k\\times$ more training runs than a single holdout split, which matters less for small-to-medium datasets (where CV shines) than for very large ones or very expensive models (where a single holdout split is often preferred).`}
  />

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.17"
    company="Spotify"
    minWords={20}
    keywords={['collaborative filtering']}
    prompt={`How would you approach creating a music recommendation algorithm?`}
    answer={`Collaborative filtering is the natural starting point — using patterns in user-item interactions to drive recommendations, the same idea behind Netflix's movie recommendations. Music has a few twists versus movies, though: there's no explicit 1-5 star rating, songs get replayed many times (unlike a movie watched once), the catalog is far larger, and tastes run more niche. So instead of ratings, build a user-song matrix from an implicit signal — stream counts (or plays / skips) — and factorize it: with $M$ songs and $N$ users, find latent factor matrices $P$ and $Q$ such that $R \\approx PQ^\\top$, where a user's predicted affinity for a song is the dot product of their latent vector and the song's latent vector, $r_{us} = q_s^\\top p_u$. Alternating least squares (ALS), run in a distributed setting given the data scale, is a common way to solve this factorization. Once trained, recommend by sorting a user's unstreamed songs by predicted relevance, and reuse the same latent vectors for song-to-song or user-to-user similarity (e.g. k-nearest-neighbors in the latent space).`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.18"
    company="Amazon"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['local minimum']}
    prompt={`Define what it means for a function to be convex. Give an example of a machine learning algorithm whose objective is not convex, and explain why.`}
    answer={`A function $f$ is convex if, for any two points $x, y$ in its domain and any $a \\in [0,1]$: $f((1-a)x + ay) \\le (1-a)f(x) + af(y)$ — the line segment connecting any two points on the graph never dips below the graph itself. The payoff of convexity is that every local minimum is automatically a global minimum, which makes optimization far more tractable and reliable. Neural network training objectives are a canonical non-convex example: because a sufficiently large network is a universal function approximator, it must be able to represent non-convex target functions, so its own loss surface (as a function of the weights) is non-convex too — concretely, you can permute neurons within a hidden layer and get an identical input-output mapping with a different weight vector, so the loss surface has many equivalent (and many non-equivalent) local minima and saddle points rather than one global bowl.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="7.19"
    company="Microsoft"
    decimals={2}
    prompt={`You have 10 examples split evenly into two classes (5 of label a, 5 of label b). Splitting on an attribute $X$ puts 6 examples into the $X=1$ branch (5 a's, 1 b) and 4 examples into the $X=0$ branch (0 a's, 4 b's). What is the information gain $IG(X,Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\\mid X)$ of this split?`}
    answer={0.61}
    why={`Entropy is $H = -\\sum_k P(Y{=}k)\\log_2 P(Y{=}k)$. Before splitting, $Y$ is a 50/50 mix, so $H(Y) = -0.5\\log_2 0.5 - 0.5\\log_2 0.5 = 1$. The $X=0$ branch is pure (all b's), so $H(X{=}0)=0$. The $X=1$ branch is $\\tfrac56$ a's and $\\tfrac16$ b's: $H(X{=}1) = -\\tfrac56\\log_2\\tfrac56 - \\tfrac16\\log_2\\tfrac16 \\approx 0.65$. Weighting each branch's entropy by its share of the 10 examples: $H(Y\\mid X) = \\tfrac{4}{10}(0) + \\tfrac{6}{10}(0.65) \\approx 0.39$. So $IG = H(Y) - H(Y\\mid X) = 1 - 0.39 = 0.61$ bits — a good split, since it moved most of the way toward pure branches.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.20"
    company="Uber"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['sparse']}
    prompt={`What is L1 and L2 regularization? What are the differences between the two?`}
    answer={`Both penalize a model's loss $L$ by adding a term that shrinks the weights $w$ toward zero, controlled by a strength $\\lambda$. L2 (ridge) adds the squared magnitude of the weights: $\\text{Loss}_{L2} = L + \\lambda\\sum w_j^2$. L1 (lasso) adds their absolute value instead: $\\text{Loss}_{L1} = L + \\lambda\\sum |w_j|$. The practical difference shows up under gradient descent: L2's penalty gradient is proportional to $w$ itself, so as a weight nears zero the pull toward zero weakens — weights shrink smoothly but rarely hit exactly zero. L1's penalty gradient is a constant $\\pm\\lambda$ regardless of how small $w$ already is, so it keeps pushing right through zero and can zero a weight out completely. That makes L1 a built-in feature-selection tool (sparse models, some weights exactly zero), while L2 is better suited when you believe most features carry some signal and just want to tame their scale — L2 is also generally preferred when features are correlated, since it tends to shrink correlated coefficients together rather than arbitrarily picking one to keep.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.21"
    company="Amazon"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['stochastic']}
    prompt={`Describe gradient descent and the motivation behind stochastic gradient descent.`}
    answer={`Gradient descent minimizes an objective $f$ by repeatedly stepping opposite the gradient: $x^{t+1} = x^t - \\alpha \\nabla f(x^t)$, where $\\alpha$ is the learning rate (step size). Since most ML loss functions decompose as a sum over training examples, $\\nabla f(x) = \\frac{1}{n}\\sum_i \\nabla f_i(x)$, computing the exact gradient at every step means touching all $n$ examples — expensive at scale, and prone to stalling at saddle points or poor local minima. Stochastic gradient descent (SGD) instead estimates the gradient from a single (or mini-batch of) randomly chosen example each step: it's an unbiased estimate of the true gradient, since for i.i.d. data $E[\\nabla f_i(x)] = \\nabla f(x)$, and each step is far cheaper to compute. That makes SGD dramatically faster per step, better suited to huge datasets, and — thanks to the added noise — sometimes better at escaping shallow local minima, at the cost of a noisier, less direct path to convergence than full-batch gradient descent.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.22"
    company="Affirm"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['monotonic']}
    prompt={`A fraud classifier scores each application between 0 and 1. If you take the square root of every score, how does the ROC curve change? If it doesn't change, what kinds of transforms would change it?`}
    answer={`The ROC curve doesn't change. It's built entirely from the *relative ordering* of scores across thresholds — true-positive rate and false-positive rate at any given decision threshold depend only on which applications rank above or below that threshold, not on the scores' actual magnitudes. The square root is strictly increasing on $[0,1]$ ($y > x \\implies \\sqrt y > \\sqrt x$), so it preserves every pairwise ordering — only the numeric threshold values shift, not which applications end up classified as fraud at a matched operating point. Any monotonically increasing transform (e.g. squaring positive scores, a log transform, or any calibration curve that never decreases) leaves the ROC curve unchanged for the same reason. What *would* change it is a transform that isn't monotonic — reversing order (e.g. $f(x)=-x$), a transform that isn't strictly increasing everywhere (e.g. $f(x)=-x^2$, which reverses order for negative inputs), or a step function that collapses distinct scores together and can create ties that didn't exist before.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.23"
    company="IBM"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['Gaussian']}
    prompt={`If $X$ is a univariate Gaussian random variable, what is the entropy of $X$?`}
    answer={`For $X \\sim N(\\mu, \\sigma^2)$, differential entropy is $H(X) = -\\int p(x)\\log p(x)\\,dx$. Substituting the Gaussian density and splitting the log of the product $p(x) = \\frac{1}{\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi}}e^{-(x-\\mu)^2/2\\sigma^2}$ into two integrals: the first term becomes $\\log(\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi})$ (using $\\int p(x)\\,dx = 1$), and the second reduces to $\\frac{1}{2\\sigma^2}E[(X-\\mu)^2] = \\frac12$ (the definition of variance, divided by $\\sigma^2$). Adding them: $H(X) = \\frac12 + \\log\\!\\left(\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi}\\right)$ nats. Notably, this depends only on $\\sigma$, not $\\mu$ — shifting a Gaussian's location doesn't change its "spread of uncertainty," only rescaling it does. (Try the numeric compute problem below for the standard-normal case, $\\sigma=1$.)`}

  <Problem
    title="7.23b"
    company="IBM"
    decimals={2}
    prompt={`Using $H(X) = \\tfrac12 + \\log(\\sigma\\sqrt{2\\pi})$ (natural log, in nats) for a Gaussian, what is the entropy of a standard normal $X \\sim N(0,1)$?`}
    answer={1.42}
    why={`With $\\sigma=1$: $H(X) = \\tfrac12 + \\ln(\\sqrt{2\\pi}) = 0.5 + 0.5\\ln(2\\pi) \\approx 0.5 + 0.9189 = 1.42$ nats. Since $\\sigma=1$ drops out of the log term's argument entirely except for the constant $\\sqrt{2\\pi}$, this is the *minimum* entropy among all Gaussians with $\\sigma \\ge 1$ — entropy only grows as $\\sigma$ grows (a wider spread is "more uncertain"), via the $\\log\\sigma$ term.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.24"
    company="Stitch Fix"
    minWords={15}
    keywords={['propensity']}
    prompt={`How would you build a model to calculate a customer's propensity to buy a particular item? What are some pros and cons of your approach?`}
    answer={`Build a propensity model as a binary classifier: assemble a dataset of past users with covariates (age, past purchase history, browsing behavior, income proxy, etc.) and a label for whether they purchased the item (or item category), then fit a model predicting purchase probability. Logistic regression is a natural first choice — fast, interpretable log-odds output, easy to explain to merchandising stakeholders — but it can't capture feature interactions well and gets numerically unstable with correlated covariates or a small user base. A neural net or SVM captures richer interactions but costs interpretability and needs more data to avoid overfitting. Tree-based ensembles (random forest, gradient boosting) are often the practical middle ground: strong accuracy, and feature importances that double as merchandising insight (which signals actually predict purchase intent). Whatever the model, its quality is capped by feature engineering and data quality — sparse or biased purchase history (e.g. new customers) will visibly degrade propensity estimates for those segments.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="7.25"
    company="Citadel"
    minWords={20}
    keywords={['generative', 'discriminative']}
    prompt={`Compare and contrast Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) and logistic regression. When would you use one over the other?`}
    answer={`Both are linear classifiers, but they get there differently: logistic regression is discriminative, directly modeling $P(Y\\mid X)$, while GNB is generative, modeling $P(X\\mid Y)$ and $P(Y)$ and combining them via Bayes' rule — and in fact GNB's implied $P(Y\\mid X)$ has the same functional form as logistic regression's, just with different (indirectly estimated) parameters. GNB's big advantage is data efficiency: because it estimates simple per-feature, per-class distributions rather than jointly optimizing weights, it trains fast and works with very little data, and its parameters (class-conditional means/variances) are easy to interpret. Its weakness is the conditional-independence assumption between features given the class, which is frequently false in practice and can hurt calibration when features are correlated. Logistic regression has no such independence assumption and gives directly interpretable feature effects on the log-odds, but it requires numerical optimization (no closed form), is comparatively data-hungry, and — being less flexible than a fully joint model — can underfit or overfit more easily when training data is scarce. Net: prefer GNB when training data is limited or the conditional-independence assumption is roughly reasonable; prefer logistic regression once you have enough data and the extra flexibility is worth the optimization overhead.`}
