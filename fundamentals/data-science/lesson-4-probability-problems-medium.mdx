  <Problem
    title="5.19"
    company="Morgan Stanley"
    prompt={`A number $k$ from 1–6 is chosen. Players A and B take turns rolling a fair die (A first), and whoever first rolls $k$ wins 100 dollars. How much (in dollars) should A be willing to pay for the right to go first?`}
    answer={9.09}
    why={`Let $P(A)$ be A's win probability going first: $P(A) = \\frac{1}{6} + \\frac{5}{6}(1-P(A))$, since if A misses, B faces the identical game going first. Solving gives $P(A)=6/11$, so $P(B)=5/11$. A's edge from going first is worth $100(6/11-5/11) = 100/11 \\approx 9.09$ dollars.`}

  <Problem
    title="5.20"
    company="Airbnb"
    prompt={`You have a coin with an unknown, possibly unfair bias $p$ toward heads. Von Neumann's trick flips it twice: call the result "heads" on HT, "tails" on TH, and discard/reflip on HH or TT — this is fair for any $p$. For $p = 0.3$, what is the probability that a single two-flip attempt produces a usable (non-discarded) result?`}
    answer={0.42}
    why={`$P(\\text{HT}) = p(1-p)$ and $P(\\text{TH}) = (1-p)p$ are always equal — that equality is why the trick is fair regardless of bias. A pair is usable when it's HT or TH: $2p(1-p) = 2(0.3)(0.7) = 0.42$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.21"
    company="SIG"
    prompt={`A cube is cut into a 3×3×3 grid of 27 unit cubes after all 6 outer faces were painted green. You pick one of the 27 small cubes at random and see 5 of its faces are white (the 6th, hidden "bottom" face is unknown). What is the probability the bottom face is also white?`}
    answer={0.5}
    why={`Only two cube types can show 5 white faces: the 1 interior cube (0 green faces — bottom is white for sure) or one of the 6 face-center cubes (exactly 1 green face, which must be the hidden bottom — a $1/6$ chance per random orientation). $P(\\text{observe}) = \\frac{1}{27}(1) + \\frac{6}{27}\\left(\\frac16\\right) = \\frac{2}{27}$. By Bayes, $P(\\text{interior} \\mid \\text{observe}) = \\frac{1/27}{2/27} = \\frac12$ — only that case has a white bottom, so the answer is $1/2$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.22"
    company="Goldman Sachs"
    prompt={`A stick of length 1 is broken at two independent, uniformly random points into three pieces. What is the probability the three pieces can form a triangle?`}
    answer={0.25}
    why={`A triangle is impossible if any one piece exceeds $1/2$ (triangle inequality fails). Splitting on the stick's midpoint: the "both breaks on the same side" case (probability $1/2$) and one "different sides, wrong order" case (probability $1/4$) both fail to form a triangle, leaving $1 - 1/2 - 1/4 = 1/4$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.23"
    company="Lyft"
    prompt={`You flip a fair coin repeatedly. What is the probability that the pattern HHT appears before the pattern HTT?`}
    answer={0.75}
    why={`Once the first H appears: an immediate second H (probability $1/2$) guarantees HHT completes next. Otherwise a T follows (probability $1/2$): a further T (probability $1/2$) completes HTT, or a further H (probability $1/2$) resets to "just saw H". Solving $p = \\frac12 + \\frac12\\cdot\\frac12\\cdot p$ gives $p = 3/4$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.24"
    company="Uber"
    prompt={`A fair coin is tossed twice. What is $P(\\text{both heads} \\mid \\text{at least one toss was heads})$?`}
    answer={0.33}
    why={`Sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}; conditioning on "at least one head" leaves {HH, HT, TH}, so $P = 1/3 \\approx 0.33$. By contrast $P(\\text{both heads} \\mid \\text{second toss is heads}) = 1/2$ is larger, and this ordering holds for any coin (fair or biased): conditioning on the more specific "second toss" event removes less of the "both heads" probability mass than conditioning on "at least one".`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.25"
    company="Facebook"
    prompt={`Three ants sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle. Each independently picks one of the two directions along the triangle's edges and starts walking. What is the probability that no two ants ever meet?`}
    answer={0.25}
    why={`Ants collide unless all three pick the same rotational direction (all clockwise or all counterclockwise). Each of those two outcomes has probability $(1/2)^3$, so $P(\\text{no collision}) = 2(1/2)^3 = 1/4$. For $k$ ants on a $k$-gon, the same logic gives $2(1/2)^k = (1/2)^{k-1}$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.26"
    company="Robinhood"
    prompt={`A coin with $P(\\text{heads}) = p = 0.3$ is tossed $n$ times. The probability of an even number of heads follows $P_n = (1-p)P_{n-1} + p(1-P_{n-1})$ with $P_0 = 1$. What is $P_4$?`}
    answer={0.51}
    why={`The recurrence conditions on the last toss: tails (probability $1-p$) keeps the parity, heads (probability $p$) flips it. It solves in closed form to $P_n = \\frac{1+(1-2p)^n}{2}$. With $p=0.3$: $P_4 = \\frac{1+0.4^4}{2} = \\frac{1.0256}{2} \\approx 0.5128 \\to 0.51$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.27"
    company="Citadel"
    prompt={`Alice and Bob play rounds until one of them is two rounds ahead; that player wins the series. Alice wins each round with probability $p = 0.4$. What is the probability that Bob wins the series?`}
    answer={0.69}
    why={`Let $q = 1-p = 0.6$. Conditioning on the next two rounds: Bob wins outright with probability $q^2$, Alice wins outright with probability $p^2$, and with probability $2pq$ the score resets to even. So $P(\\text{Bob}) = q^2 + 2pq\\,P(\\text{Bob})$, giving $P(\\text{Bob}) = \\frac{q^2}{1-2pq} = \\frac{0.36}{0.52} \\approx 0.69$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="5.28"
    company="Google"
    prompt={`You draw three independent values uniformly from $(0,2)$. What is the probability that their median exceeds 1.5?`}
    answer={0.16}
    why={`The median exceeds 1.5 iff at least 2 of the 3 draws exceed 1.5. Each draw exceeds 1.5 with probability $q = 0.25$. $P(\\text{at least 2 of 3}) = \\binom{3}{2}q^2(1-q) + q^3 = 3(0.0625)(0.75) + 0.015625 = 0.15625 \\to 0.16$.`}
  />
