## Linear algebra

The linear-algebra topic that comes up most often is **eigenvalues and eigenvectors**. For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $x$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$ when:

$$
Ax = \lambda x
$$

Geometrically, $A$ is a linear map. On an eigenvector it only **scales** $x$ (same direction, factor $\lambda$).

Writing a square matrix in terms of its eigenvectors is an **eigendecomposition**. Not every matrix is square; non-square matrices use **singular value decomposition (SVD)**:

$$
A = U \Sigma V^{\top}
$$

with $U$ ($m \times m$), $\Sigma$ ($m \times n$), and $V$ ($n \times n$).

ML uses this toolkit constantly: matrix multiplies in backprop, eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix in PCA, projections, inverses, determinants, orthonormal bases, and diagonalization. Research / quant loops may ask you to whiteboard follow-ups on PCA or linear regression from these building blocks.

## Gradient descent

Training minimizes an **objective** (loss / cost): how badly the model fits the data. Lower is better. **Optimization** methods search for good parameters.

**Gradient descent** repeatedly steps opposite the gradient — the direction of steepest increase — so each step goes “downhill”:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t - \alpha\, \nabla f(x_t)
$$

Here $\alpha > 0$ is the **learning rate**. For convex losses, iterating until successive $x$ values barely change means **convergence** near a minimum.

Many losses are sums over examples, so the full gradient is a sum of per-example gradients. Computing that every step is expensive, and non-convex landscapes have local minima and saddles. Variants:

| Method | Data per step | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Batch GD | Full training set | Stable gradient; costly |
| Stochastic GD (SGD) | One example (classic) | Noisy; escapes flats; unbiased gradient estimate |
| Mini-batch GD | Small fixed batch | Common practical middle ground |

## Model evaluation and selection

**Evaluation:** after fitting on a **train** set, score the model on held-out **test** data. A common split is roughly 80% train / 20% test. Generalization — performance on unseen data — is what matters.

**Selection:** after evaluating candidates, pick the model to ship, under product and business constraints. Tiny metric lifts can be huge in production (e.g. ad CTR), so interview case studies often turn into “compare models and choose.”

When data is scarce, a single 80/20 cut is noisy — that is where **cross-validation** (next section) earns its keep. Until then, treat “held-out performance” as the north star.

## Bias–variance trade-off

We try to learn a function $f(x)$ that predicts $y$ from $x$:

$$
y = f(x) + \varepsilon
$$

with noise $\varepsilon$ (often modeled as zero-mean Gaussian in regression). Prediction error decomposes into:

1. **Bias** — how far average predictions sit from the true $f$ (lower is better)
2. **Variance** — how much predictions swing when the training set changes (lower is better)
3. **Irreducible error** — noise you cannot remove

**High bias / low variance** (e.g. a too-simple linear model): systematically off, but stable.  
**Low bias / high variance** (e.g. an over-flexible net): can track $f$ closely but thrash with different samples.

Interviews rarely demand the full algebra; they want situational reasoning: more data / regularization for high variance; richer features / more flexible models for high bias — always tied to product needs.

## Overfitting, underfitting, and complexity

George Box: “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” We want models that **generalize**. Occam’s razor in ML: simpler, more parsimonious models often travel better out of sample.

**Overfitting:** train fit looks great because the model absorbed noise; test performance suffers.  
**Underfitting:** the model never captured the real structure (often high bias).

Interviewers ask how you **detect** overfitting (train≪test gap, CV scores, **learning curves**) and how you **fight** it — which leads to regularization.

## Regularization

Regularization shrinks effective complexity: usually **lower variance**, slight **higher bias**. Penalties added to the objective are also called **shrinkage** methods.

| Method | Penalty idea | Typical effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **L1 (lasso)** | $\lambda \sum \|w_i\|$ | Many weights → exactly 0 (sparse / feature selection) |
| **L2 (ridge)** | $\lambda \sum w_i^2$ | Shrinks weights; rarely exact zeros |
| **Elastic net** | Mix of L1 + L2 | Sparse + grouped-shrinkage blend |

Use L1 when you want sparsity; L2 when you want smooth shrinkage of correlated features; elastic net when you want both flavors.

## Model training

Evaluation tells you *which* model won. **Training** is how you teach candidates in the first place. The 80/20 split is only the start: cross-validation, resampling, hyperparameter search, and learning curves decide whether that win was luck or signal.

### Cross-validation

**Cross-validation (CV)** estimates performance by rotating which slice of the training data is held out. For each subsample you train on most of the data, score on the left-out block, then combine the scores. That avoids “always testing the same lucky 20%” and is especially valuable when more labeled data is expensive.

**$k$-fold CV** (classic interview ask):

1. Shuffle into $k$ equal folds.
2. For each fold $i$: train on all folds except $i$; validate on fold $i$.
3. Average the $k$ validation errors → estimate of true error.

**Leave-one-out CV (LOOCV)** is $k$-fold with $k = n$ (one point held out each round). Thorough, but $n$ full retrainings — painful on large $n$.

On **big** datasets, full CV can be too expensive. Prefer a three-way split: **train / validation (dev) / test**, with the val set often ~10–20%. Use val for model / hyperparameter choices; keep test sealed for a final report.

**Time series:** do **not** shuffle into random $k$-folds — that leaks the future into the past. Train only on history up to a cutoff, validate the next window, then slide / expand the cutoff forward.

### Bootstrapping and bagging

**Bootstrapping:** sample **with replacement** from your data, estimate a quantity on each draw, then average (or otherwise aggregate) those estimates. Useful when $n$ is small, and for **rare classes** (oversample the minority via bootstrap draws).

**Bagging** (bootstrap aggregation) builds many models on bootstrap samples and averages their predictions — the engine behind **random forests**. Ensembles (RF, AdaBoost, XGBoost) are interview favorites; a classic compare/contrast is “random forest vs XGBoost” (bagged parallel trees vs boosted sequential trees — details in [Random Forests & Boosting](./lesson-26-random-forests-and-boosting)).

### Hyperparameter tuning

**Hyperparameters** (learning rate, tree depth, regularization strength, …) shape training time, cost, and generalization. You set them *outside* the core parameter updates.

| Method | Idea | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Grid search** | Cartesian product of candidate values; try all | Exhaustive but cost grows exponentially in #hparams |
| **Random search** | Sample each hparam from a distribution | Explores large spaces cheaper; no optimality guarantee |
| **Bayesian optimization** | Model which regions look promising; sample there | Smarter sequential search (common for NNs / boosting) |

Interview bar: name a few hyperparameters for a model you like and what each does to fit vs generalize. Deep Bayes-opt math is rarer outside research / MLE loops.

### Training time and learning curves

Training cost scales with **$n$** (rows) and **$d$** (features / parameters). Big-O sketches (coding chapter) help compare algorithms; in production you also weigh wall-clock and infra cost against a tiny metric lift.

**Learning curves** plot a metric (accuracy, loss, …) against experience (iterations or dataset size). Overlay **train** and **validation**:

- Train ↑ while val stalls / worsens → **overfitting** (early stop, regularize, simplify).
- Both stuck low → **underfitting**.
- Wide gap that never shrinks → data may be **non-representative** (or leakage / mismatch).

Classic interview prompt: “How do you tell the model is overfitting?” — answer with learning curves + train/val gap, not only vibes.
