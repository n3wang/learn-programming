> **Source:** Problem prompts adapted from *Ace the Data Science Interview* — Chapter 10 (“Product Sense”) (Huo & Singh). Model answers and frameworks here are original for this course.

Eighteen product-sense interview questions — metrics definition, root-cause diagnosis, A/B testing pitfalls, pricing, and feature brainstorms. Every one of these is a "talk it out" prompt (no numeric answers), so they all use the write-your-answer format: type your answer, **Check requirements**, **Show model answer**, then **Mark as reviewed**. [Product Sense](./lesson-35-product-sense) covers the underlying frameworks (AARRR, metric quality, diagnosis steps, A/B pitfalls) if a prompt feels unfamiliar.

Hints: these questions reward structure over cleverness — clarify the goal and stakeholders before naming a single metric, and always pair a headline number with a guardrail / counter-metric. Interviewers are listening for *how* you'd find out, not just a guessed final answer.

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.1"
    company="Facebook"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['degree']}
    prompt={`Imagine the social graphs for both Facebook and Twitter. How do they differ? What metrics would you use to measure how these social graphs differ?`}
    answer={`Start from how people actually use each product: Facebook connections are mutual friendships, while Twitter connections are one-directional follows, often of an influential account that never follows back. Modeled as a graph — users as nodes, relationships as edges — this means Twitter has a small number of very high-degree "hub" nodes (celebrities, media accounts) and a huge number of low-degree nodes, a hub-and-spoke shape, while Facebook's degree distribution is comparatively more uniform since friendship requires mutual consent. To quantify the difference: compare the average degree per node on each platform, plot the full degree distribution (Twitter's should show much heavier right skew), and measure reciprocity — the fraction of edges that go both directions — which should be far higher on Facebook than Twitter. Box-and-whisker plots of degree, and a skewness/kurtosis statistic on each distribution, make the comparison concrete rather than just qualitative.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.2"
    company="Uber"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['supply', 'demand']}
    prompt={`Why does surge pricing exist? What metrics would you track to ensure that surge pricing was working effectively?`}
    answer={`Surge pricing corrects a local supply/demand imbalance — too few drivers or too many riders in an area — by raising prices, which both pulls in more drivers (higher pay) and cools demand (some riders wait or skip the ride). Beyond the obvious riders and drivers, stakeholders include support, PR, and finance, since a poorly tuned surge algorithm creates real business risk. Surge-specific metrics to track: surge duration, the multiplier itself, and the number of riders/drivers in the affected area. Around any surge period, also track rides taken vs. cancelled, revenue and profit for both Uber and drivers, and average wait time, alongside topline guardrails like rider LTV, driver retention, and DAU — surge shouldn't quietly be eroding those. A counter-metric like NPS or support-ticket volume matters too: surge can feel like price-gouging to riders, and the recurring "drunk $800 Uber ride" news story is exactly the PR risk a healthy-surge metric should catch early.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.3"
    company="Airbnb"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['booking flow']}
    prompt={`What factors might make A/B testing metrics on the Airbnb platform difficult?`}
    answer={`Three structural issues. First, the booking flow is complex and partly outside Airbnb's control — some bookings go through host messaging and negotiation, others are instant, so a test window is hard to size and the resulting data is noisy; the fix is to focus on the core search-to-booking conversion rate rather than intermediate steps, and to be disciplined about event logging. Second, a booking often involves multiple people and multiple devices (a family planning a trip), which breaks the clean one-user-to-one-device assumption A/B assignment relies on — mitigated with better identity/device-graph resolution, while accepting some irreducible noise. Third, the actual outcome that matters — a happy stay — happens weeks after the test exposure, and bookings are a low-frequency event for most users, so long-run metrics like retention or LTV can't be observed directly within a normal test window; the practical workaround is to build a model that predicts the long-run metric from short-run behavior, and evaluate the A/B test against movement in those predictive short-run features instead.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.4"
    company="Google"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['upper bound']}
    prompt={`Google currently pays the Mozilla foundation nine figures per year to be the default search engine on Firefox, and Mozilla is now asking for twice that. Should Google take the deal? How would you estimate the upper bound on what Google should be willing to pay?`}
    answer={`The stakes go well beyond the immediate search-ad revenue at risk. Losing the Firefox default could hand meaningful share and investment momentum to Bing or a privacy-branded competitor like DuckDuckGo (a real branding risk, since Firefox itself is positioned around privacy), and it would weaken Google's vertical-search ecosystem — Google Shopping and Google Maps lose their default placement, ceding queries to Amazon, Expedia, and Yelp. There's also a network-effects argument: more search volume improves Google's ranking quality and feeds downstream products like Reviews and Maps, so losing volume has compounding costs beyond one line of the P&L. For an upper bound, start from first principles: estimate the search-ad revenue Google earns from Firefox users today, then estimate what fraction of that revenue would actually be lost if Google weren't the default — calibrated using browsers where Google isn't already the default, to see what share of users manually switch back anyway. That gives a realistic (not worst-case) revenue-at-risk number; add a qualitative buffer for the harder-to-quantify strategic/defensive value, and sanity-check the range against precedent (Google's prior Firefox payment, and its roughly $10B/year Apple/Safari deal scaled by relative install base) without treating precedent as the primary method.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.5"
    company="LinkedIn"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['engagement']}
    prompt={`Assume you were working on LinkedIn's Feed. What metrics would you use to track engagement? What product ideas do you have to improve these engagement metrics?`}
    answer={`Feed exists to keep members checking LinkedIn often enough that the platform stays useful even when they aren't actively job-hunting, which in turn supports ad and sponsored-job revenue. Metrics, from top-level down: DAU/WAU/MAU on Feed and L7/L28 (days active per week/month) for frequency, plus average session time for depth. To capture the quality of engagement rather than just presence, build a single weighted engagement score from posts seen/liked/commented/shared (weighting a comment above a like, say), a parallel content-creation score (posts, comments, shares made), and separately track feed-ad impressions and clicks since that's the direct revenue link — filtering all of it for bot/scraper traffic. Product ideas to move these: better personalization in the ranking model, new post formats (LinkedIn Live, Stories) to keep the content inventory fresh, and new reaction types to lower the friction of engaging. Every idea should go through an A/B test against the core engagement metrics, watched holistically — a spike in time spent that comes from more low-quality content sneaking through isn't actually a win.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.6"
    company="Lyft"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['stratified', 'network effects']}
    prompt={`Your team is trying to figure out whether a new rider app with extra UI features would increase the number of rides taken. For an A/B test, how would you split users and ensure the test has balanced groups?`}
    answer={`A naive coin-flip split risks unbalanced groups on things like demographics or location that also drive ride volume, so use stratified random sampling to balance treatment and control on those known confounders rather than pure randomization. But there's a bigger issue specific to a marketplace like Lyft: network effects. If half of riders in, say, New York get the new UI and it genuinely increases ride requests, that half now competes harder for the same local driver pool — inflating (or deflating, if the feature is bad) the measured effect beyond what a stable market would show, and contaminating the control group's driver availability too. The standard fix is to randomize at the market level instead of the individual level, comparing matched, historically-comparable cities rather than riders within one shared market. That approach has its own weaknesses worth naming — two markets are never perfectly independent, and external shocks (a competitor promo, local regulation) can make them diverge mid-test — so validate the split by checking that metrics unrelated to the feature stay similar across the two markets before trusting the result.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.7"
    company="Amazon"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['right-skewed']}
    prompt={`If you were to plot the average revenue per seller on the Amazon marketplace, what would the shape of the distribution look like?`}
    answer={`Right-skewed, following something close to a Pareto (80/20) pattern: a large mass of small sellers doing modest revenue, and a long thin tail of a relatively few power sellers generating an outsized share of total marketplace revenue. This is the same shape you see in income distribution (top 20% earning roughly 80% of income) or in software bug reports (a small share of bugs causing most crashes) — heavy-tailed, not the symmetric bell curve a first instinct might reach for.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.8"
    company="Facebook"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['false positive', 'false negative']}
    prompt={`Besides posts Facebook is legally obligated to remove, what other types of posts should Facebook take down? What features would you use to identify these posts? What trade-offs need to be considered when removing them?`}
    answer={`Beyond legally mandated takedowns (child exploitation, IP violations), candidates include explicit content, hate speech and direct threats, misinformation (health, elections), content from coordinated bad-actor networks, regulated-goods sales (weapons, narcotics), and scams. For milder cases, a warning label linking to a fact-check is often better than an outright takedown, preserving expression while limiting harm. Detection features fall into three buckets: content (curse words, nudity/hate-speech classifiers, sensitive keywords), entity (fake-account signals, unverified contact info, a history of prior takedowns, links to other known bad actors via shared IP/device), and context (how much surrounding spam or bad-actor activity exists in that group or thread — harmful behavior rarely happens in isolation). The core trade-off is false positives vs. false negatives: wrongly removing a benign post angers and alienates users, can look like political censorship, and sometimes escalates into regulatory scrutiny; wrongly leaving harmful content up causes real harm, spreads misinformation to people who never saw the original post, and creates a different kind of PR and brand-trust damage. In practice this means calibrating sensitivity per content type by downside risk, and keeping a human in the loop for high-profile accounts, where an automated false positive is especially costly.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.9"
    company="Amazon"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['correlation', 'causation']}
    prompt={`The Amazon books team finds that books with more complete author profiles sell more. A team scrapes Wikipedia and Goodreads to automatically fill in more author information, hoping to lift sales, but sales don't change. Why might this be?`}
    answer={`Correlation isn't causation — the original observation likely reflects a confound rather than the profile completeness itself. Books with fuller author profiles are plausibly published by larger, more established publishers, who also invest more in cover design, marketing spend, and placement — the actual drivers of sales — with a complete profile just being a side effect of that publisher's overall polish. Auto-filling the profile field copies the correlated symptom, not the underlying cause, so it does nothing to sales. The fix is to test this properly: run a randomized experiment across eligible books rather than relying on the observational pattern, and ideally isolate profile completeness from the confounds (publisher tier, marketing spend, cover quality) it was riding alongside.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.10"
    company="Snapchat"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['root cause']}
    prompt={`Snapchat saw an overall 5% decrease in daily active users, a trend consistent over the week. How would you go about determining the root cause?`}
    answer={`Follow a four-step diagnosis framework. First, scope the change: what exactly counts as "active" here, is the drop sudden or gradual, what's it measured against (last week, last month, last year), and is 5% actually unusual once typical week-to-week variance and seasonality (post-holiday lulls, etc.) are accounted for. Second, hypothesize in buckets: an accidental logging/pipeline break, an upstream issue (login flow, push notifications), a recent internal product change, or an external event. Third, validate each bucket — confirm the logging pipeline is healthy with the data-eng/SRE team, check login and password-reset volume and uninstall rate for upstream issues, check usage of any recently changed feature, and segment by market/OS/device to surface external or localized causes. Fourth, classify what you find: say the drop turns out to concentrate almost entirely in iOS users who upgraded to the newest app version, alongside a spike in logins and password resets that same day — that combination points to a login bug shipped in that release, not a real decline in interest, and the fix is engineering, not product strategy.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.11"
    company="Pinterest"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['nDCG']}
    prompt={`You ship a new search ranking algorithm on Pinterest. What metrics would you use to measure the impact of this change?`}
    answer={`First clarify the change's stated goal (more trending content? better niche discoverability? more personalization?) and whether it's visible in the UI or purely a backend re-ranking, since that shapes which metrics even make sense. Search matters to Pinterest both as its core discovery-engine value prop and, increasingly, as the front door to shoppable pins and e-commerce revenue. Direct engagement metrics — searches per session, time spent searching — are a starting point, but they're ambiguous on their own: more searching can just as easily mean users are struggling to find what they want as it can mean they're happily exploring. So pair them with downstream signals that indicate a search actually succeeded: search-to-pin-save rate, and for buyable pins, search-to-purchase revenue. For a more rigorous read, information-retrieval metrics like precision@10 (share of the top 10 results judged relevant) or normalized discounted cumulative gain, which additionally rewards putting the most relevant pins higher in the ranking rather than just anywhere in the top 10, directly grade result quality rather than relying on engagement as a proxy.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.12"
    company="Netflix"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['supply', 'demand']}
    prompt={`A category like sci-fi TV shows has less total watch time than other similar categories. What metrics would you look at to tell whether this is a demand problem (people aren't interested) or a supply problem (there's interest, but the content is bad)?`}
    answer={`Total watch time alone conflates catalog size with interest — fewer sci-fi titles will mechanically produce less total watch time even if each show is loved, so look at watch time per show relative to other categories first: high per-show watch time despite low totals points to supply (not enough inventory), not lack of demand. From there, check funnel and quality signals: are people browsing into sci-fi titles but not hitting play (interest exists, nothing catches them — a discovery or thumbnail/trailer problem), what's the season-1 completion rate versus other genres (low completion suggests weak content, not weak interest), and how do user ratings compare across categories. Segment everything by country and language, since a gap that looks like weak demand in aggregate might really be a localization gap (dubbing/subtitles) concentrated outside English-speaking markets. It's also worth checking a level higher in the funnel — maybe sci-fi content just isn't surfacing in recommendations or getting marketing budget, which would look like low demand without actually being one — and benchmarking against external data (e.g. audience-measurement firms) to see whether the softness is Netflix-specific or true of the genre industry-wide.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.13"
    company="Apple"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['segmentation']}
    prompt={`You have data on millions of Apple customers and their purchases at physical retail stores. How could customer segmentation analysis increase a store's sales performance? What techniques would you use to segment brick-and-mortar customers?`}
    answer={`Fully personalizing to each individual walking into a store isn't feasible, and treating every customer identically ignores real differences in what they need — segmentation is the practical middle ground, letting Apple tailor pitches, staffing, and store layout to groups at scale. One useful axis is tech-savviness, which changes the right sales pitch (a developer buying a MacBook needs a very different conversation than a non-technical buyer) and can justify having a technical staff member available in-store. Another is primary purchase type: if segmentation reveals iPhone buyers convert to AirPods purchases far more often than MacBook buyers or Genius Bar repair customers, that's a direct signal to place AirPods near iPhones, train staff to upsell AirPods specifically at iPhone checkout, and consider a discounted iPhone-plus-AirPods bundle. Technique-wise, k-means clustering on transaction data works well, with the elbow or silhouette method to choose the number of segments; where possible, join in each customer's online Apple.com purchase history too, since transaction-only segmentation misses non-Apple customers and gives an incomplete picture even of existing ones.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.14"
    company="Facebook"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['confound']}
    prompt={`70% of Facebook users on iOS also use Instagram, but only 50% of Facebook users on Android also use Instagram. How would you go about identifying the underlying reasons for this discrepancy?`}
    answer={`Start by pulling basic demographic and usage-intensity cuts for both platforms' users on both apps — age, location, overall time spent in the Facebook ecosystem — since a confound as simple as "iOS users skew younger" or "iOS users are more engaged with Facebook overall, which funnels them into Instagram too" could explain a lot on its own. Next check technical/device factors: is the Instagram app meaningfully larger on Android, does it require a recent OS version that fewer Android devices are running, and how does raw install friction compare. Then check app-quality parity directly — app-store ratings, crash rate, feed-scroll latency, and bug-report volume for Instagram on each platform — since Android under-investment in the Instagram app specifically (as opposed to Facebook, which might be equally polished on both) would show up here. Given how large this gap is and how many users it spans, this is also a case where pure SQL-based cuts likely won't find the whole answer — loop in UX research and the Android/iOS platform leads for both apps, since a gap this size across this many users often has a structural or strategic root cause that data alone won't fully surface.`}

  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.15"
    company="Capital One"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['retention', 'churn']}
    prompt={`How would you assess the stickiness of the Capital One Quicksilver credit card?`}
    answer={`A card's stickiness is how frequently and durably holders keep using it — the bank profits from interest and transaction fees the more a card gets used, which is exactly what a rewards program (cash back, perks) is designed to encourage; without those incentives, usage would likely drop. Useful metrics: a daily-to-monthly active-spender ratio (what fraction of monthly spenders also spend daily — higher means stickier), month-over-month cohort retention (grouping holders by signup month and tracking what fraction remain active spenders over time, which also captures "silent churn" — an account that isn't closed but has effectively gone dormant, common since many people keep a card reserved for one specific spend category like travel), and transaction-volume retention (comparing this month's total spend from last month's spenders against last month's total spend), which weights stickiness by revenue rather than treating every holder equally.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="10.16"
    company="Google"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['pricing']}
    prompt={`You worked on YouTube Premium, an ad-free YouTube bundled with YouTube Music. Launching in a few new countries, how would you determine pricing for each country?`}
    answer={`First clarify the goal — profit from day one, or accept a lower (even unprofitable) launch price to grab market share and monetize later — since that changes everything downstream. Assuming a normal profit goal, triangulate three approaches rather than picking one. Cost-plus: sum localization costs, marketing spend for the new market, bandwidth/serving costs, music-licensing costs (often priced per country or region), and the ad revenue foregone by going ad-free, then add a margin. Value-based: survey or focus-group willingness to pay directly, or take the already-optimized price from a comparable market that's already launched and adjust it by the new country's per-capita income. Competitor-based: benchmark against local video/music-streaming incumbents (Netflix, Spotify, Apple Music), adjusting up or down based on YouTube Premium's relative value proposition and how many substitute options locals already have — more substitutes generally means less room to price above them, especially given low switching costs. In practice the launch price is a blend of all three, set with sales, marketing, and finance, and refined post-launch with A/B tested discounts or tiered plans.`}
  />

    type="concept"
    title="10.17"
    company="Twitter"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['engagement']}
    prompt={`Should Twitter add Facebook-style emoji reactions (love, haha, sad, angry, etc.) to tweets?`}
    answer={`Start by clarifying the product hypothesis rather than jumping to an opinion: today a tweet only supports like, reply, or retweet, and reactions would lower the friction of expressing a more specific emotion — "this is funny," "this made me angry" — and fix the real awkwardness of hitting "like" on genuinely sad news. Business case: if reactions increase engagement, tweet authors get more notifications (pulling them back to the app more), which likely increases posting (more content for everyone's timeline), which increases session time and ad impressions — a plausible positive flywheel. To check whether the demand is real rather than assumed, look for existing latent demand in current behavior — how often do short replies like "lol" or "lmao" function as an improvised reaction — and confirm with user research. Weigh it against real counter-arguments: added product complexity, the risk that easy reactions can quietly reduce richer text replies, brand/aesthetic concerns about converging with Facebook, non-trivial engineering cost, and the awkward design question of which reaction set avoids redundant options like "love" vs. "like." Recommend shipping an A/B test only if the demand evidence is real and the expected engagement lift plausibly outweighs the build cost — and align stakeholders in advance that reactions likely rising while replies possibly fall is an acceptable trade, not a surprise to litigate after the test ships.`}

    type="concept"
    title="10.18"
    company="Slack"
    minWords={4}
    keywords={['stickiness']}
    prompt={`What metrics would you use to measure user engagement at Slack? How would you tell early whether user engagement was declining?`}
    answer={`Slack's business is subscription-based collaboration software, so engagement directly predicts renewal and seat expansion — a user here is simply anyone signed into an account. Core metrics: DAU/WAU/MAU and the DAU/WAU and DAU/MAU stickiness ratios (accounting for weekday/weekend seasonality, since a workplace tool naturally dips on weekends), and messages sent per day/week/month as the most direct signal of actual collaboration happening, not just logins.
