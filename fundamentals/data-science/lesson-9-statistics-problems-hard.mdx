  <Problem
    title="6.26"
    company="Google"
    prompt={`You keep drawing i.i.d. $U(0,1)$ values as long as the sequence stays strictly increasing (starting from the first draw). What is the expected length of the sequence?`}
    answer={1.72}
    why={`Let $X_i=1$ if the first $i$ draws happen to already be increasing. Since all $i!$ orderings of $i$ draws are equally likely, $P(X_i=1)=1/i!$. The expected length is $E\\left[\\sum_{i\\ge1} X_i\\right] = \\sum_{i\\ge1}\\frac{1}{i!} = e-1 \\approx 1.72$.`}

  <Problem
    title="6.27"
    company="Facebook"
    prompt={`Game 1: roll two dice, get the dollar amount equal to their product. Game 2: roll one die, get the dollar amount equal to its square. By how much does Game 2's expected payoff exceed Game 1's?`}
    answer={2.92}
    why={`Game 1's payoff is $X_1X_2$ for independent rolls, so $E[X_1X_2]=E[X_1]E[X_2]=E[X]^2$. Game 2's payoff is $X^2$, so $E[X^2]$. The gap is $E[X^2]-E[X]^2 = \\mathrm{Var}(X)$: with $E[X]=3.5$ and $E[X^2]=\\frac{91}{6}\\approx15.17$, $\\mathrm{Var}(X)\\approx15.17-12.25=2.92$. Since variance is always $\\ge0$, Game 2 always has the higher (or equal) expected value.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.28"
    company="Google"
    minWords={50}
    keywords={['unbiased', 'consistent', 'converges']}
    prompt={`What does it mean for an estimator to be unbiased? What about consistent? Give examples of an unbiased but not consistent estimator, and a biased but consistent estimator.`}
    answer={`An estimator $\\hat\\theta$ is unbiased if $E[\\hat\\theta]=\\theta$ for the true parameter $\\theta$, at any sample size. It's consistent if $\\hat\\theta$ converges in probability to $\\theta$ as the sample size $n\\to\\infty$, even if it's biased at finite $n$. Unbiased but not consistent: for $X\\sim N(\\mu,\\sigma^2)$, using just the first observation $x_1$ as the estimate of $\\mu$ is unbiased ($E[x_1]=\\mu$) but never gets more concentrated around $\\mu$ as $n$ grows. Biased but consistent: the plug-in sample variance $\\frac1n\\sum(x_i-\\bar x)^2$ has $E[\\cdot]=\\frac{n-1}{n}\\sigma^2\\ne\\sigma^2$ (biased), but $\\frac{n-1}{n}\\to1$ as $n\\to\\infty$, so it converges to $\\sigma^2$ (consistent).`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.29"
    company="Netflix"
    minWords={40}
    keywords={['likelihood', 'prior']}
    prompt={`What are MLE and MAP? What is the difference between the two?`}
    answer={`Both produce a point estimate of a parameter $\\theta$ from data $X$. MLE maximizes the likelihood alone: $\\hat\\theta_{MLE} = \\arg\\max_\\theta \\sum_i \\log P(x_i\\mid\\theta)$. MAP instead maximizes the posterior via Bayes' rule, which multiplies the likelihood by a prior $P(\\theta)$: $\\hat\\theta_{MAP} = \\arg\\max_\\theta \\left[\\sum_i \\log P(x_i\\mid\\theta) + \\log P(\\theta)\\right]$. MAP lets you inject prior belief about $\\theta$ (useful with little data); MLE is the special case of MAP with a flat (uniform) prior.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.30"
    company="Uber"
    minWords={40}
    keywords={['Bernoulli', 'CLT']}
    prompt={`Say you are given a random Bernoulli trial generator. How would you generate values from a standard normal distribution?`}
    answer={`Run $n$ i.i.d. $\\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ trials and take the sample proportion $\\hat p=\\frac1n\\sum x_i$. Once $np\\ge10$ and $n(1-p)\\ge10$, the CLT gives $\\hat p \\approx N\\!\\left(p,\\frac{p(1-p)}{n}\\right)$. Standardizing, $z = \\frac{\\sum x_i - np}{\\sqrt{np(1-p)}}$ is approximately standard normal — plugging your Bernoulli draws into that formula simulates a draw from $N(0,1)$, with larger $n$ giving a better approximation.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.31"
    company="Facebook"
    prompt={`For a geometric random variable $X$ (number of trials to first success) with success probability $p=0.25$, what is $E[X]$?`}
    answer={4}
    decimals={0}
    why={`$E[X] = \\sum_{k=1}^\\infty k(1-p)^{k-1}p$. Factoring out $p$ and using $\\sum_{k\\ge1}kq^{k-1}=1/(1-q)^2$ for $q=1-p$ gives $E[X] = p\\cdot\\frac{1}{p^2} = \\frac1p$. This holds for any $p$; with $p=0.25$, $E[X]=1/0.25=4$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.32"
    company="Goldman Sachs"
    prompt={`Say we have a random variable $X \\sim D$ for some arbitrary continuous distribution $D$. What is the distribution of $F(X)$, where $F$ is the CDF of $X$?`}
    answer={`Let $Y=F(X)$. For $y\\in(0,1)$, $F_Y(y) = P(F(X)\\le y) = P(X\\le F^{-1}(y)) = F(F^{-1}(y)) = y$. A CDF that's just $F_Y(y)=y$ on $(0,1)$ is exactly the CDF of $\\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ — this is the "probability integral transform": running any continuous random variable through its own CDF always yields a $U(0,1)$ variable.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.33"
    company="Morgan Stanley"
    prompt={`Describe what a moment generating function (MGF) is. Derive the MGF for a normally distributed random variable $X$.`}
    answer={`The MGF of $X$ is $M_X(s)=E[e^{sX}]$; its derivatives at $s=0$ give the moments of $X$ ($M_X'(0)=E[X]$, $M_X''(0)=E[X^2]$, etc). For standard normal $Z\\sim N(0,1)$: $M_Z(s) = \\int e^{sz}\\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2\\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}dz$. Completing the square in the exponent turns the integral into a shifted normal density (which integrates to 1) times $e^{s^2/2}$, giving $M_Z(s)=e^{s^2/2}$. For general $X=\\sigma Z+\\mu \\sim N(\\mu,\\sigma^2)$: $M_X(s) = E[e^{s(\\sigma Z+\\mu)}] = e^{s\\mu}M_Z(s\\sigma) = e^{\\mu s+\\sigma^2s^2/2}$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.34"
    company="Tesla"
    prompt={`You have $N$ i.i.d. draws from an $\\mathrm{Exponential}(\\lambda)$ distribution with sample mean $\\bar x = 2$. What is the MLE estimate of $\\lambda$?`}
    answer={0.5}
    why={`The likelihood is $L(\\lambda)=\\lambda^N e^{-\\lambda\\sum x_i}$; maximizing $\\log L = N\\log\\lambda-\\lambda\\sum x_i$ gives $\\frac{N}{\\lambda}-\\sum x_i=0$, so $\\hat\\lambda = N/\\sum x_i = 1/\\bar x$. With $\\bar x=2$: $\\hat\\lambda = 1/2 = 0.5$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.35"
    company="Citadel"
    prompt={`Assume that $\\log X \\sim N(0,1)$. What is $E[X]$?`}
    answer={1.65}
    why={`Let $Y=\\log X \\sim N(0,1)$, so $X=e^Y$ and $E[X]=E[e^Y]=M_Y(1)$, the MGF of a standard normal evaluated at $s=1$. From $M_Y(s)=e^{s^2/2}$, $E[X] = e^{1/2} \\approx 1.65$.`}

  <Problem
    title="6.36"
    company="Google"
    prompt={`Group 1 has $n_1=10$ points with mean $\\mu_1=5$; Group 2 has $n_2=20$ points with mean $\\mu_2=8$. What is the blended mean of the combined 30 points?`}
    answer={7}
    decimals={0}
    why={`The blended mean is the size-weighted average: $\\bar\\mu = \\frac{n_1\\mu_1+n_2\\mu_2}{n_1+n_2} = \\frac{10(5)+20(8)}{30} = \\frac{210}{30} = 7$. The blended variance needs each group's own variance too, weighted by size and adjusted for how far each group's mean sits from the blended mean: $s^2 = \\frac{n_1s_1^2+n_2s_2^2+n_1(\\mu_1-\\bar\\mu)^2+n_2(\\mu_2-\\bar\\mu)^2}{n_1+n_2}$. Both formulas extend directly to $K$ subsets by summing over all $K$ groups instead of 2.`}
  />
  <Problem
    type="concept"
    title="6.37"
    company="Two Sigma"
    prompt={`Say we have two random variables $X$ and $Y$. What does it mean for $X$ and $Y$ to be independent? What about uncorrelated? Give an example where $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated but not independent.`}
    answer={`$X,Y$ are independent if $P(X=x,Y=y)=P(X=x)P(Y=y)$ for all $x,y$ — knowing one tells you nothing about the other's distribution. They're uncorrelated if $\\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=E[XY]-E[X]E[Y]=0$, a weaker condition that only rules out *linear* dependence. Example: let $X$ be uniform on $\\{-1,0,1\\}$ and $Y=1$ if $X=0$, else $Y=0$. Then $E[XY]=0$ and $E[X]=0$, so $\\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ (uncorrelated) — but $Y$ is completely determined by $X$, so they're clearly dependent (e.g. $P(Y=1\\mid X=0)=1 \\ne P(Y=1)$).`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.38"
    company="Citadel"
    prompt={`Say $X \\sim \\mathrm{Uniform}(-1,1)$ and $Y=X^2$. What is the covariance of $X$ and $Y$?`}
    answer={0}
    decimals={0}
    why={`$\\mathrm{Cov}(X,X^2) = E[X^3]-E[X]E[X^2]$. By symmetry of $U(-1,1)$ around 0, $E[X]=0$ and $E[X^3]=0$ (both odd-power integrals over a symmetric interval), so $\\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y) = 0-0\\cdot E[X^2] = 0$ — despite $Y$ being completely determined by $X$, another example of uncorrelated-but-dependent variables.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.39"
    company="Lyft"
    prompt={`To sample a point uniformly at random inside a circle of radius $R$, you sample an angle $\\theta \\sim U(0,2\\pi)$ and a radius $r$ via inverse-CDF sampling from $F_R(r) = r^2/R^2$. What is $F_R(r)$ at $r=R/2$ — i.e., what fraction of uniformly sampled points fall within half the radius?`}
    answer={0.25}
    why={`Points are uniform over the disk's area, so $F_R(r) = P(\\text{radius}\\le r) = \\frac{\\pi r^2}{\\pi R^2} = r^2/R^2$ (inner disk area over full disk area). At $r=R/2$: $F_R(R/2) = (R/2)^2/R^2 = 1/4 = 0.25$ — only a quarter of the points fall within the inner half-radius, since area grows with $r^2$. To actually sample: draw $U\\sim\\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)$, invert to get $r=R\\sqrt U$, and pair it with $\\theta\\sim U(0,2\\pi)$ for $(x,y)=(r\\cos\\theta, r\\sin\\theta)$.`}
  />
  <Problem
    title="6.40"
    company="Two Sigma"
    prompt={`You continually sample i.i.d. $\\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ random variables until their running sum exceeds 1. How many samples do you expect to need?`}
    answer={2.72}
    why={`Let $m(t)=E[\\text{draws needed for the running sum to exceed } t]$ for threshold $t\\in(0,1)$. Conditioning on the first draw $x$: if $x>t$ you're done in 1 draw; otherwise you need $1+m(t-x)$ more. This gives $m(t) = 1+\\int_0^t m(u)\\,du$, and differentiating, $m'(t)=m(t)$ with $m(0)=1$, so $m(t)=e^t$. At the question's threshold $t=1$: $m(1)=e\\approx2.72$.`}
  />
