## Conditional probability and Bayes’ rule

You often want $P(A \mid B)$ — the chance of $A$ once you know $B$ happened. Bayes’ rule rewrites that as:

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}
$$

- $P(A)$ — **prior**
- $P(B \mid A)$ — **likelihood**
- $P(A \mid B)$ — **posterior**

If $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, then $A$ and $B$ are **independent**: learning $B$ does not change $A$. They can also be **conditionally independent** given $C$:

$$
P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\, P(B \mid C)
$$

**Interview habit:** any “given that…” probability is a Bayes hint. Rare-disease testing is the classic trap — a “highly accurate” test still needs the base rate. In ML, many models are about choosing a good conditional distribution of the target given the data.

## Law of total probability

If $\{B_i\}$ partitions the sample space (disjoint, cover everything), then:

$$
P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\, P(B_i)
$$

Think of a **tree of outcomes**: weight each branch by its probability, then sum. Common DS framing: purchase probability averaged over customer segments.

## Counting: permutations vs combinations

Counting shows up as “how many ways…?” or as the denominator/numerator of a probability (“four cards of the same suit”).

- **Order matters** → **permutations**:
  $$
  P(n,k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}
  $$
  Example: password character sequences.

- **Order does not matter** → **combinations**:
  $$
  \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
  $$
  Example: choosing a set of restaurants from a map.

First decide whether order matters, then apply the right formula. The same ideas appear in combinatorial and graph problems.

## Random variables, PMF, PDF, and CDF

A **random variable** is a numeric outcome with a distribution. It is **discrete** (countable values) or **continuous** (uncountable range).

| Type | Distribution function | Notation idea |
|---|---|---|
| Discrete | Probability **mass** function (PMF) | $f_X(x) = P(X = x)$ |
| Continuous | Probability **density** function (PDF) | densities on intervals; $P(X = x) = 0$ |

Both must be non-negative and normalize:

- Discrete: $\sum_x f_X(x) = 1$
- Continuous: $\int f_X(x)\, dx = 1$

The **CDF** is $F_X(x) = P(X \le x)$ — a sum of the PMF or an integral of the PDF. It is non-negative and non-decreasing. In interviews, name the right PMF/PDF/CDF before deriving properties.

## Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions

When two variables move together, use a **joint** PMF/PDF. For continuous $(X,Y)$:

$$
\iint f_{X,Y}(x,y)\, dx\, dy = 1
$$

**Marginal** of $X$: integrate (or sum) out $Y$:

$$
f_X(x) = \int f_{X,Y}(x,y)\, dy
$$

**Joint CDF:** $F_{X,Y}(x,y) = P(X \le x,\, Y \le y)$.

**Conditional** densities extend Bayes’ idea, e.g. relating $f_X(x)$ to $f_Y(y)$ and $f_{X \mid Y}(x \mid y)$. Deep technical rounds may ask for these; whenever multiple RVs appear, start from the joint.

## Discrete distributions: Binomial and Poisson

Interviewers care that you **recognize the story** and can use the PMF — not that you memorize every identity.

### Binomial

$k$ successes in $n$ independent trials, each with success probability $p$:

$$
P(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \quad \mathbb{E}[X] = np, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = np(1-p)
$$

Stories: coin flips, signups, any binary success/failure count with fixed $n$.

<FormulaExplorer preset="binomial" />

### Poisson

Count of events in a fixed interval at constant rate $\lambda$:

$$
P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda} \frac{\lambda^k}{k!}, \quad \mathbb{E}[X] = \lambda, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \lambda
$$

Stories: visits in an hour, defects per square foot — a rate process, not a fixed number of Bernoulli trials.

## Continuous distributions: Uniform, Exponential, Normal

### Uniform$(a,b)$

Constant density on $[a,b]$:

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}, \quad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{a+b}{2}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}
$$

Stories: random number generation, some sampling / null setups in testing.

<FormulaExplorer preset="uniform" />

### Exponential$(\lambda)$

Waiting time between events of a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$:

$$
f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x} \quad (x \ge 0), \quad \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \quad \mathrm{Var}(X) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}
$$

Stories: time until purchase, time until default. Famous property: **memorylessness** — remaining wait does not depend on how long you have already waited.

<FormulaExplorer preset="exponential" />

### Normal$(\mu, \sigma^2)$

Bell curve with PDF

$$
f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$

Everywhere in interviews because many phenomena look roughly Normal, and because of the **Central Limit Theorem**.

## Markov chains

A **Markov chain** has a finite set of states; the next state depends only on the **current** state (Markov property: past and future are conditionally independent given the present).

Transitions live in a matrix $P$ with $P_{ij} = $ probability of going $i \to j$.

Useful vocabulary:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Recurrent** state | Once entered, you return with probability 1 |
| **Transient** state | Positive chance you leave and never return |
| **Stationary distribution** $\pi$ | $\pi = \pi P$ — long-run fraction of time in each state |

Product example: user states *new / active / churned*, ask about long-run mix. Reach for Markov chains when you have discrete states, transitions, and long-run behavior.

## Interview checklist

- Hear **“given that”** → conditionals / Bayes / base rates  
- Hear a **tree of scenarios** → total probability  
- Hear **how many ways** → permutations vs combinations  
- Hear a **data-generating story** → name Binomial, Poisson, Uniform, Exponential, or Normal  
- Hear **states and long-run mix** → Markov chain + $\pi = \pi P$
