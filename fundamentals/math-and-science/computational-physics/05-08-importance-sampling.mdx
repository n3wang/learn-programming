# 5.8 Importance sampling

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §5.8. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [5.7 Monte Carlo variance reduction](./05-07-mc-variance-reduction). Labs: [5.8b Importance-sampling labs](./05-08b-importance-sampling-labs).

## Reweight the measure

Write the integral against a **weight** (density) $w$ that you choose:

$$
I=\int w(x)\,\frac{f(x)}{w(x)}\,dx
=\Biggl\langle\frac{f}{w}\Biggr\rangle,
$$

where the average is over samples drawn from $w$ (normalized so $\int w=1$ on the domain). Instead of averaging $f$ under a uniform measure, you average the ratio $f/w$ under a smarter measure.

## Flatten $f/w$

Choose $w$ roughly proportional to $f$ (where $f$ is large). Then $f/w$ is nearly constant, so its sample variance shrinks. Uniform sampling is the special case $w=\mathrm{const}$. Importance sampling is the natural next step when you can sample from a peaked $w$ that matches the integrand’s shape.

## von Neumann rejection sampling

How do you draw $x\sim w$ when $w$ is not a standard distribution? One classic construction (von Neumann): enclose $w$ in a box of height $w_0\ge w(x)$ on the support. Throw a point uniformly in that box,

$$
(x,W)=(U,\,w_0 V),
$$

with independent uniforms $U,V$ (here $x$ on the interval of interest). **Accept** the $x$-coordinate if $W\le w(x)$; otherwise reject and try again. Accepted abscissae are distributed according to $w$. The acceptance rate is the area under $w$ over the box area.

![Figure 5.7 — von Neumann rejection: throw under a box of height $w_0$; keep points that fall under $w(x)$.](/img/computational-physics/fig-5-7-von-neumann.svg)

Accepted samples feed an importance estimate $\langle f/w\rangle$. When $w$ is harder to sample by inversion or rejection, **Metropolis** algorithms (Chapter 17) generate correlated draws from $w$ without needing a tight enclosing box — mentioned here only as the later generalization.

## Inverse-CDF shortcut

If the CDF of $w$ is invertible, you can skip rejection: draw $u\sim U[0,1)$ and set $x=F_w^{-1}(u)$. Example on $[0,1]$: $w(x)=2(1-x)$ has $x=1-\sqrt{1-u}$, after which you average $e^{-x}/w(x)$.
