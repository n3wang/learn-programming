# 9.4 Noise filtering

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §9.4. Rewritten in original wording — no listing dumps. Continues [9.3b](./09-03b-dft-labs). Next: [9.4b](./09-04b-noise-filtering-labs).

## Goal

Measured data look like

$$
y(t)=s(t)+n(t).
$$

You want the frequencies that belong to $s$, then (optionally) an inverse transform to a cleaned $s(t)$. Two classical handles: **autocorrelation** and **filters** (sinc low-pass returns in §9.4.4 / wavelets later).

![Figure 9.3 — noisy trace, autocorrelation, power, low-pass sketch.](/img/computational-physics/fig-9-3-noise-autocorr.svg)

## Correlation → autocorrelation → power

With means already subtracted, correlate $y$ with $x$ at lag $\tau$, move to Fourier space, then specialize to autocorrelation. For $y=s+n$ with uncorrelated noise, cross terms drop and you recover the pure-signal power spectrum. Pick $c(\tau)$, $C(\omega)$, and $A(\omega)\propto|S|^2$ among lookalikes.

<GuidedChoiceExplanation preset="autocorrelation-power" />

Large $|c|$ at some lag means the shapes line up; random relative phase averages toward zero. Broadband high-frequency hash drops out of $A(\omega)$ relative to a direct $|Y|^2$ of the raw trace — often all you need without knowing the noise waveform.

## Practical checklist

1. Sample a known $s(t)$ (e.g. $1/(1-0.9\sin t)$ — infinite overtones from the geometric expansion).
2. DFT → semilog $|S|^2$.
3. Form $A(\tau)$ (discrete sum on your grid) and DFT it; compare to $|S|^2$.
4. Add noise $y_i=s_i+\alpha(2r_i-1)$; raise $\alpha$ until the direct spectrum drowns but autocorrelation still shows peaks — then find where even $A$ fails.

Filters (multiply $Y(\omega)$ by a low-pass mask, inverse transform) are the complementary attack when you want a cleaned **time** trace, not only a power spectrum.
