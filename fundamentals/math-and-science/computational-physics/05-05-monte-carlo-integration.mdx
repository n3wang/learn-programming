# 5.5 Monte Carlo integration

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §5.5. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [5.4 Gaussian quadrature](./05-04-gaussian-quadrature). Labs: [5.5b Monte Carlo integration labs](./05-05b-monte-carlo-integration-labs).

## Pond area by stone throwing

Imagine a pond inside a rectangular fence of known area $A_{\mathrm{box}}$. Throw $N$ stones uniformly at random into the box; count how many land in the water ($N_{\mathrm{pond}}$). The hit fraction estimates the area ratio:

$$
\frac{A_{\mathrm{pond}}}{A_{\mathrm{box}}}\simeq\frac{N_{\mathrm{pond}}}{N_{\mathrm{pond}}+N_{\mathrm{box}}}
=\frac{N_{\mathrm{pond}}}{N},
\qquad
A_{\mathrm{pond}}\simeq\frac{N_{\mathrm{pond}}}{N}\,A_{\mathrm{box}}.
$$

No antiderivative, no mesh — only a uniform sampler and a membership test (“inside the pond?”). That is the Monte Carlo idea for integration: replace geometry by counting.

![Figure 5.4 — stones in a square; hits inside the inscribed disk estimate the circle’s area.](/img/computational-physics/fig-5-4-stone-throw.svg)

## $\pi$ from a unit circle in a square

Put the unit disk $x^{2}+y^{2}\le 1$ inside the square $[-1,1]^{2}$ of side $2$ (area $A_{\mathrm{box}}=4$). Each stone draws $(x,y)$ uniformly in the square; a hit means $x^{2}+y^{2}\le 1$. Then

$$
\frac{\pi\cdot 1^{2}}{4}\simeq\frac{N_{\mathrm{hit}}}{N}
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\pi\simeq 4\,\frac{N_{\mathrm{hit}}}{N}.
$$

With the chapter LCG and $N=10^{4}$ throws you typically land near $3.14$ — close enough to see the method, not a replacement for $\mathrm{atan}(1)$ in production. The statistical error scales like $1/\sqrt{N}$: quadrupling the sample count halves the typical fluctuation.

## Why Monte Carlo for integrals?

In one dimension, trapezoid, Simpson, and Gauss usually beat crude Monte Carlo for smooth $f$. Monte Carlo shines when:

- the domain is awkward (irregular pond, complicated acceptance region);
- the dimension $D$ is large — a grid with $M$ points per axis needs $M^{D}$ evaluations, which explodes;
- you only need a rough answer with a transparent error bar $\sim 1/\sqrt{N}$.

The next lesson (§5.6) makes the mean-value form precise and shows the $D$-independence of the $1/\sqrt{N}$ law.

## Error investigation: trap / Simpson / Gauss / MC

A standard calibration integral is

$$
I=\int_0^{1} e^{-t}\,dt=1-e^{-1}.
$$

Run trapezoid, Simpson, Gauss–Legendre, and a mean-value Monte Carlo estimate at several $N$, then plot $\log_{10}|I_{\mathrm{approx}}-I|$ versus $\log_{10} N$. On that log–log plane a clean power law

$$
\varepsilon\sim C\,N^{\alpha}
$$

appears as a straight segment with slope $\alpha$. Deterministic rules for smooth $f$ often show steep negative slopes (Simpson $\alpha\approx -4$ in $h$, Gauss even steeper in $N$ for analytic integrands) until **round-off** flattens the floor — algorithms stop improving when floating-point noise dominates (the Fig. 5.5 idea). Monte Carlo’s slope is near $\alpha=-1/2$; it loses in 1D on this smooth problem, but that shallow slope does **not** get worse when $D$ grows.
