# 9.1 Fourier series

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §9.1. Rewritten in original wording — not a paste of textbook figures or listings. Continues [8.7 ODE solver sketches](./08-06-ode-sketches). Next: [9.1b Fourier series labs](./09-01b-fourier-series-labs).

## From anharmonic clocks to spectra

Chapter 8 gave periodic orbits that are **not** pure sinusoids: soft $\alpha$ wells and $|x|^p$ forces produce pyramids, asymmetric swings, and amplitude-dependent periods. Those $x(t)$ still satisfy $x(t+T)=x(t)$ once a true period $T$ is known. Fourier’s theorem then lets you write any such (nice enough) signal as a sum of harmonics of the fundamental

$$
\omega\equiv\omega_1=\frac{2\pi}{T}.
$$

The computational program of this chapter is: **measure or impose $T$**, **project coefficients**, and later replace the integrals by a **DFT / FFT**. Short-time spectra and the quantum Fourier transform appear in later chapters; here we stay classical and continuous-time.

## Series for a known period

Any single-valued period-$T$ signal with finitely many jumps admits a real Fourier series in harmonics of $\omega$. Each pair $(a_n,b_n)$ is the amplitude of the pure tone at frequency $n\omega$; intensity scales as $a_n^2+b_n^2$. Walk the series form, the orthogonality projection for $(a_n,b_n)$, and the odd-sawtooth $b_n$ — pick the matching equation each step.

This is the same least-squares projection idea as Chapter 6: the truncated series is the best trigonometric fit of that order. At a jump the partial sums converge to the **midpoint** of the left and right limits; near a sharp corner they **overshoot** (Gibbs phenomenon, roughly $9\%$ of the jump).

### Symmetry shortcuts

- $a_0=2\langle y\rangle$ (twice the mean).
- Odd $y(-t)=-y(t)$ $\Rightarrow$ all $a_n=0$; integrate sines on half a period if convenient.
- Even $y(-t)=y(t)$ $\Rightarrow$ all $b_n=0$; cosine half-range formulas.

Causal signals that vanish for $t&lt;0$ are rarely perfectly odd/even, so small “forbidden” coefficients can appear numerically.

## Why this still helps nonlinear oscillators

Linear systems enjoy superposition of normal modes. Nonlinear Newton laws do **not**. Fourier does not claim that each harmonic separately solves the ODE — only that the **observed periodic $y(t)$** can be expanded. Traditional mechanics often finds the fundamental analytically and adds overtones by perturbation; here you start from the full numerical orbit (or an analytic toy signal) and read the spectrum off.

For spectral analysis of driven / dissipative systems, wait until transients die. Strongly nonlinear attractors can jump among configurations; then a single long FFT mixes regimes — motivation for short-time transforms later.

## Worked toys: sawtooth and half-wave

### Sawtooth (odd, discontinuous)

Shifted to be odd on $(-T/2,T/2)$,

$$
y(t)=\frac{t}{T/2},\qquad -\frac{T}{2}\le t\le\frac{T}{2},
$$

and extended periodically. Only sine terms survive (the $b_n$ you picked above), so

$$
y(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\Bigl(\sin\omega t-\tfrac12\sin 2\omega t+\tfrac13\sin 3\omega t-\cdots\Bigr).
$$

A few terms capture the overall ramp; many terms are needed for the corners. At each jump the series averages to $0$; just beside the jump, partial sums overshoot (Gibbs).

### Half-wave (continuous, kinked)

$$
y(t)=\begin{cases}
\sin\omega t,&0&lt;t&lt;T/2,\\
0,&T/2&lt;t&lt;T.
\end{cases}
$$

Projection gives $b_1=\tfrac12$, other $b_n=0$, and

$$
a_n=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{-2}{\pi(n^2-1)},&n\text{ even or }0,\\
0,&n\text{ odd},
\end{cases}
$$

so

$$
y(t)=\tfrac12\sin\omega t+\frac{1}{\pi}-\frac{2}{3\pi}\cos 2\omega t-\frac{2}{15\pi}\cos 4\omega t+\cdots.
$$

No corners as harsh as the sawtooth — truncation converges faster.

## Takeaway

Know $T$, project $(a_n,b_n)$, watch power $a_n^2+b_n^2$, and expect midpoint convergence plus Gibbs at jumps. Nonlinear orbits from Chapter 8 are fair game once you extract their period. Labs lock analytic $b_n$ and a few partial sums before DFT machinery arrives.
