# 3.1 Errors

> **Source:** Numerical-error ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), Chapter 3. Rewritten in original wording; the sine series and the four-digit $1/3$ identity are standard classroom calculations, not a transcription of the book.

A computer printout looks exact. It is not. Every number you get is some mix of **what the mathematics asked for** and **what the machine and the algorithm could actually do**. Before you trust a result — especially a long run — name the error families and estimate which one is in charge.

Bugs and sloppy algebra are still the first place to look. Once the program is the program you meant to write, three more families remain.

## Random error

Uncontrolled physical events: a voltage hiccup, a cosmic-ray bit flip, someone pulling a plug. You do not choose when they happen. Their chance of showing up **grows with wall-clock time**. A twenty-second check can look rock solid; a week-long integration may need **independent reruns** before you believe it is reproducible.

That is the opposite of truncation and round-off, which (on the same binary and the same inputs) happen the same way every time.

## Approximation (algorithmic) error

You almost never run the exact mathematics. Infinite series become finite sums, $dx$ becomes a step $h$, a function that varies is frozen as a constant for one slice. The gap between the true math and that finite recipe is **approximation error** — also called **algorithmic error**, because it lives in the method, not in the hardware.

The textbook example is the sine series, which is exact as an infinite sum and approximate once you stop at $N$ terms:

$$
\begin{align*}
\sin x &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1} x^{2n-1}}{(2n-1)!} \\
&= \sum_{n=1}^{N} \frac{(-1)^{n-1} x^{2n-1}}{(2n-1)!} + \mathcal{E}(x,N).
\end{align*}
$$

$\mathcal{E}(x,N)$ is the ignored tail from $n=N+1$ onward. For a **good** algorithm that tail should shrink as $N$ grows and vanish as $N\to\infty$.

For this particular series the scale of $N$ is set by $x$: you need $N \gg |x|$. If $N$ and $|x|$ are comparable, the leftover is large even though the infinite series is perfect.

<FormulaExplorer preset="sinTaylor" />

**Theorem (truncation of a convergent scheme).** If the discrete method is consistent with the mathematics, algorithmic error $\to 0$ as the discretization parameter $N\to\infty$. For the sine Taylor sum, that limit is useful in practice only when $N$ is much larger than $|x|$.

## Round-off error

Floating-point numbers keep **finitely many digits**. That is the same kind of limitation as a lab instrument with a finite number of significant figures: the value you store is close to the true value, not equal to it. Every extra arithmetic step is another chance to round, so **the damage grows with the number of operations**. Some methods amplify that noise and go unstable; when round-off is most of the answer, you are looking at garbage.

Toy machine with four digits after the decimal: $\frac{1}{3}$ becomes $0.3333$, $\frac{2}{3}$ becomes $0.6667$ (last digit rounded). Then

$$
2\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) - \frac{2}{3} = 0.6666 - 0.6667 = -0.0001 \neq 0.
$$

Mathematically the combination is zero. On that machine it is a tiny bias. Repeat the same pattern millions of times and the tiny bias is no longer tiny.

## How a float is stored

Write a large number in scientific notation so the **exponent** is a small integer and the **mantissa** carries the significant figures:

$$
a = 9.8765432109876543 \times 10^{16}.
$$

The exponent is cheap to keep exactly. The mantissa is not: any digits past what the format allows are thrown away (or rounded). Double precision keeps on the order of **15–16 decimal digits** of $m$; a 6-digit toy word would keep only $9.87654$ and forget the rest. Relative error is $| \tilde a - a | / |a|$ — it falls as you keep more digits of $m$, and has nothing to do with how large $10^{e}$ is.

Those discarded least-significant digits are not a curiosity. Once a word has a fixed width, **every later arithmetic step works with a slightly wrong mantissa**. Subtract two such words and you can throw away the accurate part on purpose.

## 3.1.1 Courting disaster: subtractive cancellation

A stored value $x_c$ is the exact $x$ plus a little relative noise $\epsilon_x$ of size comparable to machine precision $\epsilon_m$:

$$
x_c \simeq x(1+\epsilon_x).
$$

Feed that model through a subtraction $a=b-c$:

$$
a_c \simeq b(1+\epsilon_b)-c(1+\epsilon_c)
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\frac{a_c}{a}\simeq 1+\epsilon_b\frac{b}{a}-\epsilon_c\frac{c}{a}.
$$

The error in $a$ is a weighted mix of the errors in $b$ and $c$. Nothing forces the last two terms to cancel. The dangerous case is $b\simeq c$: then $a$ is small, the subtraction has already cancelled the leading digits you trusted, and the leftover is built from the noisy tails. Relative to that small $a$,

$$
\frac{a_c}{a}\simeq 1+\frac{b}{a}(\epsilon_b-\epsilon_c)
\simeq 1+\frac{b}{a}\max\bigl(|\epsilon_b|,|\epsilon_c|\bigr).
$$

The factor $b/a$ is huge. You do not get to assume a helpful sign on $\epsilon_b-\epsilon_c$, so you plan for the worst.

**Theorem.** If you subtract two large numbers and the result is small, that small number is less significant than either of the large ones.

<FormulaExplorer preset="subtractCancel" />

You already met this in the sine series for large $x$: $x-x^3/3!+\cdots$ is a pile of big alternating terms whose true sum is only $\sin x\in[-1,1]$. The same trap sits in $e^{-x}\simeq 1-x+x^2/2!-\cdots$. A rewrite removes the cancellation: compute $e^{x}$ (all terms positive) and take the reciprocal. Round-off is still there; you just stopped manufacturing a tiny answer out of two giants.

## 3.1.2 Subtractive-cancellation labs

### Quadratic formula

$ax^{2}+bx+c=0$ has two algebraically equal closed forms

$$
x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}{2a}
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
x=\frac{-2c}{b\pm\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}}.
$$

When $b^{2}\gg 4ac$, $\sqrt{\Delta}\approx|b|$, so **one** of the $\pm$ choices subtracts two nearly equal numbers. The other root stays large; recover the tiny one from $x_1 x_2=c/a$ instead of from the cancelling $\pm$. A clean test family is $a=1$, $b=1$, $c=10^{-n}$ as $n$ grows.

## 3.1.3 Round-off in one product, quotient, or function

For a single division $a=b/c$ the same $x_c=x(1+\epsilon_x)$ model gives

$$
\frac{a_c}{a}=\frac{1+\epsilon_b}{1+\epsilon_c}\simeq(1+\epsilon_b)(1-\epsilon_c)\simeq 1+\epsilon_b-\epsilon_c.
$$

Drop the $\epsilon^{2}$ specks and take absolute values (signs are unknown):

$$
\frac{a_c}{a}\simeq 1+|\epsilon_b|+|\epsilon_c|.
$$

Multiplication obeys the same worst-case sum. It is the lab rule: **add the relative uncertainties**.

For a smooth $f$, the gap between $f(x)$ and $f(x_c)$ is first-order

$$
\mathcal{E}\simeq\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}(x-x_c).
$$

On $f(x)=\sqrt{1+x}$ that is $\mathcal{E}\simeq (x-x_c)/(2(1+x))$. Near $x=\pi/4$ a fourth-place error in $x$ stays a fourth-place error in $f$ — the derivative is not an amplifier here.

## 3.1.4 Round-off error accumulation

One rounding event is a tiny step of length $\epsilon_m$. After $N$ steps, if those steps point in **random** directions, the typical net displacement of a random walk is

$$
R\simeq\sqrt{N}\,r
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ro}}\simeq\sqrt{N}\,\epsilon_m.
$$

If the errors refuse to cancel, a sober backup is the coherent bound $N\epsilon_m$. Worse: some **upward recurrences** (spherical Bessel functions are the usual exhibit) let the error grow like $N!$. Those need a separate analysis — the $\sqrt{N}$ cartoon does not apply.
