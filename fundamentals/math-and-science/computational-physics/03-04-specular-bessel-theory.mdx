## 3.4.1 Specular reflection in a circle

For a perfect mirror, angle of incidence equals angle of reflection. Put the origin at the center of a circular mirror and track the ray by an angle $\theta$. If the grazing geometry is set by a fixed $\phi<\pi$, each bounce advances

$$
\theta_{\mathrm{new}} = \theta_{\mathrm{old}} + 2\phi.
$$

$\theta$ and $\theta+2\pi$ are the same point on the circle. So if

$$
\frac{\phi}{\pi} = \frac{n}{m}
$$

is rational, after enough bounces the ray **falls on itself** and draws a closed figure. Irrational $\phi/\pi$ yields a dense chord pattern instead.

![Figure 3.4 — left: incidence = reflection; right: repeated internal reflections in a circular mirror.](/img/computational-physics/fig-3-4-specular.svg)

<SpecularReflectionSimulator />

**Theorem (closed specular orbit).** A ray with step $2\phi$ on a circle closes if and only if $\phi/\pi$ is rational (up to the $2\pi$ identification of the hit point).

Finite precision changes the story: if you round $\phi$ and $\theta$ to four decimals each bounce (`round(x, 4)`), a tiny angle error accumulates with the step count — the same long-calculation pathology as in §3.1–3.2.

## 3.4.2 Spherical Bessel functions

Spherical Bessel $j_\ell(x)$ and Neumann $n_\ell(x)$ are the regular / irregular solutions of

$$
x^{2} f'' + 2x f' + \bigl[x^{2}-\ell(\ell+1)\bigr] f = 0.
$$

They appear in partial-wave expansions, e.g.

$$
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}} = \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty} i^{\ell}(2\ell+1)\, j_\ell(kr)\, P_\ell(\cos\theta).
$$

Seed values:

$$
j_0=\frac{\sin x}{x},\quad
j_1=\frac{\sin x}{x^{2}}-\frac{\cos x}{x},\quad
n_0=-\frac{\cos x}{x},\quad
n_1=-\frac{\cos x}{x^{2}}-\frac{\sin x}{x}.
$$

### Recurrence: up looks easy, down is stable

Same algebra, two directions:

$$
j_{\ell+1}=\frac{2\ell+1}{x}j_\ell - j_{\ell-1}
\quad\text{(up)},\qquad
j_{\ell-1}=\frac{2\ell+1}{x}j_\ell - j_{\ell+1}
\quad\text{(down)}.
$$

**Upward** from $j_0,j_1$: you eventually subtract two large numbers to make a tiny $j_\ell$ — subtractive cancellation. Because $n_\ell$ obeys the **same** recurrence, the computer’s value is effectively

$$
j_\ell^{(c)} = j_\ell + \epsilon\, n_\ell.
$$

When $|n_\ell|\gg|j_\ell|$, even a speck of Neumann swamps the answer.

**Miller’s device:** start at large $L$ with arbitrary seeds, recur **downward** (small + small → larger), then renormalize

$$
j_\ell^{\mathrm{N}} = j_\ell^{c}\,\frac{j_0^{\mathrm{anal}}}{j_0^{c}},
\qquad j_0^{\mathrm{anal}}=\frac{\sin x}{x}.
$$

Relative $\ell$-dependence stabilizes; absolute scale comes from $j_0$.
