# 4.1 Monte Carlo — random numbers

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), Ch. 4 intro–§4.2. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [3.4b Specular and Bessel labs](./03-04b-specular-bessel-labs). Labs: [4.1b Monte Carlo labs](./04-01b-monte-carlo-labs).

Deterministic programs are comforting: same input, same output. Yet huge amounts of machine time go into **Monte Carlo** work — calculations that feed **chance-like** numbers into a model of a random process (thermal motion, radioactive decay) or into an average that solves an equation. Later chapters reuse the same tools for integration and for thermal / quantum fluctuations. This lesson is the foundation: where those numbers come from, how to spot a bad generator, and what a random walk predicts for diffusion.

## 4.1 Random numbers

A sequence $r_1,r_2,\ldots$ is **random** if successive values are uncorrelated. That is not the same as **uniform**. Uniform means every value in an interval is equally likely ($P(r)=\mathrm{const}$). The integers $1,2,3,\ldots$ are uniform on their lattice and still perfectly predictable. Conversely, a sequence can look “random” while hiding short-range correlations, for example

$$
r_1,\ (1-r_1),\ r_2,\ (1-r_2),\ \ldots
$$

The usual library generator aims for **uniform and uncorrelated** draws on $[0,1)$.

Computers are deterministic machines, so they cannot produce true randomness. A computed stream always has a finite period and hidden structure; given enough previous terms you can predict the next. We call the output **pseudorandom** (and then casually drop the “pseudo”). Better generators hide correlations longer; if you look hard enough, or run long enough, the correlations show. Tables of radioactive counts or hardware noise sources exist as sanity checks, not as production defaults.

### Linear congruent (power-residue) method

The classic integer recurrence on $\{0,1,\ldots,M-1\}$ is

$$
r_{i+1} \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=} (a r_i + c) \bmod M.
$$

The user supplies a **seed** $r_1$. In Python the remainder is `%`. As a tiny walkthrough: $a=4$, $c=1$, $M=9$, $r_1=3$ yields

$$
3,\ 4,\ 8,\ 6,\ 7,\ 2,\ 0,\ 1,\ 5,\ 3,\ \ldots
$$

— a cycle of length $M=9$. Dividing by $M$ maps into $[0,1)$:

$$
0.333,\ 0.444,\ 0.889,\ \ldots
$$

For a general interval $[A,B]$,

$$
x_i = A + (B-A)\, r_i, \qquad 0\le r_i\le 1.
$$

Once any integer repeats, the whole cycle repeats. Large $a$ and $M$ stretch the period (48-bit generators may reach $M\sim 2^{48}$), but overflow and careless constants produce garbage. Prefer an industrial generator (`random.random()` — Mersenne Twister in CPython) over a homemade LCG for serious work; still **plot** before you trust.

## 4.2 Random walk (theory)

A perfume molecule in air takes many collisions before it reaches the back of the room. Model the path as $N$ planar steps $(\Delta x_k,\Delta y_k)$. The squared distance from the origin is

$$
R^{2} = \Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta x_k\Bigr)^{2} + \Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\Delta y_k\Bigr)^{2}.
$$

Expanding produces diagonal squares and cross terms. If each step’s direction is independent and isotropic, the **cross terms average to zero** over many walks, leaving

$$
R_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2} = \langle R^{2}\rangle \simeq N\, r_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
R_{\mathrm{rms}} \simeq \sqrt{N}\, r_{\mathrm{rms}}.
$$

The mean **vector** displacement vanishes,

$$
\langle\vec{R}\rangle \simeq \vec{0},
$$

but the mean **length** does not. Endpoints smear uniformly in angle; $\sqrt{N}\, r_{\mathrm{rms}}$ is much smaller than the straight-line $N r_{\mathrm{rms}}$, yet it grows without bound — that is normal diffusion.

## Checking the $\sqrt{N}$ law (assessment)

Theory assumed isotropic, uncorrelated steps. After $K$ independent trials,

$$
\langle R^{2}(N)\rangle = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} R_{(k)}^{2}(N),
\qquad
R_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle R^{2}(N)\rangle}.
$$

Two diagnostics:

1. **Cross terms.** The derivation needs
   $$
   \frac{\langle\Delta x_i\Delta x_{j\neq i}\rangle}{R^{2}}
   \simeq
   \frac{\langle\Delta x_i\Delta y_j\rangle}{R^{2}}
   \simeq 0
   $$
   on a long single walk and after averaging trials.
2. **$R_{\mathrm{rms}}$ vs $\sqrt{N}$.** Start at small $N$ (noisy) and push to large $N$ (a few correct digits on average). Different ways of injecting randomness (unit-normalized vs raw box steps) shift the slope but not the $\sqrt{N}$ shape.

## 4.2.2 Random walks in a brain

Understanding a brain is not only neuron wiring — the **fluid-filled extracellular spaces** between cells shape how drugs, metabolites, and molecular signals diffuse [Nicholson, 2022]. Random-walk models of chemical probes look strikingly like the free walks above. Adding randomly placed **circular obstacles** (inaccessible pockets) changes the picture: walks that **stop** or **bounce** on contact explore less volume, so $R_{\mathrm{rms}}$ drops.

![Figure 4.5 — left: free multi-walk diffusion; right: circular extracellular obstacles.](/img/computational-physics/fig-4-5-brain-walks.svg)

<BrainWalkSimulator />

Einstein’s 1905 estimate for an effective diffusion coefficient in $d$ dimensions is

$$
D \simeq \frac{R_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}}{2\, d\, t},
$$

with $t$ the walk duration. At fixed $d$ and $t$, obstacles that shrink $R_{\mathrm{rms}}$ shrink $D$ by the same factor in $R_{\mathrm{rms}}^{2}$.

## 4.2.3 Random protein folding (HP model)

A protein chain is a string of monomers: **hydrophobic (H)** residues prefer to bury away from water; **polar (P)** residues do not. Folding searches low-energy geometries. Full molecular dynamics is slow; a cheap Monte Carlo cartoon is a **self-avoiding random walk** on a 2D square lattice [Yue et al., 1995]:

- From the tip, step only to an **empty** neighbor (three choices at most once the previous site is excluded).
- At each new site, drop an H or P with $P(\mathrm{H})>P(\mathrm{P})$.
- Stop when the tip has no free neighbor (or you hit a length cap).

Energy of a finished chain:

$$
E = -\varepsilon f,
$$

where $\varepsilon>0$ and $f$ counts **non-bonded H–H neighbor pairs** (lattice neighbors that are not consecutive along the backbone). P–P and H–P contacts do not lower $E$. Compact cores with many H–H contacts are the toy “native” states.
