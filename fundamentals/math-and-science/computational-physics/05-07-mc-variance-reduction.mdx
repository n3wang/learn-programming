# 5.7 Monte Carlo variance reduction

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §5.7. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [5.6 Mean-value theorem and high-D Monte Carlo](./05-06-mean-value-nd). Labs: [5.7b Variance-reduction labs](./05-07b-mc-variance-reduction-labs).

## When plain sampling wastes effort

Crude Monte Carlo draws $x$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ and averages $f(x)$. That is fine when $f$ is smooth and comparable everywhere. It is wasteful when $f$ is **rapidly varying** — for example a narrow Gaussian peak on a wide interval. Most samples land where $f\approx 0$ and barely change the average, while the rare hits near the peak dominate both the estimate and its variance. Raising $N$ still helps like $1/\sqrt{N}$, but you are paying for samples that teach you almost nothing.

## Control-variate idea

Pick a comparison function $g$ that tracks $f$ closely,

$$
\bigl|f(x)-g(x)\bigr|\le\varepsilon,
$$

and whose integral is known exactly:

$$
J=\int_a^b g(x)\,dx.
$$

Then rewrite the target as a small correction plus the known piece:

$$
I=\int_a^b f=\int_a^b\bigl(f-g\bigr)+J.
$$

Estimate only the residual by Monte Carlo:

$$
I\simeq\frac{b-a}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(f(x_i)-g(x_i)\bigr)+J
$$

(with $x_i$ uniform on $[a,b]$; on $[0,1]$ the factor $b-a$ is $1$).

## Goal: shrink the variance

The method pays off when

$$
\mathrm{Var}(f-g)<\mathrm{Var}(f).
$$

If $g$ mirrors the shape of $f$, the residual $f-g$ is flatter and less noisy under uniform sampling. Exact $J$ absorbs the bulk of the integral; Monte Carlo only has to resolve a small leftover. A simple textbook calibration on $[0,1]$ is $f(x)=e^{-x}$ with $g(x)=1-x$ (so $J=1/2$): the control-variate estimate typically beats plain mean-value MC at the same $N$ and seed.
