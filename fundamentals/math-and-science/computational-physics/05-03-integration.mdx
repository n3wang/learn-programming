# 5.3 Integration algorithms

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §5.3–§5.3.5. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [5.2 Extrapolated difference](./05-02-extrapolated-diff). Labs: [5.3b Integration labs](./05-03b-integration-labs).

## Problem: integrate a spectrum

A counter records a **rate** $dN/dt$ — particles per unit time. The number that arrived in the first second is the area under that rate curve:

$$
N(1)=\int_0^1 \frac{dN(t)}{dt}\,dt.
$$

You may have no antiderivative in closed form — only samples of the rate. The job is numerical quadrature: turn the integral into a finite, stable sum.

## Box counting (Riemann sums)

Graph-paper counting of boxes under a curve is the oldest numerical integration picture; the name **quadrature** stuck even for rules far past plain boxes.

The Riemann definition is the limit of equal-width boxes as the width $h\to 0$:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{h\to 0}\Biggl[h\sum_{i=1}^{(b-a)/h} f(x_i)\Biggr].
$$

On a computer there is no infinitesimal $h$. Every practical rule is a **weighted sum** of $N$ samples:

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\simeq\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i)\,w_i.
$$

Choose nodes $x_i\in[a,b]$ and weights $w_i$. As $N\to\infty$ (with sensible $w_i$) you recover the integral for nice $f$; for some polynomials a clever finite rule is already exact.

Figure 5.2 shows the idea: four equal panels of width $h$, five marked abscissae from $a$ to $b$. The shaded rectangles are one concrete choice of heights (here left-endpoint samples).

![Figure 5.2 — area under f(x) broken into equal-width boxes; five sample points from a to b.](/img/computational-physics/fig-5-2-riemann.svg)

**Equal-width left boxes.** With $h=(b-a)/N$ and samples at the left edge of each panel,

$$
\int_a^b f\simeq h\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} f(a+ih).
$$

Midpoint and right-endpoint rules only change where $f$ is evaluated inside each panel; trapezoid and Simpson change the **weights**. Same skeleton: $\sum f_i w_i$.

## Equal-spacing workhorses: trapezoid and Simpson

Partition $[a,b]$ into $N$ panels of width $h=(b-a)/N$ and write $f_i=f(a+ih)$.

**Composite trapezoid** connects neighboring samples with straight chords:

$$
\int_a^b f\simeq\frac{h}{2}\Bigl(f_0+2f_1+2f_2+\cdots+2f_{N-1}+f_N\Bigr).
$$

**Composite Simpson** needs even $N$ and fits a parabola on each pair of panels:

$$
\int_a^b f\simeq\frac{h}{3}\Bigl(f_0+4f_1+2f_2+4f_3+\cdots+4f_{N-1}+f_N\Bigr).
$$

## Simple integration error estimates

Choose a rule that reaches a useful answer with the **fewest** samples. A crude estimate of the algorithmic error $\mathcal{E}$ for equal-spacing rules expands $f$ in a Taylor series about a panel midpoint, then multiplies by the number of panels to cover $[a,b]$. Relative error is $\varepsilon=|\mathcal{E}|\big/\big|\int_a^b f\big|$ when the integral is not tiny.

Standard leading results for smooth $f$:

$$
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{trap}}\sim O(h^{2})\sim\frac{(b-a)\,h^{2}}{12}\langle f''\rangle,
\qquad
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{simp}}\sim O(h^{4})\sim\frac{(b-a)\,h^{4}}{180}\langle f^{(4)}\rangle.
$$

Those scalings are illuminating:

- Simpson needs fewer points and has less error than trapezoid for the same smooth $f$.
- With Simpson (and other higher-order rules) you can push error near machine precision.
- The best numerical integral is **not** $N\to\infty$. A modest $N\lesssim 10^{3}$ often sits near the sweet spot; larger $N$ mostly buys round-off.

## Higher-order algorithms (Romberg)

As with differentiation, if you know how the error depends on $h$, you can cancel the leading term. Suppose trapezoid-like evaluations satisfy

$$
A(h)\simeq\int_a^b f+\alpha h^{2}+\beta h^{4}+\cdots,
\qquad
A\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)\simeq\int_a^b f+\frac{\alpha h^{2}}{4}+\frac{\beta h^{4}}{16}+\cdots.
$$

Then the combination

$$
A\simeq\frac{4}{3}A\!\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{3}A(h)
\simeq\int_a^b f-\frac{\beta h^{4}}{4}+\cdots
$$

removes the $h^{2}$ piece (**Romberg** extrapolation). It works only while that $h^{2}$ term actually dominates.

## Elementary weights and a sanity check

Equal-interval “single-block” rules use a small stencil that you tile across $[a,b]$. Shared endpoints of adjacent blocks are counted twice when blocks are strung together. Typical elementary weights:

| Rule | Panels / block | Weights (factor $h$ omitted) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trapezoid | 1 | $\tfrac12,\;\tfrac12$ |
| Simpson | 2 | $\tfrac13,\;\tfrac43,\;\tfrac13$ |
| Three-eighths | 3 | $\tfrac38,\;\tfrac98,\;\tfrac98,\;\tfrac38$ |
| Milne (Boole) | 4 | $\tfrac{2}{45}(7,32,12,32,7)$ |

Quick audit: sum only the weights of any complete rule. For $f\equiv 1$ the integral is $b-a$, so

$$
\sum_i w_i = h\times N_{\mathrm{intervals}} = b-a.
$$

If your weight list fails that identity, the stencil is wrong before you ever touch a nontrivial $f$.
