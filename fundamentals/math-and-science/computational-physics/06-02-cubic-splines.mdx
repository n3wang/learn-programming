# 6.5.2 Cubic spline interpolation and quadrature

> **Source:** Ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §6.5.2–6.5.3. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [6.5b Lagrange interpolation labs](./06-01b-lagrange-interpolation-labs). Labs: [6.5.2b Cubic splines labs](./06-02b-cubic-splines-labs).

## From local polynomials to splines

A three-point (parabolic) local Lagrange fit already avoids the wild oscillations of a single high-degree global polynomial. **Cubic splines** push the idea further: fit a cubic polynomial in each interval, but additionally force the first and second derivatives to match at every shared node. The result is a curve that looks and behaves like a physical drafting spline — a flexible strip of material bent through pins at the data points — smooth in both slope and curvature.

Because each piece is only a cubic, splines never show the runaway oscillations of a high-degree Lagrange fit, and because the pieces are matched smoothly, the composite curve can be safely differentiated and integrated. That matters physically: if the interpolated quantity is a potential, its spline derivative gives a well-behaved force.

## Matching conditions and panel integral

Interior matching alone leaves two free second derivatives at the ends. Common closures:

- **Natural spline:** $g''(a)=g''(b)=0$.
- **Given first derivatives** at the endpoints (measured or estimated) to back out $g''(a)$, $g''(b)$.
- **Given (or estimated) second derivatives** directly.

Solving the linear system by hand is mechanical — in practice delegate to a library spline routine rather than rederiving each time. When $g$ is known only at nodes, summing cubic panel integrals is about as accurate as tabulated quadrature gets; if you can evaluate $g$ freely, prefer Gauss. Finer panels do not automatically help — subtractive cancellation in the endpoint evaluations can grow.
