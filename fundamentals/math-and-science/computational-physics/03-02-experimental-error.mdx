# 3.2 Experimental error investigation

> **Source:** Error-tradeoff ideas from *Computational Physics* (Landau, Páez, Bordeianu), §3.2. Rewritten in original wording. Continues [3.1 Errors](./03-01-errors).

An algorithm is not “done” when it prints a number. You still have to decide:

1. **Does it converge**, and how fast?
2. Even if it converges, **how many digits are real**?
3. **What did those digits cost** (steps, and therefore round-off)?

“Just run longer” is not a method. Some expansions only work in a patch of parameter space and stall. Even a series that is uniformly convergent can **lose** digits if you keep adding terms: approximation error falls, round-off error climbs, and the sum of the two has a **best** $N$, not an infinite one. A cheaper algorithm is often more accurate for that reason — fewer steps, less garbage.

## Two errors, one $N$

Let the method take $N$ steps. Algorithmic error typically drops as an inverse power once $N$ is large:

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}} \simeq \frac{\alpha}{N^{\beta}}.
$$

$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are empirical; they can drift, and the power law is an $N\to\infty$ cartoon. That the error **does** fall is what it means for the algorithm to work.

Round-off, if the per-step mistakes are uncorrelated, still looks like the random walk from 3.1:

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{ro}} \simeq \sqrt{N}\,\epsilon_m.
$$

Add them:

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}} \simeq \frac{\alpha}{N^{\beta}} + \sqrt{N}\,\epsilon_m.
$$

Small $N$: the first term wins (you are still converging). Large $N$: the square root takes over and the answer gets **worse**.

Plot $\log_{10}$ of the **relative** error versus $\log_{10} N$. The negative of that ordinate is the number of decimal places you can defend: if $(\mathcal{A}-A(N))/\mathcal{A}\simeq 10^{-9}$, then $\log_{10}|(\mathcal{A}-A(N))/\mathcal{A}|\simeq -9$.

<FormulaExplorer preset="errorTradeoff" />

**Theorem (best $N$).** Total error is smallest near the $N$ where $\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}\simeq\epsilon_{\mathrm{ro}}$. Past that trough, extra steps buy round-off, not truth.

On a realistic integrator you would see a steep drop (power-law convergence), then a noisy slow rise. The trough for a careful double-precision run often sits around $10^{-14}$ on that plot — roughly fourteen digits — before the walk upward begins. Quit **before** the uptick.

## You do not know $\mathcal{A}$

If you already had the exact answer, you would not be computing. You might have a **cousin** problem with a known result, and you can tune the method there. Or you watch $A(N)$ itself.

While round-off is still small,

$$
A(N) \simeq \mathcal{A} + \frac{\alpha}{N^{\beta}}.
$$

Run $2N$ steps and subtract:

$$
A(N)-A(2N) \simeq \frac{\alpha}{N^{\beta}}\bigl(1-2^{-\beta}\bigr).
$$

Plot

$$
\log_{10}\left|\frac{A(N)-A(2N)}{A(2N)}\right|
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\log_{10} N.
$$

A **straight steep drop** means you are in the converging region; the slope is $-\beta$. When the line flattens and then wanders upward, round-off has arrived. A good production $N$ sits just before that change.

In the mantissa you will see the same story without a plot: stable digits march rightward as $N$ grows, then the last places start to flicker and the flicker slowly gets worse. Stop before the flicker.

## Stationary point of the cartoon

Take $\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}=1/N^{2}$ and $\epsilon_{\mathrm{ro}}=\sqrt{N}\,\epsilon_m$:

$$
\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}+\sqrt{N}\,\epsilon_m.
$$

Set the derivative to zero:

$$
\frac{d\epsilon_{\mathrm{tot}}}{dN}=-\frac{2}{N^{3}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\epsilon_m}{\sqrt{N}}=0
\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad
N^{\beta+1/2}=\frac{2\alpha\beta}{\epsilon_m}
\quad(\alpha=1,\;\beta=2\Rightarrow N^{5/2}=4/\epsilon_m).
$$

For a general power, $N^{*}=(2\alpha\beta/\epsilon_m)^{1/(\beta+1/2)}$. Slide $\beta$ and $\epsilon_m$ on the first chart; the dashed line is that $N^{*}$.

A method with $\epsilon_{\mathrm{app}}\simeq 2/N^{4}$ reaches its trough at **smaller** $N$ (higher power in the denominator of $N^{*}$). Fewer steps also means a shorter random walk, so the round-off piece is smaller too. The “better” algorithm is not only faster — it is often **cleaner**.
