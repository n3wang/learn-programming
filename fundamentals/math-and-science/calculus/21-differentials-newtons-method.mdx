# 21. Differentials · Newton's Method

> **Adapted from:** Classical single-variable calculus (linear approximation and Newton root-finding). Rewritten for teaching — not a verbatim textbook dump.

If $f$ is differentiable at $x$, then

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x},\qquad \Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x).
$$

So for small $\Delta x$,

$$
\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\sim f'(x)
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\Delta y\sim f'(x)\,\Delta x. \tag{21.1}
$$

Rearranged:

$$
f(x+\Delta x)\sim f(x)+f'(x)\,\Delta x. \tag{21.2}
$$

This is the **linear** (or tangent-line) approximation to $f$ near $x$.

:::caution No free error bound
Formula (21.2) does not by itself say *how small* $\Delta x$ must be for a chosen number of correct decimals. That needs remainder estimates (later: Taylor with remainder).
:::

## Example 21.1 — Estimate $\sqrt{16.2}$

Take $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $x=16$, $\Delta x=0.2$. Then $f(16)=4$ and

$$
f'(x)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\implies f'(16)=\frac18.
$$

So

$$
\sqrt{16.2}\sim 4+\frac18(0.2)=4.025.
$$

(True value $\approx 4.0249$ — three decimals agree.)

## Example 21.2 — Estimate $\sin(0.1)$

Take $f(x)=\sin x$, $x=0$, $\Delta x=0.1$. Then $f(0)=0$, $f'(0)=\cos 0=1$, so

$$
\sin(0.1)\sim 0.1.
$$

True value $\approx 0.0998$. Same idea: **$\sin u\sim u$ for small $u$**.

## The differential

The product on the right of (21.1) is the **differential** of $f$:

$$
df = f'(x)\,\Delta x.
$$

It depends on both $x$ and $\Delta x$. For small $\Delta x$,

$$
f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)\sim df. \tag{21.3}
$$

  caption="Fig. 21-1 — RT = df approximates RQ = Δy"

In the figure, the tangent $\mathscr{L}$ at $P$ has slope $f'(x)$, so the vertical rise over $\Delta x$ is $df$. The true change along the curve is $\Delta y$; they nearly match when $\Delta x$ is small.

### Writing $dx$

If $f(x)=x$, then $dx=\Delta x$. Hence we may rewrite

$$
df = f'(x)\,dx,
$$

and when $\Delta x\neq 0$,

$$
\frac{df}{dx}=f'(x).
$$

With $y=f(x)$ one writes $dy$ for $df$ — the familiar $dy/dx$.

### Algebra of differentials

For functions $u,v$ and constant $c$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
d(c)&=0,&
d(cu)&=c\,du,&
d(u+v)&=du+dv,\\
d(uv)&=u\,dv+v\,du,&
d\!\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)&=\frac{v\,du-u\,dv}{v^{2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Examples: $d(\tan x)=\sec^{2}x\,\Delta x$, and $d(x^{3}-2x)=(3x^{2}-2)\,\Delta x$.

## Newton's method

Suppose $x_0$ is near a root of $f(x)=0$ and $f$ is differentiable. The tangent at $x_0$ usually hits the $x$-axis at a better guess $x_1$.

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-21-2-newton.svg"
  alt="Fig. 21-2 Newton tangent intercept"
  caption="Fig. 21-2 — tangent at x₀ meets the axis at x₁"
  maxWidth={340}
/>

Point-slope for the tangent:

$$
y-f(x_0)=f'(x_0)(x-x_0).
$$

At the intercept $(x_1,0)$, if $f'(x_0)\neq 0$,

$$
x_1=x_0-\frac{f(x_0)}{f'(x_0)}.
$$

Repeating from $x_1,x_2,\ldots$ gives

$$
x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}. \tag{21.5}
$$

:::warning When Newton fails
If $f'(x_n)\approx 0$, or the seed is far from a simple root, the sequence can diverge, cycle, or jump to another root. Always plot or sanity-check the start.
:::

---

## Example 21.3 — Approximate $\sqrt{3}$

Solve $f(x)=x^{2}-3=0$. Then $f'(x)=2x$ and (21.5) becomes

$$
x_{n+1}=\frac{x_n^{2}+3}{2x_n}. \tag{21.6}
$$

With $x_0=1$:

| $n$ | $x_n$ (approx.) |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $2$ |
| $2$ | $1.75$ |
| $3$ | $1.732142857$ |
| $4$ | $1.73205081$ |
| $5$–$6$ | $1.732050808$ (stable) |

So $\sqrt{3}\approx 1.732050808$ to the digits shown.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- $\Delta y\sim f'(x)\,\Delta x$ and $f(x+\Delta x)\sim f(x)+f'(x)\,\Delta x$
- $df=f'(x)\,dx$; algebra mirrors derivative rules
- Newton: $x_{n+1}=x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$ — fast near a simple root, not magic from a bad start
:::
