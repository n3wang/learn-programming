## Continuity of $\cos x$ and $\sin x$

For any fixed $\theta$,

$$
\lim_{h\to 0}\cos(\theta+h)=\cos\theta
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\lim_{h\to 0}\sin(\theta+h)=\sin\theta.
$$

On the unit circle (Fig. 17-1), as $h\to 0$ the terminal point $C$ for angle $\theta+h$ approaches the point $B$ for angle $\theta$. Hence the $x$-coordinate $\cos(\theta+h)$ approaches $\cos\theta$, and the $y$-coordinate $\sin(\theta+h)$ approaches $\sin\theta$.

## Two key limits

To differentiate $\sin$ and $\cos$ we need

$$
\begin{align}
\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}&=1 \tag{17.1}\\[0.4em]
\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta}&=0 \tag{17.2}
\end{align}
$$

(Use **radians** — these limits fail in degrees.)

From (17.1) we get (17.2):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta}
&=\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta}\cdot\frac{1+\cos\theta}{1+\cos\theta}
=\frac{\sin^{2}\theta}{\theta(1+\cos\theta)}
=\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\cdot\frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Hence

$$
\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\theta}
=\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}\cdot\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}
=1\cdot\frac{0}{1+1}=0.
$$

## Derivatives of sine and cosine

$$
\begin{align}
D_{x}(\sin x)&=\cos x \tag{17.3}\\[0.35em]
D_{x}(\cos x)&=-\sin x \tag{17.4}
\end{align}
$$

The proof of (17.3) expands $\sin(x+h)$ with an addition formula, then applies (17.1)–(17.2). From (17.3) and the cofunction identity $\cos x=\sin(\pi/2-x)$, the Chain Rule gives

$$
D_{x}(\cos x)
=D_{x}\!\left(\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\right)
=\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)\cdot(-1)
=-\sin x.
$$

## Graph of $\sin x$

Since $\sin(x+2\pi)=\sin x$, it is enough to sketch one period $[0,2\pi]$.

Critical numbers where $D_{x}(\sin x)=\cos x=0$ in $[0,2\pi]$: $x=\pi/2$ and $x=3\pi/2$.

Second derivative: $D_{x}^{2}(\sin x)=-\sin x$.

- At $\pi/2$: $-\sin(\pi/2)=-1<0$ → relative **maximum** $(\pi/2,1)$
- At $3\pi/2$: $-\sin(3\pi/2)=1>0$ → relative **minimum** $(3\pi/2,-1)$

Increasing where $\cos x>0$ (first and fourth quadrants on $[0,2\pi]$): $0<x<\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2<x<2\pi$.

Concave up where $-\sin x>0$ (third and fourth): $\pi<x<2\pi$. Inflection at $(\pi,0)$, and also at $(0,0)$ and $(2\pi,0)$ by periodicity.

## Graph of $\cos x$

Cofunction: $\sin(\pi/2+x)=\cos x$. So the graph of $\cos x$ is the graph of $\sin x$ shifted **left** by $\pi/2$.

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-17-3-cos.svg"
  alt="Fig. 17-3 graph of y = cos x"
  caption="Fig. 17-3 — y = cos x (sin shifted left by π/2)"
/>

Both graphs are repeating waves of length $2\pi$. Multiplying the **argument** by a constant changes the period/frequency; multiplying the **value** changes the amplitude.

### Example 17.1 — $y=\cos 3x$

$\cos 3(x+2\pi/3)=\cos(3x+2\pi)=\cos 3x$, so the period is $p=2\pi/3$. Over an interval of length $2\pi$ there are **3** waves — the **frequency** $f=3$. In general $pf=2\pi$, so $f=2\pi/p$.

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-17-4-cos3x.svg"
  alt="Fig. 17-4 y = cos 3x"
  caption="Fig. 17-4 — y = cos 3x (period 2π/3, frequency 3)"
/>

For $b>0$, $\sin bx$ and $\cos bx$ have frequency $b$ and period $2\pi/b$.

### Example 17.2 — $y=2\sin x$

Double the $y$-values of $\sin x$. Period and frequency unchanged ($p=2\pi$, $f=1$); **amplitude** (max height) is $2$.

If $b>0$, then $y=A\sin bx$ and $y=A\cos bx$ have period $2\pi/b$, frequency $b$, and amplitude $|A|$.

## Other trigonometric functions

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tan x&=\frac{\sin x}{\cos x},&
\cot x&=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}=\frac{1}{\tan x},\\
\sec x&=\frac{1}{\cos x},&
\csc x&=\frac{1}{\sin x}.
\end{aligned}
$$

### Derivatives

$$
\begin{align}
D_{x}(\tan x)&=\sec^{2} x \tag{17.5}\\
D_{x}(\cot x)&=-\csc^{2} x \tag{17.6}\\
D_{x}(\sec x)&=\tan x\,\sec x \tag{17.7}\\
D_{x}(\csc x)&=-\cot x\,\csc x \tag{17.8}
\end{align}
$$

(Quotient / reciprocal rules from $\sin$ and $\cos$.)

<GuidedChoiceExplanation preset="trig-other-derivatives" />

### Other relationships

$$
\tan^{2} x+1=\sec^{2} x \tag{17.9}
$$

Proof: $\dfrac{\sin^{2} x}{\cos^{2} x}+1=\dfrac{\sin^{2} x+\cos^{2} x}{\cos^{2} x}=\sec^{2} x$.

$$
\tan(x+\pi)=\tan x,\qquad \cot(x+\pi)=\cot x \tag{17.10}
$$

So $\tan$ and $\cot$ have period $\pi$ (not $2\pi$).

$$
\tan(-x)=-\tan x,\qquad \cot(-x)=-\cot x \tag{17.11}
$$

(Odd functions: $\sin$ odd, $\cos$ even.)

## Graph of $y=\tan x$

Period $\pi$: sketch on $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$. Oddness: draw $(0,\pi/2)$ and reflect through the origin.

Vertical asymptotes where $\cos x=0$: $x=\pm\pi/2$ (and every odd multiple of $\pi/2$ by periodicity).

$D_{x}(\tan x)=\sec^{2} x>0$ wherever defined → strictly increasing on each interval between asymptotes.

$$
D_{x}^{2}(\tan x)=2\sec x\cdot(\tan x\sec x)=2\tan x\,\sec^{2} x,
$$

so concave up when $\tan x>0$ (e.g. $0<x<\pi/2$), with an inflection at $(0,0)$.

| $x$ | $\tan x$ |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $0$ |
| $\pi/6$ | $\sqrt{3}/3\approx 0.58$ |
| $\pi/4$ | $1$ |
| $\pi/3$ | $\sqrt{3}\approx 1.73$ |

## Graph of $y=\sec x$

Asymptotes where $\cos x=0$: $x=(2n+1)\pi/2$. Period $2\pi$ like $\cos$. Also $|\sec x|\ge 1$ because $|\cos x|\le 1$.

Critical numbers from $D_{x}(\sec x)=\tan x\sec x=0$: $x=0$ (relative min) and $x=\pi$ (relative max) on $(-\pi,\pi)$, by the first-derivative test.

$$
D_{x}^{2}(\sec x)=\sec x\,(\tan^{2} x+\sec^{2} x),
$$

so no inflection on $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ (second derivative keeps the sign of $\sec x>0$ there) — concave upward.

## Angles between curves

The **angle of inclination** of a nonvertical line $\mathscr{L}$ is the smaller counterclockwise angle $\alpha$ from the positive $x$-axis to $\mathscr{L}$. If $m$ is the slope, then

$$
m=\tan\alpha.
$$

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-17-9-inclination.svg"
  alt="Fig. 17-9 inclination angle α"
  caption="Fig. 17-9 — inclination α"
  maxWidth={280}
/>

Reason (Fig. 17-10): a parallel unit-direction ray meets the circle at $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)$, so slope $=\sin\alpha/\cos\alpha=\tan\alpha$.

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-17-10-slope-tan.svg"
  alt="Fig. 17-10 slope equals tan α"
  caption="Fig. 17-10 — m = tan α"
  maxWidth={280}
/>

The **angle between two curves** at an intersection $P$ is the smaller of the two angles between their tangent lines at $P$. If the slopes are $m_1$ and $m_2$,

$$
\tan\phi=\left|\frac{m_2-m_1}{1+m_1 m_2}\right|
$$

when $1+m_1 m_2\neq 0$. If $1+m_1 m_2=0$, the tangents are perpendicular ($\phi=\pi/2$).

:::important Key takeaways
- Continuity of $\sin$ and $\cos$ from the unit circle; key limits $(\sin\theta)/\theta\to 1$ and $(1-\cos\theta)/\theta\to 0$ (radians).
- $D_x(\sin x)=\cos x$, $D_x(\cos x)=-\sin x$; then $\tan,\cot,\sec,\csc$ by quotient/reciprocal.
- $A\sin bx$: amplitude $|A|$, period $2\pi/b$, frequency $b$.
- $\tan$ has period $\pi$ and vertical asymptotes at odd multiples of $\pi/2$; $\sec=1/\cos$ shares those poles.
- Inclination: $m=\tan\alpha$; angle between curves = acute angle between tangents.
:::
