# 19. Rectilinear and Circular Motion

> **Adapted from:** Classical single-variable calculus (position–velocity–acceleration on a line; free fall; angular velocity on a circle). Rewritten for teaching — not a verbatim textbook dump.

Motion is calculus with a physical story: derivatives of position give velocity; derivatives of velocity give acceleration.

## Rectilinear motion

Motion on a **straight line**. Place a coordinate $s$ on that line; the object's position at time $t$ is

$$
s=f(t).
$$

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-19-1-rectilinear.svg"
  alt="Fig. 19-1 particle on a line"
  caption="Fig. 19-1 — position s = f(t)"
/>

Between $t$ and $t+\Delta t$, the change in position is $f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)$. The **average velocity** is

$$
\frac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}.
$$

(This “distance” can be negative if the object moves toward decreasing $s$.)

The **instantaneous velocity** is the limit:

$$
v=\lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{f(t+\Delta t)-f(t)}{\Delta t}=f'(t)=\frac{ds}{dt}.
$$

- If $v>0$ on an interval, $s$ is increasing (Theorem 13.7) — motion toward larger $s$.
- If $v<0$, motion toward smaller $s$.

**Speed** is $|v|$ — how fast, without direction (what a speedometer reports).

**Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity:

$$
a=\frac{dv}{dt}=\frac{d^{2}s}{dt^{2}}.
$$

### Example 19.1

$s=t^{2}-5t$ (miles, hours): $v=2t-5$ mi/h, $a=2$ mi/h². Velocity increases by $2$ mi/h every hour.

### Turning around

A change of direction occurs at a relative extremum of $s$, which requires $v=ds/dt=0$. The converse fails: $s=t^{3}$ at $t=0$ has $v=0$ but no turn.

### Example 19.2

$s=(t-2)^{2}$ (feet, seconds): $v=2(t-2)$, $a=2$.

- For $t<2$: $v<0$ — moving left.
- For $t>2$: $v>0$ — moving right.
- At $t=2$: $v=0$ — the object **turns**; it is not “standing still.” Standing still means $s$ is constant on a whole time interval.

## Motion under gravity (free fall)

Object thrown straight up or down (or released from rest); the only force is Earth's gravity. Put $s$ **upward** on the vertical line, with ground at $s=0$.

Physics: acceleration is constant

$$
a\approx -32\ \mathrm{ft/s^{2}}
\quad(\text{or }-9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^{2}}).
$$

Negative because upward is positive — gravity decreases $v$.

<LessonFigure
  src="/img/calculus/fig-19-4-free-fall.svg"
  alt="Fig. 19-4 free fall with upward positive"
  caption="Fig. 19-4 — upward +; a = −32 ft/s²"
  maxWidth={240}
/>

Since $dv/dt=-32$:

$$
v=v_{0}-32t \tag{19.1}
$$

with $v_{0}=v(0)$. Then $ds/dt=v$ gives

$$
s=s_{0}+v_{0}t-16t^{2} \tag{19.2}
$$

with $s_{0}=s(0)$.

(Reason: $D_t(v_{0}-32t)=-32=D_t v$, so the two velocities differ by a constant; they agree at $t=0$, so they are identical. Same argument for $s$.)

## Circular motion

Particle $P$ on a circle: describe the motion by the central angle $\theta=f(t)$ in **radians**. If the radius is $r$,

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.
$$

**Angular velocity**

$$
\omega=\frac{d\theta}{dt}.
$$

**Angular acceleration**

$$
\alpha=\frac{d\omega}{dt}=\frac{d^{2}\theta}{dt^{2}}.
$$

---

:::important Key takeaways
- Rectilinear: $v=ds/dt$, speed $=|v|$, $a=dv/dt=d^{2}s/dt^{2}$
- Sign of $v$ = direction; turns need $v=0$ at an extremum of $s$ (not every critical point)
- Free fall (upward +): $a=-32$ ft/s², $v=v_{0}-32t$, $s=s_{0}+v_{0}t-16t^{2}$
- Circular: $\omega=d\theta/dt$, $\alpha=d\omega/dt$; $(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$
:::
