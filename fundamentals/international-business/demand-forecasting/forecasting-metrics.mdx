# 8. Forecasting Metrics: Bias, MAE, MAPE, RMSE

> **Teaching rewrite** — the *Metrics* step of the framework (Part 2: measuring forecasting quality). Original wording; all numbers recomputed.

## Introduction

You can only improve what you measure. But the metric you choose shapes the forecasts people produce: one metric quietly rewards under-forecasting, another over-reacts to outliers, another ignores your most valuable products. There is no single perfect indicator — each avoids some traps and falls into others.

This lesson covers:

- **Accuracy vs bias** — two different questions
- How to compute **Bias**, **MAE**, **MAPE**, and **RMSE**, plus their **percentage** versions
- What each one rewards or punishes
- A **case study**: three forecasts, four metrics, four different winners

:::caution Measure against demand
Compare forecasts with **demand**, not constrained **sales** (lessons 2–3). Otherwise a forecast that predicted a shortage looks perfect.
:::

---

## 8.1 Accuracy and bias

| Concept | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | How **big** are the errors? (spread between forecast and actual, ignoring direction) |
| **Bias** | Which **direction** do errors lean? Are forecasts on average too high or too low? |

Think of a dartboard: darts tightly grouped but off to one side are **accurate-ish but biased**; darts scattered evenly around the bullseye are **unbiased but inaccurate**. You want tight **and** centred.

The two can conflict:

- **Unbiased but inaccurate** — swings wildly around demand → noisy signal to production.
- **Accurate but biased** — always a bit low → steady lost sales. (Consistent bias often comes from intentional manual edits; see the review-process lessons.)

---

## 8.2 Error and bias

**Error** for period t:

> e<sub>t</sub> = f<sub>t</sub> − d<sub>t</sub> (forecast minus demand)

Positive = over-forecast; negative = under-forecast.

**Bias** = average error = Σe<sub>t</sub> / n

**Bias%** = Σe<sub>t</sub> / Σd<sub>t</sub> (total error ÷ total demand over the **same** periods)

### Worked example (used throughout this lesson)

| Period | Demand | Forecast | Error | Abs. error | APE | Squared error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 10 | 15 | +5 | 5 | 50% | 25 |
| Feb | 5 | 12 | +7 | 7 | 140% | 49 |
| Mar | 8 | 5 | −3 | 3 | 37.5% | 9 |
| Apr | 10 | 8 | −2 | 2 | 20% | 4 |
| **Total** | **33** | **40** | **+7** | **17** | | **87** |

- **Bias** = 7 / 4 = **1.75** units
- **Bias%** = 7 / 33 = **21.2%** (over-forecasting)

Why scale? A bias of 43 units is excellent if average demand is 10,000, terrible if it is 20.

:::tip Attention points
- Compute bias over **many periods** (or many products), not one product in one period.
- Divide error and demand over the **same** time window — a classic spreadsheet mistake is dividing by demand from a longer range.
- Bias alone can hide big errors: +50 and −50 average to zero.
:::

**How much bias is normal?** Statistical and ML engines often end slightly **negative** across portfolios: they see old products declining but can’t foresee new launches. Human processes, by contrast, often drift **positive** under management pressure.

## 8.3 Mean Absolute Error (MAE)

> **MAE** = Σ|e<sub>t</sub>| / n  **MAE%** = Σ|e<sub>t</sub>| / Σd<sub>t</sub>

From the table: MAE = 17 / 4 = **4.25** units; MAE% = 17 / 33 = **51.5%**.

Compute MAE% from the **totals** at the end — not period by period (that would be MAPE).

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Simple to compute and explain | Minimising MAE pulls the forecast toward the **median** of demand, which is usually **below the mean** for skewed demand → tends to **under-forecast** |
| Easy to express as a % | |

**Median vs mean**: values 1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 6, 100 have median **4** but mean **13.2**.

---

## 8.4 Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)

> **MAPE** = (1/n) Σ |e<sub>t</sub>| / d<sub>t</sub> — each period divided by **its own** demand

From the table: (50% + 140% + 37.5% + 20%) / 4 = **61.9%**.

Why it is flawed:

- **Skewed weighting** — an error in a low-demand period counts far more than the same error in a high-demand period (Feb’s 7-unit error = 140%; Jan’s 5-unit error = 50%).
- **Asymmetric cap** — forecasting 0 can never exceed a 100% error, but over-forecasting has no ceiling → minimising MAPE **pushes forecasts down**.
- **Undefined** whenever demand is zero.

:::caution Naming confusion
Many people compute MAE% (total abs error ÷ total demand) and call it “MAPE”. Always state the formula when comparing numbers.
:::

## 8.5 Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)

> **RMSE** = √( Σe<sub>t</sub>² / n )  **RMSE%** = RMSE / average demand

Read the name backwards as a recipe: **error → square → mean → root**.

From the table: √(87 / 4) = √21.75 = **4.66** units; average demand = 33/4 = 8.25 → RMSE% = **56.5%**.

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Penalises **big errors** most — matching supply chain reality (large misses cause stock-outs and dead stock; small ones barely matter) | Harder to explain |
| Linked to safety-stock formulas | **Over-reacts to outliers** |
| Minimising it tends to give an **unbiased** forecast (it aims at the mean) | Doesn’t scale well across portfolios (lesson 11) |

## 8.6 Case study — three flat forecasts

A product has 21 periods of demand (total **8,019**, average ≈ **382**, median **254**). Demand is volatile: 170, 254, 360, 221, 351, **1,034**, 632, 142, 421, 200, 405, 904, **8**, 52, 528, 60, 97, **1,482**, 70, 233, 395.

Three (deliberately simple) flat forecasts:

| Forecast | Value every period | Bias% | MAE% | MAPE | RMSE% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 380 | **−0.5%** | 68.4% | 352.9% | **94.4%** |
| **2** | 260 | −31.9% | **65.7%** | 239.1% | 99.6% |
| **3** | 200 | −47.6% | 67.6% | **187.1%** | 105.7% |

Look at the bold “winners”:

- **Bias** and **RMSE** pick Forecast 1 (≈ the mean).
- **MAE** picks Forecast 2 (closest to the **median**, 254).
- **MAPE** picks Forecast 3 — the **lowest** forecast — exactly the downward pull described above.

Which would you choose for your supply chain? Lesson 9 settles it.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Track **accuracy** (size) **and** **bias** (direction) — they can conflict
- Error = forecast − demand; **Bias%** = total error ÷ total demand
- **MAE** is simple but aims at the **median** → slight under-forecast
- **MAPE** over-weights low-demand periods, rewards under-forecasting, breaks on zeros — avoid it
- **RMSE** punishes big errors, aims at the mean, but over-reacts to outliers
- Different metrics can crown **different** forecasts
:::
