# 7. Should We Reconcile Forecasts to Align Supply Chains?

> **Teaching rewrite** — the *Objective* step, across teams: one shared forecast, or aligned but different forecasts? Original wording; numbers are illustrative.

## Introduction

Supply chains need teams to act in concert: if marketing launches a product in Q2, supply must have stock by the end of Q1. A popular answer is the **one number forecast** — a single reconciled forecast everyone uses. An alternative is the **one number mindset** — shared data, assumptions, and events, but each team keeps the forecast that best fits its decisions, within tolerance of the others.

This lesson covers:

- Why different roles need **different granularities and horizons**
- The **one number forecast** and its three challenges
- Why the **forecast of the sum ≠ sum of the forecasts** (spot sales, life cycles)
- A worked **top-down vs bottom-up** comparison
- The **one number mindset** in practice

---

## 7.1 Different roles, different forecasts

| Role | Decision | Granularity | Horizon |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Logistics manager** | Which stores to ship to | SKU × store × day | Next days |
| **Plant manager** | What to produce | SKU × global × week | Next weeks |
| **Marketing strategist** | Which products to renew or launch | Brand × channel × quarter | 6–9 months |
| **Finance manager** | Cash-flow plan | Revenue × business unit × quarter | Next quarters |

Pattern: **short-term = granular**, **long-term = aggregated**. How do you keep all these aligned?

---

## 7.2 The one number forecast

One reconciled forecast, shared by everyone; different views are produced mechanically with **top-down, middle-out, and bottom-up** (lesson 5).

| Challenge | Why it bites |
|---|---|
| **Efficiency** | Reconciling weekly forecasts for thousands of SKUs over 18 months, every month, is a heavy recurring process |
| **Optimality** | The best forecast at one level is not the best at another — one size can’t fit all |
| **Alignment** | Agreeing a number doesn’t guarantee everyone uses it. Case studies describe companies that agreed a single S&OP number while departments still ran their own private forecasts for raw materials or daily logistics |

---

## 7.3 Different levels, different optimal forecasts

> The forecast of the sum is **not** the sum of the forecasts.

Forecast a family of products A, B, C two ways:

- **Bottom-up**: forecast A, B, C separately and add them.
- **Direct**: forecast the family total as its own time series.

The two totals differ — not just mathematically, but for business reasons:

### Spot sales and stock clearances

A family can look steady while individual SKUs show one-off spikes: flash promotions, surplus clearances, one-time contracts. A model can’t predict a clearance on a SKU that hasn’t sold for months. A **family-level** forecast can assume “some spot sales will happen” — but no top-down rule can say **which SKU** they will hit.

Also: **don’t feed past clearances into future forecasts** as if they were normal demand, or you will replenish dying items.

:::info Case snapshot: the green cars
In the 1990s a carmaker had too many green cars. Sales pushed them out with heavy discounts. Manufacturing, seeing green-car sales rise and unaware of the discounts, scheduled **more** green cars. The signal was promotion-driven clearance, not real demand.
:::

### Product life cycles

Engines forecast from history: they spot a product **declining** toward end-of-life but cannot foresee the **new SKU** that will replace it. Long-term **bottom-up** forecasts therefore drift **low**. A stable family forecast (top-down) doesn’t suffer this.

So an 18-month family forecast may be good, but translating it to SKUs is hard — you don’t know which SKUs will make up the family’s future sales.

## 7.4 Worked example: top-down vs bottom-up

A supermarket sells about **10,000 fruits a month**: mainly apples and pears, plus a few odd fruits. This month, besides apples and pears, it sold **500 kiwis** (not usually stocked in quantity).

| Fruit | Actual demand | **Bottom-up** forecast | Abs. error | **Top-down** forecast (10,000 scaled by apple/pear shares) | Abs. error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Apples | 4,500 | 4,500 | 0 | 4,737 | 237 |
| Pears | 5,000 | 5,000 | 0 | 5,263 | 263 |
| Kiwis | 500 | 0 | 500 | 0 | 500 |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **9,500** | **500** | **10,000** | **0** |
| **Sum of SKU errors** | | | **500** | | **1,000** |

- At the **family** level, top-down wins (error 0 vs 500).
- At the **SKU** level, bottom-up wins (500 vs 1,000).
- Scaling the apple/pear forecast up to 10,000 doesn’t help SKUs — it **adds** error, because the extra 500 belongs to some unpredictable “odd fruit”, not to apples and pears.

Scale factor check: 10,000 / 9,500 ≈ 1.0526; 4,500 × 1.0526 ≈ 4,737; 5,000 × 1.0526 ≈ 5,263.

:::info Advanced — probabilistic forecasts
Reconciling **quantile** forecasts is even harder: item demands are correlated (store-wide promotions lift everything; customers switch between fruits), so the distribution of the total is **not** the sum of the item distributions — e.g. the 95th percentile of apples + pears is not the 95th percentile of apples plus that of pears. **Point** (mean) forecasts, by contrast, add up cleanly. Forecasting competitions on hierarchical retail data (e.g. M5, 2020) asked for separate distributions per level for this reason.
:::

## 7.5 The one number mindset

Instead of forcing every team onto one number, align everyone on **one set of data, assumptions, and events** — and let each team forecast at its own level.

| Rule | In practice |
|---|---|
| **Same data** | One reconciled source of demand history, prices, and master data. Two teams must never forecast from different histories |
| **Same events** | A formal channel — typically S&OP — to share price changes, campaigns, launches, competitor moves |
| **Visible forecasts** | Every team can see the others’ forecasts |
| **Tolerance band** | Small differences are natural; large ones (e.g. **> 5%** at global level) trigger a review |

Benefits: each team uses the model and granularity that fit its decisions, forecast quality improves, and meetings shift from **arguing over numbers** to **discussing plans**.

### Worked example: S&OP vs weekly warehouse forecast

- **S&OP** forecast: country × month — **12,000** units for next month.
- **Logistics** forecast: warehouse × week, built from the same history with a different model — weeks sum to **12,480** for the month.

Gap = 480 / 12,000 = **4%** → within a 5% tolerance; keep both.

If logistics had summed to **13,200** (**10%** over), planners would investigate — e.g. a promotion known in logistics but not discussed in S&OP — and either share the information or **scale** the weekly forecast toward the S&OP total (× 12,000/13,200 ≈ 0.909).

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Different roles need different **granularities and horizons**
- A **one number forecast** helps clarity but costs **efficiency**, sacrifices **optimality**, and doesn’t guarantee **alignment**
- The **forecast of the sum ≠ sum of the forecasts** — spot sales and life cycles show up at different levels
- Forcing SKU forecasts to match a family total can **destroy** SKU accuracy
- A **one number mindset** aligns **data, assumptions, and events**; teams keep fit-for-purpose forecasts within a **tolerance band**
:::
