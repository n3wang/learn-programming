# 1. Demand Planning Excellence

> **Teaching rewrite** of demand-planning best practices, organised around a five-step framework. Original wording and examples; numbers are illustrative.

## Introduction

A supply chain makes thousands of decisions every day: what to buy, how much to produce, where to ship, how much to keep in stock. Almost all of them depend on one unknown — **how much customers will want in the future**. The better that estimate, the better the decisions: fewer shortages, less obsolete stock, more sales, lower cost.

A demand planner is like the lookout on a ship with a spyglass. The lookout does not steer; the job is to report what is on the horizon — accurately and without bias — so the crew on deck can decide what to do.

This lesson covers:

- What **demand planning excellence** means: **efficacy** and **efficiency**
- Why **accuracy alone is not the goal**
- The **five-step framework**: Objective → Data → Metrics → Baseline model → Review process
- How the framework changes for **short-, mid-, and long-term** forecasts

---

## 1.1 Efficacy and efficiency

| Pillar | Meaning | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Efficacy** | Forecasts actually help decision-makers | “Does this forecast change a decision for the better?” |
| **Efficiency** | The team spends as little time as possible producing them | “Could a model or a rule do this work instead of a person?” |

The aim is to **work smarter, not harder**: automate what can be automated, and point human effort where it adds the most value.

:::caution Accuracy is necessary, not sufficient
A forecast can be accurate and still useless — for example, if it is made at the wrong level of detail (country per month when the warehouse needs SKU per week) or if it forecasts the wrong thing (shipments instead of demand). **Usefulness** is the goal; accuracy is one ingredient.
:::

### Worked example: accurate but useless

A planner forecasts total monthly demand for a product family at **10,000 units** and the actual is **10,100** — 99% accurate. But replenishment decisions are made **per SKU per warehouse per week**. Inside the family, one SKU sold 40% more than its share, another 40% less. The family forecast was excellent; the stock was in the wrong places.

## 1.2 The five-step framework

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  A[1. Objective] --> B[2. Data]
  B --> C[3. Metrics]
  C --> D[4. Baseline model]
  D --> E[5. Review process]
```

| Step | Core question | Key ideas |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Objective** | What do you need to forecast, and why? | Start from the **decision** supported; choose aggregation level (product, location, time bucket) and horizon |
| **2. Data** | What data feeds the forecast? | Collect **unconstrained demand**, not constrained sales; gather **demand drivers** (promotions, prices, launches) |
| **3. Metrics** | How do you judge quality? | Measure **accuracy** and **bias**; compare with **benchmarks**; weight metrics for broad portfolios |
| **4. Baseline model** | How do you generate a forecast automatically? | Time-series, causal, or machine-learning models create a **baseline** with minimal manual work |
| **5. Review process** | Who adjusts the baseline, and does it help? | Humans add information the model lacks; track it with **forecast value added (FVA)** |

### Step 1 — Objective

Always start with **why**:

- *Why* do we forecast demand?
- *Which decisions* does this forecast support?
- (And therefore) *Who* will use it?

The decision sets the **granularity** (SKU × store × week? brand × country × month?) and the **horizon** (next week? next year?). Different teams may need different forecasts, made with different models and processes.

### Step 2 — Data

The most important data is **unconstrained demand** — what customers asked for, not what you managed to ship (the next two lessons). Next come **demand drivers**: promotions, prices, product launches, events, weather.

### Step 3 — Metrics

Track both **accuracy** (how big are the errors?) and **bias** (do errors lean consistently one way?). A **benchmark** — e.g. a simple naive or moving-average forecast — tells you whether your process is actually any good.

### Step 4 — Baseline model

Planners should not type every number by hand. A model generates a **baseline** for all items, using as many relevant insights as possible. Humans then review only where they can add value.

### Step 5 — Review process

Sales, marketing, planners, and customers may know things the model does not (a lost tender, a competitor’s launch). Their edits should be **measured**: the **forecast value added (FVA)** framework compares the accuracy of each step (baseline → planner edit → sales edit → final) to see who improves the forecast and who makes it worse. Without it, forecast reviews turn into **influence wars**.

## 1.3 Three horizons, three set-ups

| | **Short term** | **Mid term (S&OP)** | **Long term (budget)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision | What to ship to each store this week | What to produce over the next months | Annual budget, capacity, launches |
| Granularity | SKU × store × week | SKU (global) × month | Brand/segment × revenue × month/quarter |
| Update cadence | Weekly | Monthly | Yearly (with re-forecasts) |
| Who reviews | Few planners, only high-value items | Many stakeholders: sales, marketing, finance, supply, customers | Management, finance, marketing |
| Model | **Machine learning** using granular drivers (promotions, prices, shortages, weather) | Statistical baseline + reviewed inputs; **value-weighted** metrics; **FVA** | **Causal/driver model** to build scenarios (price, marketing, new products) |
| Main risk | Too many items to review by hand | Functional bias in overrides | Intentional and cognitive bias, wishful thinking |

### Worked example: where to spend review time

A planner has **5,000** SKU-store combinations to review each week and **10 hours**. At 2 minutes each, reviewing everything would take 5,000 × 2 = **10,000 minutes ≈ 167 hours**. Instead:

- Let the model handle the long tail.
- Review the top **300** items by value or uncertainty: 300 × 2 = **600 minutes = 10 hours**.

The team’s time goes where a human is most likely to add value.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Forecasts exist to **support decisions** — usefulness beats raw accuracy
- Excellence = **efficacy** + **efficiency**
- Framework: **Objective → Data → Metrics → Baseline model → Review process**
- Start with **why** and **which decision**; that sets granularity and horizon
- Use a **model baseline**; let humans enrich where they add value, measured with **FVA**
- Short, mid, and long-term forecasts need different granularity, models, and reviewers
:::
