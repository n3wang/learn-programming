# 11. Measuring Forecasting Accuracy on a Product Portfolio

> **Teaching rewrite** — the *Metrics* step, scaled from one item to thousands. Original wording; numbers recomputed.

## Introduction

Lessons 8–10 judged **one** time series. Planners manage hundreds or thousands. Adding up errors across products mixes **apples and pears**: a 500-unit miss on nails and a 2-unit miss on anvils are not equally important. Many companies retreat to reporting accuracy **per category** to reduce the mix — but there is a better way: **value-weighted KPIs**.

This lesson covers:

- Why unweighted totals over-emphasise **high-volume** items
- Why **averaging percentages** across products fails
- **Value-weighted** bias, MAE, and RMSE
- Why **RMSE** doesn’t scale to portfolios
- Why you shouldn’t **penalise over- and under-forecasts differently**

---

## 11.1 Unweighted metrics on a portfolio

| Product | Demand | Forecast | Error | Abs. error | Squared error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anvil | 12 | 10 | −2 | 2 | 4 |
| Hammer | 100 | 150 | +50 | 50 | 2,500 |
| Nail | 1,500 | 1,000 | −500 | 500 | 250,000 |
| **Total** | **1,612** | **1,160** | **−452** | **552** | |

- Bias% = −452 / 1,612 = **−28.0%**
- MAE% = 552 / 1,612 = **34.2%**

Both are driven almost entirely by **nails** — the cheapest item — because they sell in the largest volume.

### Averaging percentages doesn’t help

| Product | Error % | Absolute % error |
|---|---|---|
| Anvil | −16.7% | 16.7% |
| Hammer | +50.0% | 50.0% |
| Nail | −33.3% | 33.3% |
| **Average** | **0.0%** | **33.3%** |

The average error % says **zero bias** — clearly false (we under-forecast by 452 units overall). Averaging percentages also treats every product as equally important and breaks on zero demand. Averaging percentages across products and periods is (almost) always a bad idea.

---

## 11.2 Value-weighted KPIs

Give each product a **weight** *w* reflecting its importance — e.g. **unit cost** (to manage invested capital), **margin** (to manage profit), **price**, or a strategic weight.

> Weighted error: e<sub>w</sub> = w × (f − d)  Weighted demand: d<sub>w</sub> = w × d

| KPI | Absolute | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Bias | Σe<sub>w</sub> / n | Σe<sub>w</sub> / Σd<sub>w</sub> |
| MAE | Σ\|e<sub>w</sub>\| / n | Σ\|e<sub>w</sub>\| / Σd<sub>w</sub> |
| RMSE | √(Σe<sub>w</sub>² / n) | RMSE / average d<sub>w</sub> |

Errors become **money**: a 5-unit error on a $2 item = $10.

### Worked example: weighting by unit cost

| Product | Demand | Forecast | Unit cost | Weighted demand | Weighted error | Weighted abs. error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anvil | 12 | 10 | €50 | €600 | −€100 | €100 |
| Hammer | 100 | 150 | €20 | €2,000 | **+€1,000** | **€1,000** |
| Nail | 1,500 | 1,000 | €0.10 | €150 | −€50 | €50 |
| **Total** | | | | **€2,750** | **+€850** | **€1,150** |

- Weighted Bias% = 850 / 2,750 = **+30.9%** (over-invested, mainly in hammers)
- Weighted MAE% = 1,150 / 2,750 = **41.8%**

The story flips: the **hammer** is now the main offender, not the nail. Even the **sign** of the bias changed — in units we were short; in money we were over-stocked.

## 11.3 RMSE doesn’t scale

Squaring errors across very different items lets a few large items dominate. In the example:

- Unweighted RMSE ≈ √((4 + 2,500 + 250,000) / 3) ≈ **290** units — almost all from nails.
- Weighted RMSE ≈ √((10,000 + 1,000,000 + 2,500) / 3) ≈ **€581** — almost all from hammers.

RMSE can be useful for **one** item; for portfolios, use **weighted MAE + weighted bias**.

---

## 11.4 Don’t penalise over- and under-forecasts differently

It is tempting to weight positive and negative errors differently because the costs differ: one unit too many costs holding or spoilage; one unit too few costs a sale and goodwill. But a metric that punishes under-forecasting more will **produce biased forecasts**. Other teams will notice, lose trust, and start making their own forecasts — or second-guessing yours (“planners inflate, so I’ll cut”).

Handle cost asymmetry where it belongs: in **service-level targets** and **safety stocks** set by inventory management — not in the demand forecast.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Unweighted totals are dominated by **high-volume** items; averaged percentages hide bias and ignore importance
- Use **value-weighted MAE and bias** (weights: cost, margin, price, or strategic)
- Weighting can change both **who** the offender is and the **sign** of the bias
- **RMSE** doesn’t scale to portfolios
- Don’t encode cost asymmetry in the forecast metric — use **service levels and safety stock**
:::
