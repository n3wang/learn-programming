# 12. Forecast Value Added (FVA)

> **Teaching rewrite** — Part 3, a data-driven forecasting process (the *Review process* step). Original wording; illustrative numbers.

## Introduction

A typical process: software produces a **baseline** → **demand planners** adjust → **sales** adjust → a **consensus meeting** agrees the final number. The final forecast has **45% MAE** and **+4% bias**. Is that good? Is the software set up well? Is sales over-forecasting to avoid shortages? Is the consensus meeting about forecasting — or about matching the budget?

The **Forecast Value Added (FVA)** framework answers these questions by measuring the accuracy of **every step** and comparing it with the step before. It needs little investment, often delivers the best return of any planning improvement, and should be the backbone of your improvement journey.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  B[Benchmark] -.compare.-> M[Baseline model]
  M --> P[Demand planners] --> S[Sales team] --> C[Consensus meeting]
```

This lesson covers:

- **Internal** vs **industry** benchmarks
- Measuring **efficacy** (does each step improve accuracy?) and **efficiency** (is it worth the time?)
- Best practices from research on judgmental adjustments
- Setting **objectives** without inviting metric hacking
- How to **get started**

Track FVA for **every relevant lag** (lesson 6) and with **value-weighted** metrics (lesson 11).

---

## 12.1 Compare the process with a benchmark

### Internal benchmarks — do this

Run a simple method (best moving average or seasonal moving average, lesson 10) through history and compare it with your process.

| Step | MAE | Bias |
|---|---|---|
| Benchmark | 52% | −1% |
| **Overall process** | **45%** | **+4%** |

The process beats the benchmark on accuracy — good news. How much investment (software and people) it takes to gain those 7 points is the efficiency question.

### Industry benchmarks — don’t

| Problem | Why |
|---|---|
| Unknown method | Often survey-based: you don’t know how others compute accuracy |
| Different levels | Country vs region, month vs week, different lags |
| Different strategies | A 20-SKU low-cost competitor will naturally beat a 500-SKU catalogue |
| Different footprints | Different markets and channels |
| Single lag | You need value across the whole horizon |

Expensive, unreliable, apples vs pears. Use **internal** benchmarks.

---

## 12.2 Process efficacy: does each step add value?

FVA = accuracy improvement versus the **previous** step. For MAE, FVA = previous MAE − current MAE. For bias, FVA = previous |bias| − current |bias|.

| Step | MAE | FVA (MAE) | Bias | FVA (bias) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Benchmark | 52% | | −1% | |
| Baseline model | 45% | **+7** | −3% | −2 |
| Demand planners | 43% | **+2** | +1% | +2 |
| Sales team | 45% | **−2** | +5% | **−4** |
| Consensus | 45% | 0 | +4% | +1 |

Reading it:

- The **model** adds most of the accuracy.
- **Planners** improve accuracy and remove the model’s negative bias (they handle new products and key-customer information).
- **Sales** makes it **worse** and pushes bias to +5% (“we want to secure stock”).
- **Consensus** adds nothing to accuracy (“we want to match the yearly budget”).

FVA creates **ownership and accountability**: each team’s contribution is visible instead of diluted in the final number. You can even track individuals and **customer-supplied forecasts** (often trusted, not always helpful).

---

## 12.3 Process efficiency: is it worth the time?

Add person-hours per cycle:

| Step | Person-hours | MAE | FVA (MAE) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline model | — | 45% | +7 |
| Demand planners | 72 | 43% | +2 |
| Sales team | 20 | 45% | −2 |
| Consensus | 8 | 45% | 0 |

Marginal gains shrink at each step: fixing the model’s big blind spots is easy; improving an already-reviewed forecast is hard. Past a point, extra hours aren’t worth it — FVA shows where to stop.

## 12.4 Best practices

- **Don’t overreact** to a few negative FVA rounds by deleting process steps. Find root causes — bias, ownership, incentives — and fix them. Removing a step that should add value is the last resort.
- Report FVA **by product group, channel, region** where teams and information differ.
- Track it **over the whole horizon** and **value-weighted**.

Research on judgmental adjustments in companies (Fildes & Goodwin, 2007) suggests:

| Finding | Action |
|---|---|
| Many **small** adjustments add almost nothing and cost time | Don’t tweak within the error margin |
| **Large** adjustments more often help (they need justification and carry personal risk) | Focus effort on big, well-founded changes |
| **Positive** (upward) adjustments often reduce accuracy; negative ones more often help | Track FVA **separately** for upward and downward edits |

**Present results tactfully.** FVA can embarrass non-value-adders; share early results privately. The aim is to improve the process, not humiliate anyone. (For a consultant, a process that loses to a benchmark is easy to improve: switch to the benchmark.)

### Setting objectives

| Do | Don’t |
|---|---|
| Base targets on what’s achievable (benchmarks) | Set arbitrary targets |
| Remember external shocks (e.g. a pandemic year) | Blame or reward teams for things they don’t control |
| Use metrics to **discuss and improve** | Use them to **reward and punish** — that invites metric hacking (choosing a convenient lag, excluding zero-demand periods, dropping “top offenders”) |

## 12.5 Getting started

FVA needs data: one forecast **version per step per lag**. With 5 steps (benchmark, model, planners, sales, consensus) and 6 lags, that is **30** versions per cycle — you need automation, not spreadsheets.

Two ways to start:

1. **Quick check**: compare the consensus forecast with a moving-average benchmark. Promotion-heavy businesses can beat benchmarks by ~20%; if you beat it by **less than ~5%**, there is probably room for improvement.
2. **Full FVA**: use software that stores versions, or build a data pipeline (e.g. in Python).

### Worked example: is the planners’ time worth it?

Planners spend **72 hours** per month and improve weighted MAE by **2 points** on a portfolio with €10 M monthly value. If each MAE point is worth roughly **€15,000** a month in lower safety stock and fewer lost sales, the gain is about **€30,000/month** for 72 hours ≈ **€417/hour** — clearly worthwhile. Sales’ 20 hours with **−2** points destroys about €30,000 a month.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Compare your process with an **internal benchmark**, never with industry surveys
- **FVA** = accuracy and bias change at each step → ownership and accountability
- Add **time spent** to judge efficiency; marginal gains shrink
- Avoid tiny tweaks; track **upward vs downward** edits; present results tactfully
- Use metrics to **discuss and improve**, not to reward and punish
- Start with a benchmark check; scale with automated version storage
:::
