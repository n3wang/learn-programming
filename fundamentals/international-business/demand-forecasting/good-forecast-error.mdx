# 10. What Is a Good Forecast Error?

> **Teaching rewrite** — the *Metrics* step: judging accuracy against a fair **benchmark**. Original wording; examples recomputed.

## Introduction

Your tool reports an MAE of **36%** and a bias of **+1.6%**. Good or bad? Common answers don’t hold up:

| Common answer | Problem |
|---|---|
| “Better than last year” | What if last year was exceptional (e.g. a pandemic year)? |
| “Better than our target” | Chicken-and-egg: how was the target set? |
| “Better than some other forecast” | Which one? |
| “It looks right — follows trend and seasonality” | Not standardised; doesn’t scale to thousands of items |
| “Industry benchmark” | Not comparable (lesson 12) |
| “Demand COV says it’s hard to forecast” | Misleading — see 10.2 |

Achievable accuracy depends on the data: national monthly phone sales are far easier than one model at one store on one day; collaborative manufacturers can be very accurate, fashion struggles. You can’t know a “good” error in advance — you must **compare against a simple benchmark on the same data**.

This lesson covers:

- **Naïve**, **moving-average**, and **seasonal** benchmarks
- Fair benchmarking rules (same lag, out-of-sample, ongoing)
- Why the **coefficient of variation (COV)** is a poor measure of forecastability

---

## 10.1 Benchmarks

A **benchmark** is a simple, free forecasting method. If your model or process can’t beat it, it isn’t adding value — and if the benchmark wins, you have just found your new model.

### Naïve forecast

Always predicts the **last observed value**. Easy to beat — so beating it proves little. Be wary of vendors who celebrate beating the naïve forecast.

### Moving average

Average of the last *n* periods; **3–6 periods** usually works best. A much fairer contender.

### Worked example: choosing a benchmark

Demand history: 100, 120, 90, 110, 130, 95, | 105, 125, 100, 115, 120, 90 (forecast the last 6 periods, one period ahead).

| Benchmark | MAE% | Bias% |
|---|---|---|
| Naïve (MA1) | 16.0% | +0.8% |
| MA3 | 9.7% | +1.5% |
| MA6 | **9.3%** | **+0.4%** |

Use the best one (MA6 here) — otherwise you compare yourself with an artificially weak benchmark.

### Seasonal benchmarks

For seasonal demand, use the **same period in previous cycles**:

- **Seasonal naïve** — last cycle’s same period (e.g. last Monday for next Monday).
- **Seasonal moving average** — average of several cycles (e.g. the last three Mondays).

For a product with a strong weekday pattern, a seasonal moving average can cut MAE roughly in half compared to a plain moving average that mixes Mondays with Saturdays.

### Rules for fair benchmarking

1. **Try several** moving averages and pick the best.
2. **Same lag** — a forecast made three months ahead must be compared with a benchmark that only had data up to three months before.
3. **Out-of-sample** — don’t compare on the period used to choose or tune your model. A model picked as the best of 15 on 2025 data will naturally beat the benchmark on 2025; the real test is 2026. Otherwise you are measuring **overfitting**.
4. **Keep tracking** — benchmarking is continuous, not a one-off (lesson 12).

## 10.2 Why COV is not a forecastability measure

**Coefficient of variation**: COV = σ / μ (standard deviation ÷ mean).

Many tools use COV to label items “easy” or “hard” to forecast. Problems:

| Problem | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Data leakage** | COV compares each period with the **same period’s** overall mean — information a forecaster never has. Comparing 2025 demand with the 2025 average looks better than any genuine forecast made from 2024 data |
| **Trend inflates COV** | A steadily growing product has high variation around its mean, yet a moving average follows it easily |
| **Seasonality inflates COV** | A highly seasonal product has high COV, yet a seasonal benchmark forecasts it well |

Only for **flat** demand (no trend, no seasonality) does COV roughly track forecastability — and even then it flatters itself through leakage. Deseasonalising or detrending first doesn’t fix it: that also uses hindsight and isn’t a simple benchmark.

### Worked example: trend fools COV

Demand grows 10 units per period: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60.

- Mean = 35; standard deviation ≈ 17.1 → COV ≈ **49%** (“hard to forecast”).
- A simple trend-following forecast (last value + last change) predicts each period exactly from period 3 on → error **0**.

High COV, perfectly forecastable.

> **Better**: judge forecastability by the **error of a good benchmark** on the item.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- You can’t know a good error in advance — **benchmark** it
- **Naïve** is too weak; use the best of several **moving averages** (3–6) or **seasonal** benchmarks
- Compare at the **same lag**, **out-of-sample**, and **continuously**
- **COV** leaks future information and is inflated by trend and seasonality — don’t use it as forecastability
- Judge forecastability by **benchmark error**
:::
