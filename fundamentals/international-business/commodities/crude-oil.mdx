# 6. Crude Oil

> **Teaching rewrite** of crude oil: quality, the supply chain, refining economics, supply and demand, price drivers, benchmarks, the Brent and WTI complexes, and hedging for producers, refiners, and consumers. Original wording; worked numbers recomputed. Prices are teaching values. Not investment advice.

## Introduction

Crude oil is still the world’s largest energy source — about **33%** of primary energy (2019), ahead of coal (27%) and gas (24%); renewables ≈ 5%. But “crude oil” isn’t one thing: price reporters track **100+** grades, from pale straw-coloured liquids to tar. Its value lies in **what a refinery can make from it** — so demand for crude is really **refinery demand**.

This lesson covers:

- Crude **quality**: API gravity, sulfur, acidity, flow
- The **supply chain**, tankers, and **refining** — yields, gross product worth, **crack spreads**
- **Reserves, production, consumption, trade**, security of supply
- **Price drivers**: macro, supply chain, geopolitics, OPEC
- **Benchmarks** (WTI, Brent, Dubai/Oman), pricing methods, cargo pricing
- The **Brent complex** (Dated Brent, forwards, CFDs, futures, EFPs) and **US crude** (WTI, NYMEX roll, **negative prices** in 2020)
- **Hedging**: producers (futures, basis, swaps, two-asset barriers), refiners (crack spreads, margin swaps, calendar spread options), consumers (jet fuel swaps, jet diff, regression hedges, compound options)

## 6.1 What makes crude valuable

| Property | Measure | Better for refiners |
|---|---|---|
| **Density** | **API gravity** (water = 10; higher = lighter) | **Light** (high API) → more gasoline, kerosene |
| **Sulfur** | % by weight; **sweet** < 0.5%, **sour** > 0.5% | **Sweet** — less processing |
| **Acidity** | Total Acid Number; high > 0.7 | Low — acidic crudes corrode equipment |
| **Flow** | **Viscosity**; **pour point** (lowest flowing temperature) | Low — easier to pump, store, ship |
| **Type** | Paraffinic (low viscosity, flammable → lubricants); naphthenic (viscous → bitumen); intermediate | Depends on target products |

**Light, sweet, low-TAN** crudes (WTI, Brent; API ~33–45) trade at **premiums** to heavy, sour, acidic grades (e.g. Urals). The most common quality is **medium sour** (~41% of output); light sweet ~19%.

**Conventional** vs **unconventional** oil: shale oil can be light and sweet; Canadian **oil sands** bitumen (API 7–13, 4–6% sulfur) must be upgraded — 2 t of sand ≈ 1.25 bbl bitumen ≈ 1 bbl crude, with heavy energy and water use.

## 6.3 Refining

### Distillation cuts

| Temperature (°C) | Product |
|---|---|
| < −45 | Gas |
| −45 to 35 | LPG |
| 35–150 | Gasoline, naphtha |
| 150–230 | Kerosene (jet fuel) |
| 230–340 | Gas oil (diesel, heating oil) |
| 340–400 | Heavy gas oil |
| > 400 | Residue / fuel oil, coke, asphalt |

Then **conversion** (cracking: heat + pressure + catalyst turn heavy molecules into lighter, more valuable ones), **treating** (remove contaminants), and **blending** to legal specs.

**Refinery gain**: a 42-gallon barrel yields ~**45 gallons** of products because lighter products occupy more volume — but never more **weight**. US yields (2017): ~20 gal gasoline, 11 gal diesel, 4 gal jet per barrel.

### Optimisation: gross product worth

A refinery chooses crudes and configuration to maximise **gross product worth (GPW)** − crude cost.

**Worked example**: a European refinery can run **Forties** (USD 64.15) or **Oseberg** (USD 63.45). Product prices (USD/t) and conversion factors (bbl per t):

| Product | Price (USD/t) | bbl/t | Forties yield | Oseberg yield |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gasoline | 558 | 8.35 | 35% | 25% |
| Jet | 582 | 7.88 | 10% | 15% |
| Gas oil | 615 | 7.46 | 20% | 15% |
| Fuel oil | 350 | 6.35 | 35% | 45% |

Revenue per barrel = price ÷ factor × yield (e.g. gasoline from Forties = 558 / 8.35 × 35% = **23.39**).

| | Forties | Oseberg |
|---|---|---|
| Gasoline | 23.39 | 16.71 |
| Jet | 7.39 | 11.08 |
| Gas oil | 16.49 | 12.37 |
| Fuel oil | 19.29 | 24.80 |
| **GPW** | **66.55** | **64.95** |
| − Crude cost | 64.15 | 63.45 |
| **Margin** | **2.40** | **1.50** |

Choose **Forties** — dearer crude, better margin. In theory a grade’s **differential** to a benchmark equals the difference in product-slate value: the discount at which the refiner is **indifferent**.

### Crack spreads

Price reporters publish indicator margins (US Gulf Coast sour cracking, Singapore hydrocracking, NW Europe light sweet cracking).

**3-2-1 crack**: 3 bbl crude → 2 bbl gasoline + 1 bbl heating oil (distillate). Crude **USD 50**, gasoline **USD 1.40/gal**, heating oil **USD 1.20/gal**:

Crack = ⅔ × 1.40 × 42 + ⅓ × 1.20 × 42 − 50 = 39.20 + 16.80 − 50 = **USD 6.00/bbl**

:::caution Note on the source
The source rounds the weights to 0.67/0.33 (giving 6.03) and calls the third leg “fuel oil” while pricing heating oil. The 3-2-1 crack uses **gasoline and heating oil (distillate)** with exact ⅔ and ⅓ weights.
:::

**Drivers of refinery profit**: *internal* — product slate value, crude choice, configuration, reliability, location, specialty products, costs, own stocks; *external* — product demand, market inventories, seasonality, crude prices, trade, substitution, specs, technology, gasoline vs diesel demand, weather.

## 6.4 Demand and supply

- **Proved reserves** rose from ~684 bn bbl (1980) to ~1,734 bn (2019) — higher prices and technology turn resources into reserves. Middle East ≈ ½; South & Central America (largely Venezuela) ≈ 19%.
- **R/P ratio** (reserves ÷ annual production) ≈ **50 years** in 2019 (≈ 41 in 2004) — a snapshot, not a countdown.
- **Production** ≈ 95 m bbl/day (2019); Middle East ≈ 32%, North America ≈ 26% after the **shale** boom (US output fell from 1970 to 2008, then surged past 13 m bbl/day).
- **Consumption**: Asia Pacific ≈ 37%; China ≈ 14% of world demand.
- **Mismatch** between reserves, production, and consumption drives trade (Middle East and West Africa → Europe, US, China).
- **Security of supply** (importers diversify sources) and **security of demand** (exporters diversify buyers).
- **Brent’s own field** output fell from ~2.9 m bbl/day (1999) to ~1.1 m (2019) — hence a widened basket.

## 6.5 Price drivers

| Group | Drivers |
|---|---|
| **Macro** | GDP growth; reserves and recovery technology (typical recovery ~30%); **strategic reserves** (US SPR, capacity ~700 m bbl); substitution (gas, biofuels, EVs, hydrogen); differentials to markers; refined product demand (heating oil in winter, gasoline in the US driving season); **taxation** (European diesel incentives); transport trends; **investor flows** (index rolls, active funds); **USD**; weather and hurricanes |
| **Supply chain** | Spare upstream capacity (Saudi spare capacity is mostly heavier, sour crude); production costs (from ~USD 9/bbl in Saudi Arabia to ~USD 44 in the UK, 2016); rig counts and maintenance; **refining capacity** (US refineries fell from 325 in 1981 to ~135 by 2017; none built 1998–2014); environmental rules; crack spreads; **storage and inventories**; rigs, tankers, skilled labour; distribution bottlenecks; disasters (Hurricanes Katrina and Rita, 2005); field quality changes |
| **Geopolitics** | War, terrorism, sanctions (Iraq, Iran); internal unrest; security-of-supply tensions; **resource nationalism**; NOCs limiting IOC access; **OPEC** |

### OPEC

OPEC coordinates members’ output quotas to stabilise prices and income. Its influence is greatest when the market is tight; shale has reduced it. Since 2016 the wider **OPEC+** alliance (including Russia) coordinates output too.

:::info Membership update
The source lists 14 members. Ecuador left in 2020 and Angola in 2024, so OPEC now has about **12** members (Algeria, Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Libya, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Venezuela).
:::

### Forward curve segments

- **0–18 months**: physical — supply/demand, inventories, storage, security of supply.
- **Beyond 18 months**: financial — rates, inflation, capex expectations.
- Oil curves are more volatile than financial ones, can be **seasonal**, and **mean-revert** long term.

## 6.6 Prices and benchmarks

Quoted in **USD per barrel** (42 US gal ≈ 159 L). Inflation-adjusted peaks: 1864 and ~1980 (> USD 100 in today’s money).

**Delivered cost** = FOB price + shipping + secondary transport + losses (evaporation) + insurance + financing in transit. **FOB** compares crudes worldwide; buyers care about **CIF**.

### Marker crudes

| Marker | Origin | API / sulfur |
|---|---|---|
| **WTI** | US (Cushing, Oklahoma) | 39.6 / 0.24% |
| **Brent** (basket) | North Sea | 38.8 / 0.37% |
| **Dubai / Oman** | Middle East (Asia benchmark) | medium sour |
| **Urals** | Russia (Mediterranean) | 31.7 / 1.35% |

Also: Light Louisiana Sweet, Mars (US sour), and the **Argus Sour Crude Index (ASCI)** (Mars, Poseidon, Southern Green Canyon) used by Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Iraq for US sales. **WTI** is “landlocked” at Cushing — local storage and refinery outages can distort it.

Saudi crude isn’t a global marker: after the 1980s **netback pricing** episode it prices by destination (WTI/ASCI for the US, Brent for Europe), bans resale, and sells on evergreen contracts.

### Pricing methods

Official selling prices · **fixed** price (most long-term volume) · **floating** (PRA averages around delivery) · **differential to a marker** · **futures**-based · **exchange for physicals (EFP)**.

### Worked example: pricing a cargo

“**Platts Dated Brent + USD 1.00/bbl, FOB, pricing 2-1-2**” = average of Dated Brent on the 2 days before, the day of, and the 2 days after the **bill of lading** date, plus USD 1.00. For a **600,000 bbl** cargo the buyer’s price exposure builds by 600,000 / 5 = **120,000 bbl per pricing day**.

## 6.7 The Brent complex

North Sea grades: **Brent, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk, Troll** (**BFOET**). Benchmarks are built from them; since 2023, US **WTI Midland** delivered to Europe has also been included in Dated Brent.

| | **Dated Brent** | **Brent forward** (“paper”, cash BFOET) | **ICE Brent futures** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | OTC physical | OTC | Exchange |
| What | Cargoes with a known 3-day loading window, **10 days to ~1 month** ahead | Cargo in a **future month**, loading dates nominated later | Standard 1,000 bbl contracts |
| Size | 600,000 bbl | 600,000 bbl (partials smaller) | 1,000 bbl |
| Settlement | Physical | Physical (or “booked out”) | EFP or cash vs **ICE Brent Index** |
| Expiry | — | — | Last business day of the **second** month before delivery |

- **Dated Brent** is a PRA **assessment** (lowest-priced grade, quality-adjusted to remove “cheapest to deliver” effects), rolling daily over its 10-day-to-1-month window.
- **Forwards** become “wet” when the seller **nominates** a 3-day **laycan** with at least **one month’s** notice (SUKO 90 terms).

### Contracts for difference (CFDs)

**CFD = Dated Brent (for a future week) − third-month Brent forward**. Weekly out to 8 weeks; 50,000–100,000 bbl.

> **Implied forward Dated Brent = CFD + third-month forward**

| Quote side | Market user | Profits if the differential… |
|---|---|---|
| Offer | **Buys**: pays fixed, receives actual | Rises (more positive / less negative) |
| Bid | **Sells**: receives fixed, pays actual | Falls |

**Speculation**: buy a CFD at **+1.55**; actual averages **+1.75** → +0.20 × 50,000 = **USD 10,000**. With negative quotes the logic holds: buy at −2.05, settle at −1.85 → **+0.20/bbl**.

**Hedge example** (early March): buy a cargo loading 21–23 March, priced on average Dated Brent. Buy the 20–24 March CFD at **−1.39** and go long **June forward at 68.72** → implied cost **67.33**.

| Day | Dated | June fwd | Diff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon 20 | 69.48 | 70.79 | −1.31 |
| Tue 21 | 69.49 | 70.78 | −1.29 |
| Wed 22 | 69.50 | 70.80 | −1.30 |
| Thu 23 | 69.51 | 70.82 | −1.31 |
| Fri 24 | 69.52 | 70.81 | −1.29 |

- Cargo price = average Dated 21–23 = **69.50**
- CFD: average diff −1.30 vs −1.39 → **+0.09**
- June forward closed at 70.81 → **+2.09**
- Net = 69.50 − 0.09 − 2.09 = **67.32** ≈ the locked 67.33 (rounding from averaging vs single close)

### Exchange for physicals (EFP)

Swap a **physical** position for a **futures** position, quoted as a differential (time, quality, location).

**Example**: a refiner long 1,000 futures at **63.00** buys 1 m bbl from a producer via EFP at front-month **62.00 + 0.10 = 62.10**, handing its futures to the producer:

- Refiner: physical 62.10 + futures loss 1.00 = **63.10**
- Producer: sells at 62.10 and holds long futures to close when it chooses

## 6.8 US crude markets

- **WTI** delivered at Cushing; pipeline nominations by the **25th** of the prior month (so 26 March–25 April prices reflect **May** delivery). Other US grades: WTS (Midland), LLS (St James), Mars (Clovelly), ANS (Long Beach).
- **CME WTI futures**: 1,000 bbl, physical at Cushing, expire **3 business days before the 25th** of the prior month; also RBOB gasoline and ULSD (42,000 gal, NY Harbor). **RBOB** is a blendstock — like base metals’ intermediate-stage contracts.

### Pricing physical WTI: CMA + roll

**Price = calendar-month average (CMA) of front-month futures in the delivery month + NYMEX roll**

Roll, computed daily over the prior trading month and averaged:

> ((M1 − M2) × ⅔) + ((M1 − M3) × ⅓)

**Example day**: May 40.28, June 40.58, July 40.87 → (−0.30 × ⅔) + (−0.59 × ⅓) = −0.20 − 0.20 = **−0.40**.

:::caution Correction
The source’s formula uses (M1 − M3) for the second term but its arithmetic uses (M2 − M3), giving −0.30. Applying the stated formula gives **−0.40**.
:::

If the May CMA is **41.00** and the averaged roll **−0.50**, the producer receives **40.50/bbl**.

### Negative WTI: 20 April 2020

COVID-19 crushed demand; Cushing was ~79% full (≈ 60 m of ~76 m bbl working capacity) with much remaining space already booked. Holders of the expiring **May** contract (≈ 13,000 lots open) faced taking delivery with nowhere to put the oil. The contract opened at **USD 17.73**, hit **−40.32**, and settled **−37.63** — a **USD 58.05** range — then expired next day at **USD 10.01**. Brent stayed positive. Index **rolls** added selling pressure. (Lesson 2: why Bachelier-type models were adopted.)

## 6.9 Hedging along the supply chain

| Participant | Exposures |
|---|---|
| **Producer** | Flat price (falling), quality differentials |
| **Refiner** | Crude cost, financing, freight, insurance, storage, **crack spread** |
| **Consumer** | Product price, location differential, lack of a liquid hedge → **proxy** hedges |

### Producers

**Basis risk** arises from: hedging one product with another, timing mismatches, early termination, different delivery locations, quality differences.

#### Futures strip with basis

A producer sells 50,000 bbl a month (Jan–Mar), each priced at Platts WTI on the first business day; it **sells** Feb/Mar/Apr futures on 1 December and buys them back on each pricing day.

| Delivery | Cash price | Futures sold → bought | Futures P&L | Effective price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 67.93 | 69.67 → 69.32 | +0.35 | **68.28** |
| Feb | 67.55 | 70.04 → 69.29 | +0.75 | **68.30** |
| Mar | 68.79 | 70.63 → 71.15 | −0.52 | **68.27** |

Average effective **68.28** vs unhedged average **68.09**. **Basis** = cash − futures; effective price = **original futures price + basis at close-out** (e.g. 69.67 − 1.39 = 68.28). The producer swaps volatile **price** risk for smaller **basis** risk.

#### Multi-day pricing

Selling 300,000 bbl priced on the average front-month close over 3 loading days (62.00, 63.50, 61.25 → avg **62.25**): short 300 futures at **59.37**, buy back 100 each day → futures loss 263,000 + 413,000 + 188,000 = **864,000**; physical **18,675,000** → net **17,811,000** = **59.37/bbl** exactly. (Perfect only because the physical references the same future and closes at the same prices.)

#### Swaps

A producer with a **fixed** sales contract who wants **floating** exposure **pays fixed USD 50, receives** the monthly average futures price. Average **49.50** on 70,000 bbl → pays **USD 35,000** — net, it sells at floating.

Common swaps: Brent and WTI futures averages; Dated Brent, Dubai, Tapis (PRA averages); gasoline Eurobob barges, RBOB, ICE gas oil, ULSD, gas oil and fuel oil barges/cargoes. Variants: fixed vs futures, **index vs index** (e.g. Urals vs Dated Brent), index vs futures, **crude vs products**.

#### Two-asset barrier: cheaper FX hedge

A non-US producer receiving USD buys a 1-month **EUR call / USD put** on **EUR 50 m**, strike **1.1524**: premium **USD 0.0106 per EUR** = **USD 530,000** (= EUR 460,870 at spot 1.15, ≈ 0.92%).

Make it **knock out if Brent trades above a barrier** (when the producer is cash-rich anyway) → cheaper. Premium depends on EUR–oil correlation: **−1 → 0.0106** (never knocks out), **0 → 0.0101**, **+1 → 0.0080**. Closer barriers and continuous monitoring cut the premium; one-month range ≈ 20% / √12 ≈ **5.8%** of the oil price.

### Refiners

**FX-reset swap**: a EUR refiner pays fixed **USD 60** (vs crude at 70) for two years — but if EUR/USD ever trades above **1.25**, the fixed price **resets to USD 85**. The subsidy is paid for with an embedded **digital, American-barrier, cross-asset** option.

**3-2-1 futures crack**: to protect margin, **sell the crack** = buy 3 crude futures, sell 2 gasoline + 1 heating oil (often later-dated products). Basis risks: **location** (Cushing/NY Harbor vs Gulf Coast) and **quality**.

#### Refinery margin swap

Margin = 0.03 × naphtha + 0.41 × gasoline + 0.35 × gas oil + 0.21 × fuel oil − Dated Brent (products converted to USD/bbl). Refiner **receives fixed 7.24** on 160,000 bbl/month for Q4:

| Month | Actual margin | Physical | Swap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oct | 6.82 | 1,091,200 | +67,200 |
| Nov | 6.03 | 964,800 | +193,600 |
| Dec | 8.11 | 1,297,600 | −139,200 |

Total 3,475,200 / 480,000 bbl = **7.24/bbl**.

**Options**: buy a put (or sell a call) on the margin; a **refining margin option** is a basket spread option tailored to the refinery’s slate.

#### Calendar spread options and storage

Calendar spread = **near − far** (negative in contango). Owning storage ≈ a put on the spread: in contango you store and sell later.

**Example**: gas oil Jan **903**, Jun **889** (backwardated, spread +14). A storage owner **sells a put** on Jan − Jun, strike **−10**, premium **0.50**; storage costs **7**.

| Scenario | Jan | Jun | Spread | Option payout | Store & sell? | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 860 | 890 | −30 | −20 | Yes (+30 − 7) | **3.50** |
| 2 | 860 | 868 | −8 | 0 | Yes (+8 − 7) | **1.50** |
| 3 | 860 | 866 | −6 | 0 | No (6 < 7) | **0.50** |
| 4 | 860 | 850 | +10 | 0 | No | **0.50** |

### Consumers (airlines)

**Jet fuel swap**: airline pays fixed **USD 650/t**, receives the monthly average Platts Jet CIF NWE, 10,000 t/month → net fixed **650**.

**Jet diff**: jet = gas oil + jet differential. Gas oil USD 72/bbl × 7.45 = **536.40/t**; jet 586.40 → diff **50**.

**Gas oil futures + jet diff swap**: buy gas oil futures at **536.40** and a jet diff swap at **45** → locked **581.40**. At maturity gas oil **550**, diff **40** → jet **590**; futures **+13.60**, swap **−5** → 590 + 5 − 13.60 = **581.40**.

Typical airline layering: long-dated crude (3–4 years) → gas oil (~2 years) → jet swaps (~1 year) → location-specific hedges (~6 months).

#### Regression hedge ratio (bank hedging a jet swap with crude futures)

Δjet = α + β Δcrude + ε. With **β = 0.8**, hedge **1,000 t** of jet (× 7.45 = 7,450 bbl) with 1,000 bbl crude futures:

Contracts = 0.8 × 7,450 / 1,000 = 5.96 ≈ **6**. A USD 1 crude move → futures **6,000** vs jet exposure **5,960**.

Questions: which jet index, sample length and frequency, timing, maturity, and conversion factor? **Stack** (one maturity, rolled — liquid) vs **strip** (matching maturities — may be illiquid)? β drifts, so rebalance; the bank may widen the swap price for imperfect hedging.

#### Compound options for uncertain volume

For base consumption buy a strip of calls; for **uncertain** extra volume, buy a **call on a call**: pay **USD 10** now for the right to buy, in one month, a one-month call (strike 600) for **USD 21**. If vol rises so the call would cost **24**, exercise — but the total paid (31) exceeds 24: flexibility has a price.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Crude is valued by its **refined products**: API, sulfur, acidity, flow decide the slate
- Refiners maximise **gross product worth − crude cost**; **crack spreads** measure margins
- Reserves and R/P ratios move with **price and technology**; reserves, production, and consumption are geographically mismatched
- Drivers span **macro, supply chain, geopolitics, OPEC/OPEC+**; the short end is physical, the long end financial
- Benchmarks: **WTI** (Cushing), **Brent** (BFOET basket, plus WTI Midland), **Dubai/Oman**; most crudes price as **differentials**
- Brent complex: **Dated** (physical), **forwards** (paper), **CFDs** (Dated vs forward), **futures**, **EFPs**
- WTI physical = **CMA + roll**; April 2020 proved prices can go **negative**
- Hedges: producers (futures + **basis**, swaps), refiners (cracks, **margin swaps**, **calendar spread options**), consumers (**jet swaps**, **jet diff**, regression proxies, compound options)
:::
