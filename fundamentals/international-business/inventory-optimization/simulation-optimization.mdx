# 12. Simulation Optimization

Closed-form inventory models need strong assumptions: Normal or gamma demand, fixed or Normal lead times, pure backorders, non-perishable stock. Real chains often violate several of those at once—custom demand shapes from the previous lesson, stochastic lead times, time-dependent backlog penalties, partial lost sales, or product aging.

**Simulation optimization (sim-opt)** does not solve those cases with a single equation. It **simulates** many candidate policies and **keeps the one with the best estimated cost** (or another KPI). The price is compute time and noisy estimates; the gain is workable policies when math is intractable.

## When mathematical models stop being enough

Typical breaks in earlier assumptions:

- Excess demand is partly **lost sales**, or backlog that ages into cancellations—not a fixed per-unit backorder cost.
- Demand follows a **custom discrete PMF** (KDE), not Normal/gamma.
- Lead time is itself a **discrete PMF**.
- Goods **deteriorate** or perish within a short horizon.

If you cannot write a trustworthy closed form, simulate the policy and search.

## Case: safety stock with custom demand and lead time

Consider a periodic $(R,S)$ setting with:

- Demand PMF from a discrete KDE (previous lesson), with moments $\mu_d,\sigma_d$.
- Lead-time PMF, for example:

| Lead time $L$ | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Probability | 0.1 | 0.7 | 0.2 |

- Holding cost $h$ per unit per period, fixed order cost $k$, and a **backlog cost** $b_\tau$ charged **per unit short per period** (not a one-shot backorder fee).

Three building blocks:

1. Estimate demand and lead-time distributions (and their moments).
2. Write a simulator that maps a candidate safety stock $S_s$ (given $R$) to average cost and service KPIs.
3. Run a **search** over $S_s$ until a stopping rule fires.

From safety stock to order-up-to:

$$
S = S_s + \mu_d(\mu_L + R)
$$

Use **mean** lead time $\mu_L$ in that formula—not only the modal (“most common”) lead time—unless your model explicitly says otherwise.

## Simulation sketch

A practical discrete-time loop (same spirit as earlier policy labs):

1. Maintain on-hand (can go negative under backlog) and a **transit pipeline** sized to the **maximum** lead time.
2. Each period: receive due receipts, observe demand, update on-hand and stockout flags, advance the pipeline.
3. Every $R$ periods: compute inventory position (on-hand + pipeline), sample a random lead time from the lead-time PMF, and place an order that restores position to $S$.
4. Accrue holding on average physical stock, backlog cost $b_\tau\cdot\max(0,-\text{on-hand})$, and transaction cost $k$ when ordering.
5. Return average cost per period, cycle service, fill rate, and the $S_s$ you tested.

Long horizons (e.g. tens of thousands of periods) reduce noise but raise runtime.

## Search method 1: start at zero and climb

1. Simulate $S_s=\texttt{start}$ (often 0).
2. Step $S_s \leftarrow S_s + \Delta$.
3. If the new cost is better than the best so far, update the best.
4. Stop when the latest cost exceeds $\texttt{threshold}\times\text{best cost}$.

**Step size $\Delta$:** often $1\%$–$5\%$ of mean demand, or a practical batch size (e.g. 100).

**Threshold:** larger values explore farther past the valley but cost more iterations. Simulation noise can trip a tight threshold early—raise the threshold or lengthen the horizon when the chain is more random.

Starting at $S_s=0$ often wastes iterations in a high-cost region.

## Search method 2: heuristic start and double run

1. Pick a **heuristic** $S_s$ near the analytical optimum under Normal assumptions.
2. Search **upward** with $+\Delta$ and **downward** with $-\Delta$ from that seed (or from seed $-\Delta$ for the decrease pass).
3. Keep the best policy seen across both runs.

### Heuristic seed for $(R,S)$

If you temporarily pretend demand and lead time are Normal, a backlog-style service target is

$$
\alpha^* \approx 1-\frac{hR}{b}
$$

With **time-based** backlog cost $b_\tau$, inflate $b$ slightly in the heuristic (e.g. $1.1\,b_\tau$) so multi-period shortage penalty is not understated:

$$
\alpha^* \approx 1-\frac{hR}{1.1\,b_\tau}
$$

Protection-period standard deviation with uncertain lead time:

$$
\sigma_x=\sqrt{(\mu_L+R)\sigma_d^2+\sigma_L^2\mu_d^2}
$$

$$
S_s \approx z_{\alpha^*}\,\sigma_x,\qquad z_{\alpha^*}=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha^*)
$$

That seed is not “the” optimum under a custom PMF—it is a **warm start** for sim-opt.

### Further search ideas

- **Two-loop grid:** coarse $\Delta$ to locate the valley, then fine $\Delta$ around the winner.
- **Faster simulation code** (vectorization, fewer allocations).
- **Smooth noisy costs** (e.g. local average of neighboring $S_s$ evaluations) instead of only lengthening the horizon.
- **Variable step** from the local slope; or fit a curve / use a black-box optimizer after a few evaluations.

## Multi-parameter policies

### $(R,S)$: nest searches

1. Seed $R$ (e.g. power-of-two ideas from deterministic review design).
2. Optimize $S_s$ for that $R$ with the methods above.
3. Try shorter and longer $R$; optimize $S_s$ for each.
4. Keep expanding $R$ in the improving direction until neighbors no longer win.

### $(R,s,Q)$: harder analytically, natural for sim-opt

Every $R$ periods, if inventory position $\le s$, order $Q$ (or enough multiples of $Q$ to clear the gap).

If **review itself is free**, prefer small $R$ and let $Q$ carry the fixed-order-cost trade-off. If counting inventory is costly, search over $R$ as well.

**Heuristic starts (wisdom-of-crowd style averages):**

$$
\alpha \approx \tfrac12\left[\left(1-\frac{hR}{b}\right)+\left(1-\frac{hQ}{bD}\right)\right]
$$

(again inflate $b$ if you only observe $b_\tau$).

Risk period for the buffer: between $L$ and $L+R$, a practical compromise is $L+R/2$:

$$
S_s=z\sigma_d\sqrt{L+R/2},\qquad
s=\mu_d(L+R/2)+S_s
$$

(with lead-time variance folded in as in the $(R,S)$ protection formula when $L$ is random).

Order quantity: start from EOQ $\sqrt{2kD/h}$, optionally one stochastic refinement $\sqrt{2(k+bU_s)D/h}$ with a rough $U_s$ (e.g. a small fraction of $Q$).

Pack $(s,Q)$ into a vector and hand it to a black-box minimizer (e.g. SciPy **Nelder–Mead**) whose objective is the simulated average cost. Round $s$ and $Q$ inside the simulator. Cap iterations (`maxiter`) so the search stays affordable.

## Stability: sim-opt results move

Two runs rarely return identical policies. Noise comes from random demand, lead times, and finite horizons. Planners dislike jittery parameter updates (bullwhip risk and trust erosion).

Three mitigations:

1. **Fix a random seed** (`np.random.seed(...)`) for reproducible **comparisons**—not a cure for every parameter change.
2. **Lengthen the horizon** to cut variance (slower, but better chance of a policy that was not lucky on a short run).
3. **Update only on material improvement**—e.g. adopt a new policy only if estimated cost falls by at least 5% versus the current live policy under the same cost assumptions.

Seeds help demos; significant-change rules help production stability; longer sims help both reliability and fairness in scenario studies.

## Takeaways

- Use **sim-opt** when shortage regimes, custom PMFs, random lead times, or perishability break closed forms.
- Pipeline: **model distributions → simulate cost(policy) → search** (climb, double search, or black-box).
- Warm-start from **Part III heuristics**; search both directions with a **cost-ratio stop**.
- Nest searches for $R$ and $S_s$; pack $(s,Q)$ for multi-parameter optimizers.
- Stabilize with **seeds**, **longer horizons**, and **material improvement** before changing live policies.
