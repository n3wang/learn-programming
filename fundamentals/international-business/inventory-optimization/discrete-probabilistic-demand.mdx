# 11. Discrete Probabilistic Demand

Earlier lessons often assumed **Normal** demand, then introduced **gamma** as a continuous alternative. Those parametric families are convenient, but they can misrepresent a custom sales history.

This lesson builds a **tailor-fitted discrete** demand model from observations: start with histograms and their limits, improve with **kernel density estimation (KDE)**, then discretize the smooth density into a **probability mass function (PMF)** suitable for simulation and later policy optimization.

Original teaching rewrite for this course. Numeric drills use synthetic samples; they mirror the chapter workflow without reprinting a commercial dataset or textbook figures.

## Why leave parametric densities?

A Normal or gamma model forces a fixed shape. Histograms and KDEs instead estimate density **from the data**, then compress the result onto integer demand values. The next chapter in the track uses such a custom PMF inside simulation-based policy search.

## Histograms as a first density estimate

A histogram splits the observed range into **bins**. Each bin’s height is the fraction (or count) of observations that fall inside it. A common inventory shortcut treats that frequency as a point mass at the **bin center**.

### How a histogram is built

1. Choose left and right edges of the support.
2. Divide that interval into $k$ bins.
3. Count observations in each bin and convert counts to frequencies.
4. Optionally assign each frequency to the bin midpoint.

That yields a crude discrete distribution—but only at the midpoints.

### Four histogram limitations

1. **Sparse support.** Probability lives only on bin centers (e.g. 21.7% at 1375 and 0% at 1376).
2. **Arbitrary bin count.** Changing $k$ changes the shape of the estimated density.
3. **Arbitrary edges.** Shifting the left/right limits reshapes the same sample into a different “distribution.”
4. **Not smooth.** Adjacent bins can jump abruptly even when the true process is gradual.

Histograms remain useful for **exploration**. They are a weak default for a simulation PMF.

## Kernel density estimation

**Kernel density estimation** places a small probability kernel around **every observation**, then sums and normalizes those kernels. You no longer choose bin edges or a bin count; each point gets its own local contribution.

**Kernel:** a probability density used to spread mass around one observation.

**Bandwidth:** the width of that kernel (for a Gaussian kernel, its standard deviation).

### Box (squared) kernel

A box kernel spreads equal density on an interval of width equal to the bandwidth centered at each observation. Overlapping boxes add. The result is continuous in the support sense (every nearby real value gets mass) but **not smooth**—edges of the boxes create flat stretches and abrupt slopes.

Bandwidth still matters: a narrow box is jagged; a wide box oversmooths multimodal structure.

### Gaussian kernel

A **Normal** kernel around each point yields a smooth density. The estimated PDF at $x$ is the average of Normal densities centered at the observations (scaled so the whole curve integrates to 1).

Larger bandwidth → smoother, flatter estimate. Smaller bandwidth → more peaks that hug the sample.

### Choosing bandwidth: Scott and Silverman

With $n$ observations and sample standard deviation $\sigma_d$:

$$
h_{\text{Scott}}=\sigma_d\,n^{-1/5}
$$

$$
h_{\text{Silverman}}=\sigma_d\left(\frac{4}{3n}\right)^{1/5}
$$

Both are **rules of thumb**. Scott’s rule often **undersmooths less aggressively than intuition wants** in the other direction: in practice it can look **too smooth** (underfit). A common heuristic is to use about **$0.9\,h_{\text{Scott}}$** when the Scott curve looks too loose.

KDE support extends slightly **beyond** the sample min and max—which is desirable: never observing 1902 does not prove $\Pr(D=1902)=0$.

**Near-zero demand:** Gaussian kernels can leak probability onto **negative** demand. Fold that mass onto 0 when building a PMF (below).

### Practical SciPy sketch

`scipy.stats.gaussian_kde(data, bw_method=...)` accepts `'scott'`, `'silverman'`, or a scalar factor times $\sigma_d$. The object exposes `.pdf(x)`. SciPy does not ship a vectorized KDE `.cdf`; integrate one point at a time, e.g. `integrate_box_1d(-inf, x)`, or build your own loop.

## From continuous KDE to a discrete PMF

Simulation engines need a **finite integer domain** and a PMF that sums to 1. Continuous KDE lives on $(-\infty,\infty)$ in theory, so truncate thoughtfully.

### Bounds from the sample and bandwidth

With safety factor $m$ (often 3 or 4):

$$
L=\left\lfloor d_{\min}-m\,h\right\rfloor,\qquad
U=\left\lceil d_{\max}+m\,h\right\rceil
$$

For a single Normal kernel, $\Phi(-3)\approx 0.0013$, so mass below $d_{\min}-3h$ from the lowest observation’s kernel is tiny; across $n$ kernels the tail is still small if other kernels sit higher. Floor $L$ and ceil $U$ for a conservative integer grid.

Then take domain $x=L,L+1,\ldots,U-1$ (or through $U$, consistently), evaluate the KDE PDF on $x$, and **normalize**:

$$
\mathrm{pmf}_i=\frac{f(x_i)}{\sum_j f(x_j)}
$$

Sampling: `numpy.random.choice(x, size=..., p=pmf)`.

### Fold negative demand to zero

If any $x_i<0$ carry mass:

1. Add $\sum_{x_i<0}\mathrm{pmf}_i$ onto the probability at $0$.
2. Drop all negative support from $x$ and `pmf`.

### Mean and variance from a PMF

$$
\mu_d=\sum_i \mathrm{pmf}_i\,x_i
$$

$$
\mathrm{Var}_d=\sum_i x_i^2\,\mathrm{pmf}_i-\mu_d^2,\qquad
\sigma_d=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_d}
$$

### Independence reminder

A good **per-period** fit does not automatically describe **lead-time** demand. Autocorrelation (high after high, or high after low) breaks the independent-period assumption. When dependence matters, simulate demand over the **risk period** as a path, not as i.i.d. draws of one-period PMFs.

## Recap path

1. Prefer KDE over raw histograms when you need a custom density.
2. Use a **Gaussian** kernel for smoothness; set bandwidth with Scott/Silverman (or a tuned fraction of Scott).
3. Truncate with $d_{\min}-mh$ and $d_{\max}+mh$, evaluate PDF on integers, normalize to a PMF.
4. Clamp negative mass to 0; report $\mu_d$ and $\sigma_d$ from the discrete support.
5. Feed that PMF into simulation / policy search (next lessons).

## Takeaways

- Histograms need arbitrary bins and edges; they put mass only on midpoints and stay jagged.
- **Gaussian KDE** places a Normal kernel on each observation; **bandwidth** (Scott/Silverman or a tuned fraction) controls smoothness.
- Discretize by truncating with $d_{\min}-mh$ / $d_{\max}+mh$, evaluating the PDF on integers, and **normalizing**.
- Fold negative KDE mass onto **0** for operational demand.
- PMF moments $\mu_d,\sigma_d$ follow from $\sum p_i x_i$ and $\sum p_i x_i^2-\mu_d^2$; check independence before stacking periods.
