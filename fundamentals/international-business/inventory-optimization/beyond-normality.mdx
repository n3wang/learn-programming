# 7. Beyond Normality

Normal demand is a useful starting point, but it can be a poor description of some inventory items. Demand is often nonnegative and right-skewed: most periods are ordinary, with occasional unusually large orders. A Normal model is symmetric and assigns some probability to negative demand.

This chapter introduces the gamma distribution as one practical alternative. We will fit its parameters from demand summaries, account for a known minimum-demand offset, aggregate independent periods, and connect the distribution's upper tail to inventory targets and expected shortages. These are model-building tools, not an automatic rule that gamma is always better.

## Diagnose the shape before choosing a model

Consider this synthetic monthly sales sample for one item:

| Month | Units |
|---|---:|
| Jan | 68 |
| Feb | 73 |
| Mar | 75 |
| Apr | 76 |
| May | 78 |
| Jun | 79 |
| Jul | 80 |
| Aug | 81 |
| Sep | 82 |
| Oct | 84 |
| Nov | 92 |
| Dec | 112 |

The sample mean is about 81.7 units and the median is 79.5; the large December value pulls the mean upward. A fitted Normal curve may understate the chance of a large positive demand and may assign probability below zero. This does not establish that gamma is the correct model; it tells us to inspect the data, business calendar, and candidate distributions.

For observations $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, the population-moment skewness is:

$$
\gamma_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})^3}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\bar{x})^2\right)^{3/2}}
$$

Positive skew indicates a longer right tail, zero skew indicates symmetry in the third moment, and negative skew indicates a longer left tail. Different libraries use different sample corrections; state which convention is used when comparing values. Skewness alone does not describe the entire distribution.

### Why inspect normality?

Two common mismatches matter for inventory:

- **Support:** an ordinary Normal distribution permits negative values. Clipping simulated values at zero changes the distribution and its mean; it is not a neutral implementation detail.
- **Tail shape:** Normal demand is symmetric around its mean. A right-skewed item can have more unusually high demand than a Normal model predicts, which matters when setting a high service target.

Returns should usually be modeled as a separate flow unless the business can truly use a return to satisfy a future customer demand. Do not casually treat returns as negative demand.

## Fit a gamma distribution by moments

Use the shape-scale parameterization $X\sim\Gamma(k,\theta)$, where $k>0$ is the shape and $\theta>0$ is the scale. Its moments are:

$$
\mu=k\theta,\qquad \sigma^2=k\theta^2,\qquad \gamma_1=\frac{2}{\sqrt{k}}
$$

Matching a positive mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ gives:

$$
k=\frac{\mu^2}{\sigma^2},\qquad \theta=\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu},\qquad \gamma_1=\frac{2\sigma}{\mu}
$$

Unlike the ordinary Normal distribution, gamma demand is supported on nonnegative values. Larger variation relative to the mean yields smaller $k$ and a more pronounced right tail. Be explicit about the software convention: some libraries use **scale** $\theta$, while others use a rate $1/\theta$.

### A known minimum or baseline demand

If demand never falls below a credible operational baseline $d_{min}$, model:

$$
D=d_{min}+X,\qquad X\sim\Gamma(k',\theta')
$$

Fit the gamma component to the excess-demand mean $\mu'=\mu-d_{min}$ and the original standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$
k'=\frac{(\mu-d_{min})^2}{\sigma^2},\qquad
\theta'=\frac{\sigma^2}{\mu-d_{min}},\qquad
\gamma'_1=\frac{2\sigma}{\mu-d_{min}}
$$

This requires $\mu>d_{min}$ and $\sigma>0$. The floor should have a business or data-generating reason. Choosing the observed sample minimum automatically can overfit a small sample; validate the offset on new periods and compare tail behavior.

## Aggregate demand over a policy risk period

Let period demands be independent and identically distributed gamma variables with the same scale $\theta$. The sum of $\tau$ periods is also gamma:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}X_t\sim\Gamma(\tau k,\theta)
$$

For a demand floor $d_{min}$ per period:

$$
D_\tau=\tau d_{min}+\Gamma(\tau k',\theta'),\qquad
\mu_\tau=\tau\mu,\qquad
\sigma_\tau=\sqrt{\tau}\,\sigma
$$

For continuous review, use $\tau=L$; for periodic review, use $\tau=L+R$. The risk-period safety stock for cycle service target $\alpha$ is the gamma quantile minus the expected demand:

$$
\iota=F^{-1}_{D_\tau}(\alpha),\qquad SS=\iota-\mu_\tau
$$

Here $\iota$ is the reorder point $s$ or order-up-to level $S$, depending on the policy. In general this quantile requires a numerical library or table. SciPy's `scipy.stats.gamma.ppf(alpha, a=shape, loc=offset, scale=scale)` uses shape, location, and scale in that order. The lesson's Python exercises below avoid relying on SciPy by using precomputed CDF inputs where appropriate.

The exact sum result requires independent gamma variables with a common scale. If the period distributions have different scales, are correlated, or have changing parameters, the sum is not generally gamma; moment-matching another gamma is an approximation that should be validated.

### Worked risk-period target with an offset

Suppose weekly demand has mean 800 units, standard deviation 200, and a justified floor of 400. The gamma component has excess mean $\mu'=800-400=400$, shape $k'=400^2/200^2=4$, and scale $\theta'=200^2/400=100$. For a five-week protection period, its shape is $5(4)=20$, its scale remains 100, and the offset is $5(400)=2{,}000$ units.

If a numerical gamma quantile calculation gives the 95th percentile of $\Gamma(20,100)$ as 2,787.92, the total inventory threshold is $2{,}000+2{,}787.92=4{,}787.92$ units. Expected five-week demand is $5(800)=4{,}000$, so the implied safety stock is about 788 units. The quantile here is illustrative; calculate it with a trusted distribution implementation and validate the chosen demand model on observed data.

## From cycle service to expected shortage

Cycle service asks whether demand exceeds the inventory threshold at all. Fill rate asks what fraction of units are supplied immediately. For risk-period demand $D_\tau$ and inventory threshold $\iota$, expected units short are:

$$
U_s=\mathbb{E}[(D_\tau-\iota)^+]
$$

For $X\sim\Gamma(k,\theta)$ with $\iota\ge 0$, the gamma loss expression is:

$$
L_\Gamma(\iota;k,\theta)
=k\theta\left[1-F_\Gamma(\iota;k+1,\theta)\right]
-\iota\left[1-F_\Gamma(\iota;k,\theta)\right]
$$

For an offset gamma with risk-period floor $m_\tau$, shift the inventory threshold to $I'=\iota-m_\tau$ and evaluate the loss of the gamma component. If $I'\le0$, every nonnegative gamma-component demand exceeds that shifted threshold, so expected shortage is $k\theta-I'$; for $I'>0$, use $L_\Gamma(I';k,\theta)$. This keeps offset handling explicit rather than applying a zero-location formula to the unshifted threshold.

A simple long-run fill-rate approximation for periodic review is:

$$
\beta\approx 1-\frac{U_s}{\mu_dR}
$$

The denominator is expected demand per replenishment cycle. For continuous review with fixed order quantity, the cycle-demand denominator is $Q$. This approximation and the loss expression rely on a specified shortage model; backorders, lost sales, and timing details can change the operational fill rate. Cycle service and fill rate are related but are not interchangeable.

## Select a model carefully

Use diagnostics to decide what to investigate, not to declare a winner automatically.

### Compare skewness as a first screen

A Normal distribution has theoretical skewness 0. A moment-matched gamma has skewness $2\sigma/\mu$; an offset gamma has $2\sigma/(\mu-d_{min})$. As a rough screen, compare $|\gamma_{obs}-\gamma_{candidate}|$ across candidates. If observed skewness is much closer to one candidate, that is a useful signal for further testing. It does not prove that the candidate matches the actual tail or service-level quantiles.

### Compare empirical and fitted distributions

A histogram can reveal asymmetry and outliers. If comparing candidates with a histogram, use the same bin edges and range. Convert each candidate's CDF into probability mass per bin, $F(b_{j+1})-F(b_j)$, and compare like with like. Density heights have units of inverse demand and depend on bin width; normalizing histogram heights by their sum is not generally a probability-density normalization unless the bin width is also accounted for.

For empirical bin proportions $p_j$ and model bin probabilities $q_j$, one possible summary is:

$$
RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}(p_j-q_j)^2}
$$

An RMSE over bin probabilities is one summary, not a universal goodness-of-fit test. It weights all bins equally unless designed otherwise and may prioritize the center over the high-demand tail that drives shortages. For inventory decisions, inspect upper quantiles, exceedance counts, and service performance on held-out periods. A more advanced fit that fails out-of-sample checks is not an improvement.

## Takeaways

- Normality is convenient, not guaranteed; support and upper-tail fit matter to inventory decisions.
- For moment-matched $\Gamma(k,\theta)$, $k=\mu^2/\sigma^2$, $\theta=\sigma^2/\mu$, and skewness is $2/\sqrt{k}$.
- An offset gamma requires a defensible floor and $\mu>d_{min}$; fitting the sample minimum blindly can overfit.
- Independent same-scale gamma periods aggregate exactly by adding shapes; different scales or correlated periods need another model or an explicit approximation.
- Quantiles and expected-shortage formulas connect the chosen distribution to cycle service and fill rate, but model validation should focus on held-out upper-tail behavior.
