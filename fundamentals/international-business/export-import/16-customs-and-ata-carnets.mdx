# 16. Customs and ATA Carnets

> **Teaching rewrite** of customs clearance essentials for exporters and importers. Pedagogical summaries only — not customs or tax advice. Duty rates, valuation rules, and origin rules are set by each country and each trade agreement; always check the current tariff or obtain a binding ruling.

## Introduction

Every international shipment crosses at least two customs borders: export clearance in the seller’s country and import clearance in the buyer’s. Getting customs wrong means delays, storage charges, penalties, seized goods — or simply paying more duty than necessary.

This lesson covers:

- **Who** is responsible for clearance under each Incoterm®
- The **World Customs Organization** and the **Harmonized System (HS)**
- **Classification**, binding rulings, and customs brokers
- **Export controls**: licences and quotas
- **Customs valuation** (transaction value; CIF vs FOB bases)
- **Rules of origin** and preferential duty
- Duty-saving tools: **bonded warehouses**, **free trade zones**, and **duty drawback**
- Temporary imports with the **ATA Carnet**

---

## 16.1 Customs responsibilities and Incoterms®

Customs clearance involves four components:

1. **Classifying** the goods under the tariff
2. **Valuing** the goods for duty
3. Determining the **origin** of the goods
4. **Paying** duties and taxes and filing the declarations (often via a broker)

The Incoterm in the sale contract decides **who clears where**:

| Incoterm group | Export clearance | Import clearance & duties |
|---|---|---|
| **EXW** | Buyer | Buyer |
| **F** terms (FCA, FAS, FOB) | Seller | Buyer |
| **C** terms (CPT, CIP, CFR, CIF) | Seller | Buyer |
| **DAP, DPU** | Seller | Buyer |
| **DDP** | Seller | **Seller** |

:::caution Practical traps
- Under **EXW** the buyer must clear export — but many countries only let a resident party file the export declaration. Prefer **FCA**.
- Under **DDP** the seller must clear import and pay duties and **VAT** in the buyer’s country — which may require local registration. A seller unable to recover import VAT bears it as a cost.
:::

## 16.2 The WCO and the Harmonized System

The **World Customs Organization (WCO)**, Brussels, has about 186 members. It maintains the **Harmonized Commodity Description and Coding System (HS)**, used by over 200 economies for tariffs and trade statistics.

- Structure: **21 sections**, **~97 chapters**, then **headings (4 digits)** and **subheadings (6 digits)** — the 6-digit code is common worldwide.
- Countries add digits for their own tariffs and statistics (e.g. 8 digits in the EU’s Combined Nomenclature, 10 in the U.S. HTS).
- The HS is updated about every **5 years**; the current edition is **HS 2022** (HS 2028 is in preparation). Re-check codes after each update.
- Classification follows the **General Interpretative Rules (GIRs)** — applied in order, starting with the wording of the headings and notes.

### Case snapshot: coat or sweater?

An importer brought in knitted garments with a zip and pockets, declaring them as sweaters at a lower duty. Customs classified them as **jackets/coats**, which carried a higher rate, and assessed back duties on several years of imports. The dispute turned on technical criteria in the chapter notes (fabric weight, closures, intended outer use). The lesson: **small product features change codes**, and codes change duty.

### Worked example: the classification gap

Annual imports valued at **USD 400,000**. The importer used a code at **17.5%**; customs says the correct code carries **20%**.

| | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| Duty paid | 17.5% × 400,000 | 70,000 |
| Duty due | 20% × 400,000 | 80,000 |
| **Shortfall per year** | | **10,000** |

Over three years that is **USD 30,000** in back duty — before interest and penalties.

### Binding rulings and brokers

- A **binding tariff ruling** (e.g. EU **Binding Tariff Information, BTI**; U.S. CBP rulings) is a written decision by customs on the classification of a specific product. It binds customs for that product (EU BTI: valid 3 years) and gives the importer certainty.
- **Customs brokers** prepare and file declarations, classify, pay duties on the client’s behalf, and advise. The **importer remains legally responsible** for the accuracy of declarations in most countries.

## 16.3 Export controls

Governments restrict exports through:

- **Export licences**: required for controlled goods — military items, **dual-use** goods (usable for both civil and military purposes: certain chemicals, electronics, software, encryption), cultural goods, and sometimes strategic raw materials. Controls follow multilateral regimes (Wassenaar Arrangement, Nuclear Suppliers Group, Australia Group, MTCR) and national lists (e.g. EU Dual-Use Regulation, U.S. EAR).
- **Sanctions and embargoes**: restrictions against particular countries, entities, or persons — screen every customer and end-user.
- **Quotas**: limits on the quantity of goods exported or imported; **tariff-rate quotas** give a lower duty rate up to a set volume.

## 16.4 Customs valuation

Most countries apply the **WTO Customs Valuation Agreement**. The primary method is the **transaction value** — the price actually paid or payable — adjusted for certain items:

- **Added** if not already in the price: **selling commissions** (buying commissions are excluded), packing, **assists** (tools, moulds supplied free by the buyer), **royalties** that are a condition of sale, and proceeds of resale accruing to the seller.
- Five fallback methods apply in order if transaction value cannot be used (e.g. related-party sales where the relationship affected the price).

### CIF or FOB basis?

| Basis | Includes international freight & insurance? | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **CIF** | Yes, to the border of import | EU, China, most countries |
| **FOB** | No | United States, Canada, Australia |

### Worked example: same goods, different duty

Goods **USD 50,000** FOB; freight **USD 4,000**; insurance **USD 500**; duty rate **10%**.

| Basis | Customs value | Duty |
|---|---|---|
| FOB | 50,000 | **5,000** |
| CIF | 54,500 | **5,450** |

The CIF-basis country collects **USD 450** more — freight costs raise duty.

## 16.5 Rules of origin

Origin decides whether goods get **preferential** duty (free-trade agreements, **GSP** schemes for developing countries) or face **anti-dumping duties, quotas, or sanctions**.

- **Wholly obtained**: goods grown, mined, or made entirely in one country.
- **Substantial transformation** for goods with foreign inputs, typically tested by:
  - **Change of tariff classification** — the finished good falls under a different heading from its imported inputs
  - **Value-added (regional value content) percentage** — a minimum share of value must come from the country/region
  - **Specific processing operations**

Proof is a **certificate of origin** (non-preferential certificates usually from chambers of commerce) or an origin statement by an approved/registered exporter, depending on the agreement.

### Worked example: regional value content

A product sells for **USD 100** ex-works. Non-originating materials cost **USD 45**. The agreement requires **at least 50%** regional value content (build-down method).

RVC = (100 − 45) / 100 = **55%** → qualifies. If the non-originating materials rose to USD 52, RVC = 48% → **fails**, and the buyer pays full MFN duty.

## 16.6 Duty deferral and relief

| Tool | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **Bonded (customs) warehouse** | Goods stored under customs control with duties **suspended** until released for home use; no duty if re-exported |
| **Free trade zone / free zone** (U.S. “foreign-trade zone”) | Goods enter, are stored, assembled, or processed without duty; duty paid only on entering the domestic market — sometimes at the lower rate of the finished product (“inverted tariff”) |
| **Inward processing / duty drawback** | Duty suspended or refunded on imported inputs that are incorporated into exported products |
| **Authorised Economic Operator (AEO)** | Trusted-trader status giving simplified procedures and fewer inspections |

### Worked example: bonded warehouse cash saving

An importer brings in goods with **USD 120,000** of duty, but sells them evenly over **6 months**. Cost of money: **6% a year**.

Paying all duty on arrival ties up 120,000 for an average of 3 months: 120,000 × 6% × 3/12 = **USD 1,800** in interest. Paying as goods leave the bonded warehouse avoids most of that (less warehouse fees). If some goods are re-exported, their duty is never paid.

## 16.7 Temporary imports and the ATA Carnet

Samples, exhibition goods, and professional equipment (cameras, instruments, laptops) often travel abroad and **come back**. Paying duty — or posting a security deposit — at every border would be costly.

The **ATA Carnet** (“Admission Temporaire / Temporary Admission”) is an international customs document — a “passport for goods” — that lets these goods enter participating countries **duty- and tax-free for up to one year**.

- Accepted in **around 80+ countries and territories** (check the current list), under the WCO’s ATA and Istanbul Conventions.
- Covers **commercial samples**, **professional equipment**, and goods for **exhibitions and fairs**. Not for goods to be sold, processed, or consumed.
- Issued by **national chambers of commerce**, which **guarantee** duties to customs if goods are not re-exported. The international guarantee chain is managed by the **ICC World Chambers Federation (WCF)**.
- The holder provides security to the issuing chamber (deposit, bank guarantee, or insurance) and pays an issuing fee.
- Each border crossing uses vouchers (exportation, importation, re-exportation, re-importation, transit) stamped by customs.
- Many countries now accept or pilot **electronic ATA Carnets (eATA)**.

**Alternative**: in the U.S. and some other countries, importers may use a **Temporary Importation under Bond (TIB)** — a customs bond guaranteeing re-export, typically within one year. Unlike the carnet, a TIB is arranged per country and per entry.

### Case snapshot: the trade-show tour

A machinery firm exhibited at fairs in three countries over four months. With one carnet it crossed every border without paying duties or posting local deposits; at the end, re-export vouchers proved the goods had left each country. A sample sold at one fair would have required normal import clearance and duty for that unit — the carnet does not cover sold goods.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Clearance = **classify, value, determine origin, pay/declare** — the Incoterm decides who does it where
- The **HS** (WCO) is harmonised to **6 digits**; current edition **HS 2022**; small product features change the code
- Use **binding rulings** for certainty; the importer stays responsible even with a broker
- Valuation starts from **transaction value**; **CIF** countries tax freight, **FOB** countries do not
- Origin rules (tariff shift, value content) unlock **preferential** duty
- Defer or avoid duty with **bonded warehouses, free zones, inward processing, drawback**
- **ATA Carnet**: duty-free temporary admission for up to 1 year for samples, equipment, and exhibition goods
:::
