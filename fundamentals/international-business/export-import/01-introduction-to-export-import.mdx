# 1. Introduction to Export–Import Practice

> **Teaching rewrite** of standard export–import foundations (commercial sales, risk, documentary systems). Not a verbatim reprint of any commercial guide.

Professional exporters and importers share a common toolkit: **sale contracts**, **trade terms** (who pays freight/insurance and when risk passes), **transport documents**, **payment instruments**, and **insurance**. Distribution, IP, and cross-border e-commerce sit on the edge of that core — important, but secondary to getting goods and documents right.

This track focuses on **tangible goods** sold between businesses: the legal skeleton, how money moves, and how carriage documents prove what happened.

---

## Commercial sales and the law

**Commercial sales** means merchant-to-merchant (B2B) sales of **goods** — not consumer retail, not pure IP licenses, not real estate, not financial paper.

Domestically, special commercial codes often apply. Internationally, many goods contracts fall under the **UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG)** when both parties’ states are contracting states (or when the parties choose it).

Familiar domestic tools still appear: pro forma invoices, purchase orders, packing lists. Distance, currency, and multiple jurisdictions add instruments that domestic trade rarely needs: **Incoterms®**-style delivery terms, **letters of credit**, and carefully chosen dispute forums.

---

## The risk map

Cross-border deals add layers on top of ordinary commercial risk:

| Risk | What goes wrong | Common controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Transport** | Damage, loss, theft over long routes and hand-offs | Know carrier liability vs B/L terms; buy the right cargo cover |
| **Non-payment** | Slow pay, default, fraud | Credit checks; irrevocable documentary credits; other security |
| **Quality** | Goods not as expected when you cannot inspect pre-shipment | Independent inspection certificates (often L/C conditions) |
| **FX** | Contract currency moves against you | Invoice in home currency when you can; hedge when you cannot |
| **Legal** | Foreign courts / law make recovery costly | Negotiate forum & law; consider institutional arbitration |
| **Force majeure** | Strike, war, disaster, route collapse | Clear force majeure clauses; realistic lead times |
| **Investment** | Export program spend never pays back | Honest go/no-go before building foreign distribution |

:::tip Start small
Exporting is optional. A firm that cannot absorb documentary discipline and working-capital stretch should dominate a domestic niche first.
:::

## Documentary sales: goods **and** paper

Modern export risk control is built on a **dual pipeline**:

1. Physical goods move.
2. A matching **documentary** trail moves (often faster, and often controlling payment).

Classic “shipment” terms (think FOB / CIF-style allocation of cost and risk) trained courts and traders to treat the deal as a sale of **documents as well as goods**. Presenting the wrong bill of lading date, insurance note, or invoice description can block payment even when the container looks fine — and conversely, clean documents can force payment before quality disputes are sorted out.

### Core document set

1. **Contract of sale** (master deal — often PO + terms, or a full agreement)
2. **Transport document** (bill of lading, waybill, …)
3. **Payment documents** (documentary credit, drafts/bills of exchange, …)
4. **Insurance document** (policy, certificate, or cover note)

Inspect documents with the same seriousness you inspect goods. Paper can be forged or mistyped; banks under credits generally check **facial conformity**, not factory truth — so importers often require **pre-shipment inspection** certificates.

### Two rejection rights (intuition)

Courts have long treated **rejection of documents** and **rejection of goods** as distinct. A seller can breach on shipment timing in the papers even if later physical delivery is accepted; the documentary breach still mattered when the buyer relied on the papers. Lesson for operators: diary every documentary deadline as carefully as the sailing date.

### Limits of paperwork

Credits and inspection certificates reduce risk; they do not erase it. Add credit reports, export credit insurance, factoring, and **know-your-counterparty** checks. International fraud is ordinary — treat new partners as unverified until proven otherwise.

## Culture, language, and “same word, different meaning”

Misunderstandings are not soft skills fluff — they become **payment disputes**, **quality fights**, and **litigation in a hostile forum**.

Controls that actually help:

- Credit-check counterparties; demand references you can verify
- Negotiate hard for usable law/forum (or walk away)
- Use complete contracts / GTCs: choice of law, forum or arbitration, quality specs, payment mechanics
- Name a current **Incoterms®** rule in the quote (which edition you intend)
- Prepare negotiations: who decides, what “agent” means locally, what standards apply

Cross-border deals also collide with different **tax, customs, FX controls, accounting, product standards, and consumer tastes**. After-sales service abroad can cost more than the margin. Translating (and updating) manuals and marketing copy is a real budget line, not a weekend task.

:::caution Vague acceptance kills deals
A buyer once approved a sample garment after a lab cut for fiber testing and scrawled something like “OK — ship as agreed.” The factory read that as a production instruction and shipped thousands of units matching the *altered* sample. Lesson: acceptance notes must state **what is approved** (specs, photos, unmarked samples) and what is **not** a change order.
:::

## Risk → control (operator view)

| Situation | Typical control |
|---|---|
| Goods damaged / stolen in transit | Cargo insurance sized to the contingency; read B/L & carriage terms; packing & routing instructions |
| Exporter: buyer takes goods and stalls payment | Documentary credit under modern UCP practice; or open account **plus** standby / demand guarantee |
| Importer: quality unknown until arrival | Independent inspection certificate (often an L/C required document) |
| Counterparty may not perform as promised | Advance payment slices; performance bonds / standby credits |
| Fraud / identity theater | Deep credit investigation; industry crime-intelligence networks where available |

## Who builds the plumbing: chambers, ICC, IGOs

### Chambers of commerce

City and national chambers often run trade desks, seminars, missions to find agents/distributors, and services such as **certificates of origin**. They are the local on-ramp for firms that are new to export.

### ICC (International Chamber of Commerce)

For private traders, ICC matters because it publishes **practice standards** the market actually uses:

- **UCP** — documentary credit practice
- **Incoterms®** — delivery / cost / risk shorthand in quotes
- **Arbitration** — widely used private dispute resolution
- Model contracts, marketing ethics codes, anti-corruption guidance
- **Commercial Crime Services** (maritime fraud/piracy intelligence, financial fraud support, counterfeiting intelligence)
- **World Chambers Federation** — chamber network; ATA Carnet temporary-admission chain; chamber best-practice forums

Treat ICC instruments as **contractual tools you incorporate by reference**, not as government law by themselves.

### Other bodies you will meet by name

**Intergovernmental**

| Body | Why traders hear of it |
|---|---|
| [UNCITRAL](https://uncitral.un.org/) | CISG; model laws; guarantee / EDI / arbitration instruments |
| [Unidroit](https://www.unidroit.org/) | Private-law projects (leasing, agency, franchising, …) |
| [WCO](https://www.wcoomd.org/) | Customs cooperation & efficiency |
| [ITC](https://www.intracen.org/) | Export training/guides (esp. developing-country focus) |

**Non-governmental / industry**

| Body | Why traders hear of it |
|---|---|
| [IATA](https://www.iata.org/) | Air waybill standards |
| [FIATA](https://fiata.org/) | Forwarder standards; FIATA B/L references |
| [CMI](https://comitemaritime.org/) | Maritime law unification; Hague / Hague–Visby lineage for B/Ls |
| [IRU](https://www.iru.org/) | Road transport; **TIR** sealed-truck customs transit |

## The public framework: GATT → WTO

After WWII, governments built a multilateral system to cut trade barriers. Under **GATT** (from 1947), average industrial tariffs fell dramatically and merchandise trade exploded. The **WTO** (1995) succeeded GATT as a permanent institution with a secretariat.

Compared with classic GATT:

- Covers **goods**, **services**, and **trade-related IP** (not goods alone)
- Dispute settlement is designed to be **faster and more automatic**
- Core GATT disciplines continue as **GATT 1994** inside the WTO agreements

:::tip Private vs public stack
**WTO rules** constrain what governments may do to each other’s trade. **Your contract** still needs Incoterms®, payment terms, and documents — WTO membership does not fill in your L/C.
:::

Public portal: [wto.org](https://www.wto.org/).

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Export–import = **goods pipeline** + **document pipeline**
- Culture and language errors become **spec and payment** failures — write acceptance criteria
- Match each risk to a control (credit, inspection, insurance, bond)
- ICC / UNCITRAL tools sit in **contracts**; WTO sits in **government** rules
- Verify counterparties; documents reduce risk, they do not replace diligence
:::
