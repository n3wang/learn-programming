# 15. International Business and Intellectual Property

> **Teaching rewrite** of intellectual property (IP) essentials for exporters and importers. Pedagogical summaries only — not legal advice. IP rights are **territorial** and registration rules differ by country; confirm with an IP attorney before filing or launching.

## Introduction

A company’s brand, inventions, designs, software, and know-how are often worth more than its factories. Yet IP rights stop at the border: a trademark registered at home gives **no protection abroad** unless it is registered or otherwise protected there. Exporters who forget this find a local distributor — or a counterfeiter — has registered their brand first.

This lesson covers:

- The IP questions every international business must ask
- The international framework: **WIPO** and **TRIPS**
- **Trademarks** and the **Madrid System**
- **Patents**, the **Paris Convention** priority right, and the **PCT**
- **Copyright**, **trade secrets**, and **industrial designs**
- **Domain names** and the **UDRP**
- **Counterfeiting and piracy**, enforcement, and **parallel imports**

---

## 15.1 IP issues in international business

Typical questions:

- Are our trademarks, inventions, and designs protected in each **target market**?
- Do our product names clash with **existing rights** there?
- When we use agents, distributors, licensees, or joint-venture partners: **who owns** the IP and the goodwill, and what happens when the relationship ends?
- Do we **license** our technology, or are we licensees?
- How do we protect **confidential know-how** when sharing it with partners?
- How do we stop **counterfeits** and **parallel imports**?
- Can IP be used as **collateral**, or valued in a sale of the business?

### The international framework

| Body / treaty | Role |
|---|---|
| **WIPO** (World Intellectual Property Organization, Geneva; ~190 member states) | Administers most IP treaties (Paris, Berne, Madrid, PCT, Hague) and runs the WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center |
| **TRIPS Agreement** (WTO, 1995) | Sets **minimum standards** of protection and enforcement that all WTO members must provide; disputes go through WTO dispute settlement |

## 15.2 Trademarks

A **trademark** is a sign — word, logo, shape, colour, even sound — that distinguishes one company’s goods or services from others’.

- Usually protected by **registration** country by country (some common-law countries also protect unregistered marks through use).
- Registration lasts typically **10 years** and is **renewable indefinitely** — as long as the mark is used and renewed.
- Registrations are by **class** of goods/services (the Nice Classification has 45 classes).

### Case snapshot: the borrowed name

A clothing company had used a children’s-wear brand at home for years, then started exporting. When it tried to enter a neighbouring market, it found a local business had already registered a nearly identical name there. The exporter had to choose between buying the mark, litigating (uncertain), or **rebranding** for that market. The lesson: **search and file before you export**, not after.

### The Madrid System

The **Madrid Protocol** lets an applicant file **one international application**, in one language, through its home office, designating many member countries — the System covers over 130 countries through 110+ members (including the EU and the African Intellectual Property Organization).

- One set of fees, one renewal date, central management of changes.
- Each designated country can still **refuse** protection under its own law.
- **Dependency**: for the first 5 years the international registration depends on the home application/registration; if that falls (a “central attack”), the international registration falls too — though it can be converted into national applications.

### Paris Convention priority

The **Paris Convention** (1883) gives a **right of priority**: after first filing in one member country, the applicant has

| Right | Priority period |
|---|---|
| **Trademarks** and **industrial designs** | **6 months** |
| **Patents** and utility models | **12 months** |

to file in other member countries, and those later filings are treated as if filed on the first date.

## 15.3 Patents

A **patent** gives the owner the exclusive right to prevent others from making, using, selling, or importing the invention in that country — normally for **20 years from filing** — in exchange for public disclosure. The invention must be **new**, **inventive** (non-obvious), and **industrially applicable**.

:::caution Novelty traps
Showing a product at a trade fair or publishing it **before filing** can destroy novelty in most countries. File first, then exhibit.
:::

### The Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT)

The **PCT** (~158 contracting states) does not grant a “world patent”. It provides:

1. **One international application** — effective as a filing in all PCT states
2. An **international search report** and written opinion on patentability
3. Optional **international preliminary examination**
4. Entry into the **national phase** usually **30 months from the priority date** (31 in some offices) — where each national/regional office decides whether to grant

The value of the PCT is **time**: the applicant gets up to 30 months to test the market and raise money before paying for translations and national fees.

### Regional routes

- **European Patent Office (EPO)**: one examination for 39 member states; after grant, the patent is validated nationally — or, since June 2023, as a single **Unitary Patent** in participating EU states, with the **Unified Patent Court**.
- Other regional systems: ARIPO, OAPI (Africa), the Eurasian Patent Office, GCC Patent Office.

### Worked example: deadlines

A company files a patent application at home on **1 March 2026** (priority date).

| Route | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Paris Convention — file directly in other countries | **1 March 2027** (12 months) |
| PCT — file international application | **1 March 2027** (12 months) |
| PCT — enter national phase | **1 September 2028** (30 months) |

If it also filed its **trademark** at home on 1 March 2026, the Paris priority deadline for the mark abroad is **1 September 2026** (6 months).

## 15.4 Copyright, trade secrets, and designs

### Copyright

- Protects original **expression** — texts, software, music, images, films — not ideas.
- Arises **automatically** on creation in all **Berne Convention** countries (~180); no registration needed (though the U.S. requires registration before suing for infringement of U.S. works).
- Minimum term: **life of the author + 50 years** (Berne/TRIPS); many countries, including the EU and U.S., grant **life + 70**.
- The WIPO **internet treaties** (WCT, WPPT) extended protection to digital use.

### Trade secrets

Confidential business information — formulas, processes, customer lists, pricing — with commercial value, kept secret by **reasonable measures**. TRIPS (Art. 39) requires protection against disclosure or use “contrary to honest commercial practices”; the EU Trade Secrets Directive (2016) and the U.S. Defend Trade Secrets Act (2016) strengthened remedies.

Protection lasts as long as secrecy does. Practical measures: **NDAs** with partners and employees, need-to-know access, marking, and exit procedures.

### Industrial designs

Protect the **appearance** of a product (shape, lines, ornamentation).

- **EU**: a **registered Community (EU) design** covers all 27 member states — up to **25 years** in 5-year terms; an **unregistered** EU design gives **3 years** from first disclosure in the EU against copying.
- **Hague System** (WIPO): one international design application for many countries.

## 15.5 Domain names

A domain name can be the most visible piece of a brand. Disputes arise when someone registers a domain matching another’s trademark (**cybersquatting**).

The **UDRP** (Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy, ICANN) — mandatory for generic top-level domains like .com — lets a trademark owner win transfer or cancellation by proving **all three** elements:

1. The domain is **identical or confusingly similar** to its trademark
2. The registrant has **no rights or legitimate interests** in the name
3. The domain was **registered and is being used in bad faith**

Cases go to providers such as the **WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center**, are decided on written submissions in about two months, and cost far less than court litigation. Remedies are limited to transfer or cancellation — no damages.

## 15.6 Counterfeiting, piracy, and parallel imports

- **Counterfeiting**: unauthorised use of a trademark on look-alike goods. **Piracy**: unauthorised copying of copyright works. Both harm brand owners, consumers (unsafe medicines, parts), and governments (lost tax).
- **Enforcement tools**: record trademarks with **customs** (e.g. EU customs applications for action, U.S. CBP recordation) so border officers can detain suspect goods; online marketplace takedown programmes; civil and criminal action.
- **BASCAP** (Business Action to Stop Counterfeiting and Piracy) is ICC’s initiative bringing companies together on the problem.

:::caution ACTA update
The **Anti-Counterfeiting Trade Agreement (ACTA)**, signed in 2011, was **rejected by the European Parliament in 2012** and never entered into force. Enforcement work has since moved to bilateral/regional trade agreements and cooperation among customs authorities and platforms.
:::

### Parallel imports (grey market)

**Genuine** goods put on the market by the right holder in one country and imported into another **without the right holder’s consent** — usually to exploit price differences.

Whether the right holder can block them depends on **exhaustion** rules, which TRIPS leaves to each country:

| Regime | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **National exhaustion** | Rights exhausted only by first sale at home — parallel imports can be blocked | Some countries for certain rights |
| **Regional exhaustion** | First sale anywhere in the region exhausts rights; imports from outside can be blocked | **EU/EEA** for trademarks |
| **International exhaustion** | First sale anywhere exhausts rights — parallel imports allowed | Several countries (varies by right) |

### Case snapshot: the cheaper bottles

A brand owner sold its product cheaply in a distant market. Traders bought stock there and shipped it back to the brand owner’s main market, undercutting authorised distributors. Because that main market applied **regional exhaustion**, and the goods were first sold outside the region, the brand owner could stop the imports — but only by proving the goods had never been put on the market inside the region with its consent.

### Worked example: is the grey market worth it?

A perfume sells for **EUR 60** wholesale in Market A and **EUR 40** in Market B. A parallel trader pays transport and handling of **EUR 4** per unit and needs a margin of **EUR 6**.

Landed cost into A = 40 + 4 = **EUR 44**; with margin the trader can offer **EUR 50**, undercutting the authorised price by **EUR 10** (about 17%). Narrowing the price gap below EUR 10 removes the incentive — one reason brands harmonise prices across markets.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- IP is **territorial** — register in each market (often before your distributor or a squatter does)
- **WIPO** runs the filing treaties; **TRIPS** sets enforceable minimum standards
- Priority: **6 months** for trademarks/designs, **12 months** for patents (Paris Convention)
- **Madrid** = one trademark application; **PCT** = one patent application and ~30 months to decide; **Hague** = designs
- Copyright is automatic; trade secrets last as long as you keep them secret
- **UDRP** for cybersquatting: similarity + no legitimate interest + bad faith
- Fight counterfeits with **customs recordation**; parallel imports depend on **exhaustion** rules
:::
