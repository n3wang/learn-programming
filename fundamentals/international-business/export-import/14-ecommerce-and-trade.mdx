# 14. E-Commerce and International Trade: Practice and Policy

> **Teaching rewrite** of cross-border e-commerce practice and policy. Pedagogical summaries only — not legal, tax, or compliance advice. E-commerce rules change fast (VAT schemes, de minimis thresholds, data-transfer frameworks); treat dates and thresholds as points to verify, not settled facts.

## Introduction

The internet lets a ten-person company sell to customers in fifty countries — and buy from suppliers just as far away. But cross-border online selling turns problems that were handled once per container into problems handled **once per parcel**: duties, VAT, customs codes, payment fraud, data protection, and which country’s law applies to an electronic contract.

This lesson covers:

- Market categories (B2C, B2B, C2C) and why measurement is hard
- **Landed cost**: who pays duties and taxes, and the DDP vs “duties for buyer” choice
- **Payments**, fraud, and **chargebacks**
- **Customs classification** for online sellers
- Legal recognition of **electronic contracts, documents, and signatures**
- **Data protection**, privacy, spam, and information compliance
- Trade policy: WTO, the e-commerce moratorium, and the forums shaping internet governance

**Assumptions.** A small or mid-sized seller shipping physical goods ordered online to buyers in other countries; digital services are mentioned where policy differs.

## 14.1 The cross-border e-commerce market

| Category | Who trades | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **B2C** | Business → consumer | Web shop shipping parcels abroad |
| **B2B** | Business → business | Online procurement portal, wholesale marketplace |
| **C2C** | Consumer → consumer (via a platform) | Online marketplaces for second-hand goods |

Measurement has always been patchy — agencies and research firms define “e-commerce” differently, so figures are not comparable across sources. The trend, however, is clear: online buying has grown from a minority activity in the 2000s to a mainstream channel, and cross-border parcels have become a major share of postal and express volumes. Check UNCTAD, OECD, or national statistics for current figures.

## 14.2 Landed cost, Incoterms®, and taxes

A domestic web shop quotes one price. A cross-border shop needs a different **landed cost** (goods + shipping + duties + taxes) for every destination — and buyers want to see it **before** they order.

### Two basic choices

| | **Duties paid by seller (DDP)** | **Duties paid by buyer** (any other Incoterm, e.g. CPT, CIP, DAP) |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer experience | Parcel arrives, nothing more to pay | Carrier demands duties and taxes before releasing the parcel |
| Seller’s risk | Estimating duties and taxes wrongly | Buyer **refuses delivery** — seller pays return shipping and loses the sale |
| Needs | Accurate duty/tax calculation and registrations | Clear disclosure at checkout |

:::caution Incoterms® edition note
Older guides mention **DDU**; it was removed in Incoterms® 2010. The current equivalent idea is **DAP** (delivered, buyer clears import and pays duties).
:::

### VAT on low-value imports

Many countries used to exempt small parcels (a **de minimis** threshold) from duty and VAT. That is changing:

- **EU (since July 2021)**: VAT is due on **all** imported goods; the old small-value VAT exemption was abolished. Sellers can register in the **Import One-Stop Shop (IOSS)** to charge VAT at checkout on consignments up to EUR 150 so parcels clear without extra charges. The EU customs reform also moves to remove the EUR 150 **duty** exemption — check the timetable.
- Several other markets (e.g. the UK, Australia, Norway, New Zealand) require foreign sellers or marketplaces to collect VAT/GST on low-value goods at the point of sale.
- The **U.S.** long had a high de minimis (USD 800) but has suspended or narrowed it — verify the current rule before quoting.

### Worked example: landed cost for one parcel

Goods **USD 100**, shipping **USD 20**, insurance **USD 1**. Destination charges duty of **5%** on the CIF value and VAT of **15%** on (CIF + duty). (Illustrative rates.)

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| CIF value | 100 + 20 + 1 | 121.00 |
| Duty | 5% × 121 | 6.05 |
| VAT base | 121 + 6.05 | 127.05 |
| VAT | 15% × 127.05 | 19.06 |
| **Landed cost** | 121 + 6.05 + 19.06 | **146.11** |

Selling at “USD 100 + shipping” and leaving the buyer to pay ~USD 25 at the door invites refusals. Selling DDP at about USD 146 removes the surprise — if the estimate is right.

## 14.3 Payments, fraud, and chargebacks

- **Cards** (Visa, Mastercard, American Express…) dominate, but many markets prefer **local methods** (domestic card schemes, bank transfers, wallets, buy-now-pay-later).
- **Payment service providers / wallets** (e.g. PayPal-type services and acquirers specialising in cross-border processing) accept payment for the seller, absorb data-security burdens, and offer local methods. Read their seller-protection rules carefully — protection usually depends on proof of delivery.
- **Strong customer authentication** (e.g. 3-D Secure; mandatory for many EU card payments under PSD2) shifts fraud liability away from the merchant when used.

### Fraud and chargebacks

Cross-border orders carry higher fraud rates than domestic ones, so merchants screen and reject more of them.

A **chargeback** is a reversal the cardholder’s bank initiates on the cardholder’s behalf — for fraud (“I didn’t buy this”) or disputes (“never arrived”, “not as described”). The merchant loses the money plus a fee unless it wins the dispute with evidence (delivery confirmation, authentication, correspondence).

### Worked example: the cost of chargebacks

1,000 orders a month, average **USD 80**; chargeback rate **1%**; fee **USD 20** per chargeback; merchant wins **40%** of disputes.

| Item | Calculation | USD |
|---|---|---|
| Chargebacks | 1% × 1,000 | 10 |
| Revenue lost (disputes lost) | 10 × 60% × 80 | 480 |
| Fees (charged on all chargebacks) | 10 × 20 | 200 |
| **Monthly cost** | | **680** (0.85% of sales) |

Reducing the rate to 0.5% (better screening, authentication, tracking) roughly halves that cost.

## 14.4 Customs classification for online sellers

Every product crossing a border needs a **Harmonized System (HS) code** (details in Lesson 16). For online sellers:

- The code sets the **duty rate**, decides **preferential treatment** under free-trade agreements, and goes on the **commercial invoice** and **certificate of origin**.
- With thousands of SKUs, classify only what you actually export — then make **classification of new products part of the listing process**.
- Small sellers often rely on carriers, marketplaces, or classification software — but the **seller remains responsible** for the declaration.

## 14.5 Law for electronic contracts, documents, and signatures

### UNCITRAL instruments

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Model Law on Electronic Commerce** (1996) and **on Electronic Signatures** (2001) | Templates many countries used for domestic e-commerce laws |
| **Convention on the Use of Electronic Communications in International Contracts** (2005; in force since 2013) | Electronic messages satisfy form requirements of other conventions; rules on location of parties, time and place of dispatch and receipt, invitations to offer; respects **party autonomy** |
| **Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records (MLETR)** (2017) | Lets electronic records act like bills of lading, bills of exchange, and promissory notes — adopted by a growing number of jurisdictions (e.g. the UK’s Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023) |

### ICC and industry tools

- **ICC eTerms** (2004): short clauses parties incorporate to confirm they intend a binding electronic contract.
- **eUCP** and **eURC**: supplements to UCP 600 and URC 522 for electronic presentation (see Lesson 9; now eUCP 2.0).
- **Digital trade platforms** — e.g. Bolero and other electronic bill of lading and trade-document platforms — replace paper document flows among traders, carriers, and banks. (Earlier pioneers such as TradeCard were absorbed into larger supply-chain networks.)

### Electronic signatures

| Term | Meaning (as used here) |
|---|---|
| **Electronic signature** | Any data in electronic form, attached to or logically linked with a message, used to identify the signer and show approval |
| **Digital signature** | A specific cryptographic technique (public/private keys) that proves who signed and that the message was not altered |

ICC’s policy recommendations for signature laws: **freedom of contract**, legal acceptance across borders, **technology neutrality**, no localisation or local-partner requirements, and competition among authentication providers. Major frameworks today include the EU’s **eIDAS** regulation and the U.S. E-SIGN Act and UETA.

### Information compliance

Rules from many fields (tax, anti-money laundering, sanctions, consumer protection, data protection, security) dictate how companies use technology. They sometimes **conflict** — e.g. one country restricts sending personal data abroad while another requires screening customers against watch lists — and may be hard to find. Map the obligations per market before launching.

## 14.6 Data protection, privacy, and spam

### From the 1995 Directive to the GDPR

The EU **Data Protection Directive 95/46/EC** was replaced in 2018 by the **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**, which applies to non-EU sellers that target EU customers. Personal data may leave the EU only with a legal basis, for example:

| Transfer tool | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Adequacy decision** | The Commission recognises a country’s protection as adequate (e.g. the EU–US Data Privacy Framework for certified U.S. companies) |
| **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)** | Approved contract terms between exporter and importer of data |
| **Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs)** | Approved group-wide rules for transfers within a multinational (ICC published a standard BCR application form) |

Other major regimes include APEC’s Cross-Border Privacy Rules, Brazil’s LGPD, China’s PIPL, and U.S. state laws.

### ICC’s privacy principles for governments

1. Flexible, responsive protection — accept **self-regulation** and privacy-enhancing technology
2. **Educate** the public
3. **Cooperate internationally**; avoid needless barriers to cross-border data flows
4. Endorse **model contracts, codes of conduct, seal programmes**

### Spam

No single legal definition exists; ICC uses “spam” for harmful, fraudulent, malicious, misleading or illegal messages, generally sent in bulk. Defence is a **toolkit**: laws (many require consent or easy opt-out for marketing email), industry codes, and technical filters and authentication.

## 14.7 Trade policy and internet governance

### WTO and digital trade

- WTO members have never settled whether electronically delivered products are **goods** (GATT: market access and national treatment) or **services** (GATS: obligations only where a country has committed).
- Since 1998, members have repeatedly renewed a **moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions** — under political pressure at each Ministerial Conference.
- A plurilateral **Joint Statement Initiative on e-commerce** has negotiated rules on e-signatures, paperless trading, consumer protection, and spam among a large group of members.

**ICC’s business objectives**: build e-business infrastructure without barriers; implement and extend **telecom and IT services liberalisation** (and eliminate duties on IT products, as in the WTO **Information Technology Agreement**); develop trade in goods and services via e-business; and protect **IP online** through TRIPS and the WIPO “internet treaties” (Copyright Treaty, Performances and Phonograms Treaty).

### Policy forums

| Forum | Role |
|---|---|
| **WSIS** (Geneva 2003, Tunis 2005) | UN summits that set the information-society agenda |
| **Internet Governance Forum (IGF)** | Annual UN multi-stakeholder dialogue since 2006 |
| **OECD** (38 members) | Guidelines on privacy, security, consumer protection, digital tax; business input via **Business at OECD (BIAC)** |
| **APEC** | Asia-Pacific trade facilitation and cross-border privacy rules |
| **ICC BASIS** | ICC’s initiative representing business in information-society dialogues |

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Cross-border e-commerce moves duties, VAT, fraud, and data rules down to **every parcel**
- Show **landed cost**: DDP removes doorstep surprises; duties-for-buyer risks refusals
- Low-value import exemptions are shrinking — **EU IOSS**, VAT collection by sellers/marketplaces elsewhere
- Manage fraud with authentication and evidence; price in **chargebacks**
- Classify products before listing them for export
- E-contracts and e-documents are legally supported (UNCITRAL convention, **MLETR**, eUCP, e-signature laws)
- **GDPR** governs EU personal data; transfers need adequacy, SCCs, or BCRs
:::
