# 13. International Transport

> **Teaching rewrite** of international transport practice, law, documents, and cargo insurance. Pedagogical summaries only — not legal, logistics, or insurance advice. Treaty memberships and liability limits change (limits are revised periodically); figures marked illustrative are for learning only.

## Introduction

How goods move decides their **price**, **delivery speed**, and **risk of loss**. Big traders run logistics departments; smaller ones rely on **freight forwarders**. Either way, the person booking transport must know exactly what the **sale contract** promised: pickup place, delivery place, who pays loading and handling, packaging, and insurance.

Transport law is a patchwork of treaties by mode, each with **limited carrier liability** and long lists of carrier defences. In practice, a cargo owner recovers losses from **insurance**, not from carriers. So the safest rule is: insure fully, including “gap” cover where another party’s insurance may fall short.

This lesson covers:

- Transport management: volume, cost, speed, packaging, customs, payment links
- The **conventions** by mode and what they mean for carrier liability
- **Charter parties**, **freight forwarders**, and transport **documents** (especially the **bill of lading**)
- **Sea**, **container/multimodal**, **air**, **road**, and **rail** in practice
- **Cargo insurance**: duty vs need to insure, insurable interest, open cover, ICC (A)/(B)/(C)

**Assumptions.** Cross-border B2B shipments. Incoterms® details are in Lesson 3; documentary credits in Lesson 9.

## 13.1 Transport management

| Lever | Idea | Practical move |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | Bigger, regular shipments get lower rates | Consolidate sales into one shipment; door-to-door contracts; shippers’ councils for joint bargaining |
| **Cost in the price** | Transport can be a large share of landed cost, especially for low-value goods | Choose the Incoterm deliberately — it decides who buys transport |
| **Transport as profit centre** | Sellers quoting EXW/FCA/FOB give the transport business away | A competent seller can quote CIF/CIP/DDP and earn on managing transport |
| **Speed & reliability** | Late goods lose customers, whoever is at fault; transit time ties up working capital | Fewer hand-overs (one party controls the chain) means fewer delays |
| **Inventory** | **Just-in-time** supply cuts warehousing but demands reliable transport | Match mode to stock policy |
| **Packaging** | Cheap freight modes may need costly packaging and suffer more damage | Ask the buyer early which mode it will use |
| **Customs** | Simplified procedures for air, postal, and some road/rail regimes speed clearance | Factor clearance time into mode choice |
| **Payment** | A credit calling for a **marine on-board B/L** rules out air shipment | Align transport documents with the credit (Lesson 9) |

:::tip One party, one chain
Transport economies are best when **one** party buys the whole move (EXW for the buyer, DDP for the seller): more volume with one provider and fewer hand-overs.
:::

## 13.2 Conventions and carrier liability

| Mode | Regime | Status (simplified) | Liability limit (indicative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sea** | **Hague Rules** (1924) | Widely adopted; criticised for low limits and many exemptions | Per package (old fixed amounts) |
| | **Hague-Visby Rules** (1968, amended) | Most major trading nations | **666.67 SDR per package or 2 SDR per kg**, whichever is higher |
| | **Hamburg Rules** (1978) | In force since 1992, but few major shipping nations | 835 SDR per package or 2.5 SDR per kg |
| | **Rotterdam Rules** (2008) | Door-to-door with a sea leg, e-documents — **not yet in force** | 875 SDR per package or 3 SDR per kg |
| **Air** | **Montreal Convention** (1999) | In force since 2003; most trading nations | About **26 SDR per kg** (revised periodically by ICAO) |
| | **Warsaw system** (1929 + protocols) | Where Montreal not ratified | Lower limits |
| **Rail** | **COTIF / CIM** | Europe and neighbouring countries | 17 SDR per kg |
| **Road** | **CMR** (1956) | Virtually all of Europe and beyond | 8.33 SDR per kg |
| **Multimodal** | UN Convention (1980) | **Not in force** | — |
| | **UNCTAD/ICC Rules for Multimodal Transport Documents** (1992) | Contractual rules used in FIATA and BIMCO (MULTIDOC) documents | Contractual |

*SDR = Special Drawing Right, the IMF’s basket currency (roughly USD 1.3–1.4; check the current rate).*

### Carrier liability in principle

- **Money cap** per package or per kg under every regime.
- **Carrier’s duty** (Hague / Hague-Visby): due diligence on seaworthiness, fitness of the holds, proper loading, stowage, and discharge.
- **Carrier’s defences** (Hague / Hague-Visby): perils of the sea, fire, war, strikes, acts of God; inherent defects or insufficient packing; **negligence in navigating or managing the ship** by master or crew.
- **Time bars** and **notice**: note visible damage **in writing on the delivery receipt immediately**, and notify the carrier and the insurer.

### Worked example: the liability gap

Ten pallets, 2,000 kg in total, worth **USD 80,000**, are lost at sea under Hague-Visby. Assume 1 SDR = USD 1.33 (illustrative).

| Basis | Calculation | SDR |
|---|---|---|
| Per package | 10 × 666.67 | 6,667 |
| Per kg | 2,000 × 2 | 4,000 |
| **Limit (higher of the two)** | | **6,667 ≈ USD 8,870** |

Even if the carrier is liable, the owner recovers about **11%** of the cargo’s value — and the carrier may have a defence. That gap is why cargo is insured.

## 13.3 Charter parties

| Charter type | Who runs ship and crew | Charterer’s role |
|---|---|---|
| **Voyage** | Shipowner | Hires space for one voyage |
| **Time** | Shipowner | Controls cargo bookings for a period |
| **Bareboat (demise)** | **Charterer** | Effectively operates the ship |

A **charter party** is only a **contract of carriage** between shipowner and charterer — not a receipt and not a document of title, so it cannot be endorsed like a bill of lading. Charter parties allow more freedom of contract (e.g. lower owner liability) than bills of lading governed by a convention.

Bills of lading issued **subject to a charter party** create questions for the cargo buyer:

- **Whom to sue** — shipowner or charterer? Under which contract?
- **Acceptability** — are charter-party B/Ls acceptable under the sale contract and the credit? (UCP 600 treats them separately.)
- **Incorporation** — a B/L may incorporate charter-party terms, including an **arbitration clause**, so a court case by the buyer could be dismissed in favour of arbitration. Incorporation must be clear and must not contradict the B/L or a mandatory convention.

## 13.4 Freight forwarders

A forwarder is to cargo what a travel agent is to passengers: it knows rates and routes and builds the best package — booking freight, preparing export and customs documents, arranging insurance, and paying port and terminal charges. Small exporters often ask their forwarder for costs **before** quoting a price.

| Acting as | Legal position | Liability for subcontractors |
|---|---|---|
| **Agent** | Arranges third-party services on the customer’s behalf; customer contracts with them directly | Generally **not liable** (owes duties of care and information) |
| **Principal** | Contracts directly with the customer and subcontracts; issues one invoice | Generally **liable** for subcontractors |
| **Hybrid** | Agent for some tasks, principal for others | Depends on the task |

| Specialist | Function |
|---|---|
| **Consolidator / NVOCC** | Groups small shipments into full containers; NVOCCs issue their own B/Ls on ships they do not own |
| **Multimodal transport operator (MTO)** | One-stop door-to-door logistics |
| **Customs broker** | Files declarations, pays duties; usually bonded — give clear limits on duties/fines |
| **Port / terminal agent** | Handles transfer between modes at a port or airport |
| **Air freight (IATA) agent** | Processes air cargo, may issue air waybills, often clears customs |
| **Road haulage broker** | Matches shippers with small road carriers, paid by commission |
| **Loading broker** | Agent of the **shipowner**, selling space and supervising loading |

## 13.5 Shipping documents

| Document | Purpose | Watch-out |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial invoice** | Bill for the goods; basis for customs duty in many countries | **Double invoicing** (a lower invoice for customs) is fraud with heavy penalties |
| **Inspection certificate** | Independent agency confirms quality/quantity | Often required under L/Cs |
| **Certificate of origin** | Country of production, usually chamber-certified | Needed for **preferential tariffs** |
| **Insurance certificate / policy** | Proof of cover (CIF/CIP) | A credit demanding a *policy* will not accept a *certificate* |
| **Packing list** | Weight, volume, contents, packaging per package | |
| **Export licence** | Government permit for controlled goods | Hazardous and sensitive (e.g. dual-use) goods may face quotas |
| **Consular invoice** | Official invoice form required by some importing countries | |

## 13.6 The bill of lading and other transport documents

### The marine bill of lading — three functions

1. **Control of the goods** — the lawful holder of an **order** B/L is entitled to delivery; endorsement transfers control, so goods can be **sold in transit** and banks can lend against it.
2. **Evidence of the contract of carriage** — on usual terms, as the CIF/CFR seller must provide.
3. **Receipt** — goods received in apparent good order. A **received-for-shipment** B/L becomes acceptable for documentary sales only once noted **shipped on board**. Notations of damage make it **claused** (unclean).

**For the buyer**, a clean on-board B/L shows the seller shipped apparently conforming goods, gives the right to demand delivery and to sue the carrier, and allows resale and financing. **For the seller**, it means payment as soon as goods are properly shipped. (UCP 600: the word “clean” need not actually appear on the document.)

**Problems:**

- **Goods arrive before the B/L** — common on short routes and in oil. Delivering without an original exposes the carrier, so it releases against a **letter of indemnity (LOI)**, usually backed by a bank.
- A marine B/L covers only the sea leg; door-to-door moves need a **multimodal** document.

### Other transport documents

| Document | Mode | Negotiable? | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Order B/L** | Sea | **Yes** | Security for banks; enables resale in transit |
| **Straight B/L** | Sea | No | Named consignee; shipper may change consignee until it is irrevocable — weak bank security |
| **Sea waybill** | Sea | No | Receipt + contract, **not** title; consignee collects without an original — good when goods outrun documents |
| **Multimodal transport document** | Two or more modes | Can be | May evidence receipt at an inland terminal, not “on board” |
| **Air waybill (AWB)** | Air | No | IATA form; **master AWB** from the airline, **house AWB** from the consolidator |
| **Rail consignment note** | Rail | No | CIM in Europe |
| **Road consignment note** | Road | No | CMR |

## 13.7 Sea transport in practice

### Market structure

| Type | Rates | Service |
|---|---|---|
| **Conferences** | Common published rates (cartel-like, historically exempt from competition law) | Regular scheduled service |
| **Outsiders / independent lines** | Usually cheaper | Large ones match conference quality |
| **Tramps** | Negotiated per voyage through brokers | Bulk cargo, flexible routes |

The trend is toward **individual confidential service contracts**. The EU ended conferences’ antitrust exemption in 2008; liner **alliances** (vessel-sharing agreements) now dominate container trades, with exemptions under separate rules.

### Freight and charges

- **Freight** on **weight or measure (W/M)**: the carrier charges per tonne or per cubic metre, whichever yields more (commonly 1 m³ treated as 1,000 kg).
- **Surcharges**: **BAF** (bunker/fuel adjustment) and **CAF** (currency adjustment) can change at short notice — include **price adjustment clauses** in sale contracts; also heavy-lift and long-length surcharges.
- **Freight prepaid** (seller pays, e.g. CIF) or **freight collect** (buyer pays on arrival).
- **Terminal/handling charges** are often outside freight; the Incoterm decides who pays.

### Liner terms vs Incoterms®

**Liner terms** belong to the **carriage contract** (shipper ↔ carrier) and decide whether loading/unloading costs are in the freight. **Incoterms®** belong to the **sale contract** (seller ↔ buyer). The party buying transport must make sure the liner term does not contradict the sale’s Incoterm.

| Liner term | Loading | Unloading |
|---|---|---|
| **Ex quay** | Carrier loads from anywhere alongside | Carrier lands, sorts, sometimes stores |
| **Under ship’s tackle** | Shipper brings goods under the crane | Carrier only places goods on deck |
| **Free in / free out (FIO)** | Shipper loads and stows | Consignee unstows and discharges |

**Stevedores** handle cargo and bill one party; they do not split charges — one reason Incoterms® 2010 moved FOB/CFR/CIF delivery from the “ship’s rail” to **on board**.

## 13.8 Containers and multimodal transport

- Standard boxes 8 ft wide and high, 20 or 40 ft long (10 ft less common); volume measured in **TEU** (twenty-foot equivalent units). Containers cut handling time from days to hours and reduce damage and theft.
- Always check the **internal dimensions** before packing.
- **FCL** (full container load): empty box delivered to the shipper, **stuffed**, **sealed** (a broken seal signals tampering), returned. **LCL** (less than container load): goods delivered to a terminal, consolidated with others, de-consolidated at destination.
- **Charges**: freight (often including the box), **terminal handling charges** at both ends, and **detention/demurrage** penalties if boxes are kept too long.

### Use container Incoterms®

| Port-based term | Container / multimodal equivalent |
|---|---|
| FOB | **FCA** |
| CFR | **CPT** |
| CIF | **CIP** |

Containers are handed over at the shipper’s premises or an inland terminal, so dividing risk “on board the vessel” leaves an exposed gap between hand-over and loading — and makes the seller pay costs it no longer controls. Use FCA/CPT/CIP.

## 13.9 Air transport

Air freight is roughly **1% of world trade by weight but far more by value** — perishables, urgent goods, and high-value items (jewellery, pharmaceuticals, art) move by air. Despite higher freight, air can be competitive overall:

1. **Insurance** — often about half the sea rate (e.g. ~0.3% vs ~0.6–0.7% of value)
2. **Customs duty** — sometimes assessed on gross weight; lighter packaging lowers it
3. **Packaging** — lighter and cheaper
4. **Inventory** — less warehousing, tax, insurance, and obsolescence
5. **Financing** — faster delivery means faster payment

**Practice.** Cargo flies in passenger aircraft, **combis**, or freighters. Use **FCA** (named airport terminal) — “FOB airport” was abandoned long ago. **IATA cargo agents** sell space for airlines; **consolidators** issue **house AWBs** (like NVOCCs at sea) and often offer door-to-door service. Cargo moves in **ULDs** (unit load devices). Ask for **guaranteed shipment times**. **Dangerous goods** rules (IATA DGR, updated yearly) are strictly enforced — wrong declarations bring heavy penalties.

### Chargeable weight

Carriers charge the higher of **actual** and **volumetric** weight:

| Mode | Common conversion (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| **Sea (W/M)** | 1 m³ = 1,000 kg |
| **Road** | 1 m³ ≈ 333 kg (varies by market) |
| **Air** | 6,000 cm³ = 1 kg → 1 m³ ≈ 167 kg |

### Worked example: chargeable weight

A consignment weighs **500 kg** and measures **3.2 m³**.

| Mode | Volumetric | Actual | Chargeable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sea | 3.2 m³ → 3.2 freight tonnes | 0.5 t | **3.2 freight tonnes** (charged by volume) |
| Road | 3.2 × 333 ≈ 1,066 kg | 500 kg | **1,066 kg** |
| Air | 3.2 × 167 ≈ 534 kg | 500 kg | **534 kg** |

Light, bulky goods are charged on volume — compact packaging saves money.

### Worked example: air vs sea total cost

Goods worth **USD 200,000**; inventory carrying cost **20% a year**; sea transit 35 days door-to-door vs 5 days by air (illustrative).

| Cost | Sea | Air |
|---|---|---|
| Freight | 2,500 | 12,000 |
| Insurance (0.7% vs 0.3%) | 1,400 | 600 |
| Packaging | 1,500 | 600 |
| Capital tied up in transit (200,000 × 20% × days/365) | 3,836 | 548 |
| Buffer warehousing at destination | 2,000 | 0 |
| **Total** | **11,236** | **13,748** |

Sea still wins by about USD 2,500 here — but the gap is far smaller than the freight difference (9,500) suggests. Add one lost sale from a stock-out or a higher-value product, and air can win.

## 13.10 Road and rail

| | Road | Rail |
|---|---|---|
| Strength | True **door-to-door**, no transhipment; flexible; Ro-Ro ferries | Very heavy loads, bulk ore and liquids; competitive per tonne; ideal with private sidings |
| Weakness | Limited payload (often ~40 t, national limits apply); driver costs vary by country | Fewer access points; gauge and clearance differences force transhipment |
| Law | **CMR** | **COTIF / CIM** |
| Customs transit | **TIR carnet**: sealed trucks cross TIR countries without full inspection at each border | Rail transit regimes |
| Pricing | Weight vs volume ratio (see chargeable weight) | Usually by weight, express vs slow service |

Road-rail combinations (trailers on wagons) add flexibility.

## 13.11 Cargo insurance

### Duty vs need to insure

- **Contractual duty**: only under **CIF** and **CIP** does the seller have to insure for the buyer.
- **Commercial need**: everyone at risk at some point should be insured — the Incoterm sets where risk passes.

### Why not split cover at the risk point?

Splitting cover (seller to the ship, buyer from the ship) creates **gaps**, makes it hard to prove **where** damage happened (both insurers may refuse), and costs more than one **warehouse-to-warehouse** policy.

:::caution FOB/CFR seller on open account
If the seller insures only up to loading and the goods are damaged at sea, the buyer may refuse to pay — leaving the seller with a lawsuit instead of an insurance claim. Sellers paid after arrival should insure their **contingency** interest for the whole transit.
:::

**Contingency / gap / difference-in-conditions (DIC)** insurance tops up when the other party’s cover is too small, excludes likely risks, or is placed with a local insurer that some countries require by law.

### Insurable interest

You can claim only if you had an **insurable interest** (ownership or risk) when the loss happened — generally the seller before the risk point, the buyer after. Policies can be **assigned** (e.g. CIF seller endorses the policy to the buyer).

### Open cover and floating policies

| Type | How | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Open cover / open policy** | General terms for all shipments in a period; limit renews after each voyage | Insurer issues **certificates** per shipment |
| **Floating policy** | Total sum insured reduced by each declared shipment | |
| **Single (facultative) policy** | One shipment | |

### Cost and clauses

- Indicative premiums: **air ~0.3%**, **sea ~0.6–0.7%**, long road/rail legs in difficult regions up to ~2% of value.
- **Institute Cargo Clauses**: **(A)** broadest (“all risks” minus exclusions), **(B)** and **(C)** named perils only. Even (A) excludes **war and strikes** (added by separate War and **SRCC** clauses), insufficient packing, delay, ordinary wear and tear, and carrier insolvency.
- **Incoterms®**: minimum sum insured **110%** of the contract price. Under **Incoterms® 2010 both CIF and CIP required at least (C)**; **Incoterms® 2020** raised **CIP to (A)** while CIF stays at (C).
- If the buyer expects a high margin, specify **120–130%**, (A) cover, and SRCC/war in the sale contract.

### Worked example: CIF insurance

CIF price **USD 100,000**; insured value 110% = **USD 110,000**; sea premium **0.65%** → premium **USD 715**. Adding war/SRCC at, say, 0.05% → 110,000 × 0.0005 = USD 55 more.

## Takeaways

:::tip Remember
- Tell the forwarder/carrier exactly what the **sale contract** requires
- Carrier liability is **capped** and full of defences — insure **warehouse-to-warehouse**, with gap cover where needed
- Know the regimes: **Hague-Visby**, Hamburg, (Rotterdam not in force), **Montreal**, **CMR**, **COTIF/CIM**
- **Order B/L** = control + contract + receipt; **sea waybill** = no title, fast release; **AWB** never negotiable
- Containers → **FCA / CPT / CIP**, not FOB/CFR/CIF
- Freight is charged on the higher of **actual vs volumetric** weight; watch **BAF/CAF**
- Air can win on **total cost**, not freight alone
- Only **CIF/CIP** oblige the seller to insure: 110%; Incoterms® 2020 CIP needs **(A)**
:::
