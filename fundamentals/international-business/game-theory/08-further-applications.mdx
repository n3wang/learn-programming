# 8. Further Applications: Partnerships, Standards, R&D Races, and Campaigns

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 8 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Models are simplified and all numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Set up** a continuous-strategy game: players, strategy intervals, payoff functions.
2. **Derive** best-response functions from first-order conditions and solve for Nash equilibria.
3. **Explain** free-riding / under-investment in partnerships by comparing the equilibrium to the jointly optimal choice.
4. **Analyse** how coordination incentives pull players' choices toward each other (software standards).
5. **Compute** equilibrium effort and rent dissipation in a contest (R&D races, lobbying, election campaigns).

---

## A recipe for continuous games

1. Write player $i$'s payoff $u_i(x_i, x_j)$.
2. Take the first-order condition $\partial u_i / \partial x_i = 0$ (check concavity and boundaries).
3. Solve for the **best-response function** $x_i^*(x_j)$.
4. Intersect the best-response functions: $x_1 = x_1^*(x_2)$, $x_2 = x_2^*(x_1)$.
5. Interpret: compare with the **efficient** (joint-surplus-maximising) choice.

## 8.1 Partnership: complementary efforts

A manufacturer and a distributor (or an employer and a worker) each invest effort $e_i \ge 0$. Each one's return rises with the other's effort: $u_i = e_i(1 + b\,e_j) - e_i^2$, where $b \in [0, 2)$ measures complementarity.

- **Best response:** $1 + b e_j - 2e_i = 0 \Rightarrow e_i^* = (1 + b e_j)/2$ — increasing in $e_j$ (strategic complements).
- **Equilibrium:** $e = 1/(2 - b)$.
- **Efficient effort** maximises the sum $2[e(1 + be) - e^2]$: $e^{\text{eff}} = 1/(2 - 2b)$ — strictly higher whenever $b > 0$.

Each partner ignores the benefit **her** effort gives the **other**, so both under-invest. This is the logic of **hold-up** in supplier relationships and joint ventures, and a reason for profit-sharing contracts, equity stakes, and long-term agreements.

:::info In the original Cobb–Douglas version
If output is $K^\alpha L^\beta$ and each input is useless without the other, $(0, 0)$ is also an equilibrium: if your partner invests nothing, so should you. Complementarity can create a **bad equilibrium** of mutual neglect alongside a good one.
:::

## 8.2 Coordination in software development

Developer $i$ has an ideal specification $\theta_i$ but also wants compatibility with the others. With two developers, $u_i = 100 - (x_i - \theta_i)^2 - (x_i - x_j)^2$.

- Best response: $x_i^* = (\theta_i + x_j)/2$ — the midpoint between your ideal and the other's choice.
- Equilibrium: $x_1 = (2\theta_1 + \theta_2)/3$, $x_2 = (\theta_1 + 2\theta_2)/3$. Each moves one-third of the way toward the other.
- With $n$ developers: $x_i^* = (\theta_i + \bar{x}_{-i})/2$, where $\bar{x}_{-i}$ is the average of the others' choices.

<BestResponseLab preset="software" />

The same structure describes firms choosing technical standards, countries harmonising regulations, or subsidiaries of a multinational choosing local vs global product specs.

## 8.3 Competition in research and development

Two start-ups race for market leadership worth $V$. Investing $x$ (with rival investment $y$) wins with probability $x/(x + y)$; the investment is spent regardless. Payoff: $V\frac{x}{x + y} - x$.

- **FOC:** $V\frac{y}{(x+y)^2} = 1 \Rightarrow x^*(y) = \sqrt{Vy} - y$ (rising then falling in $y$).
- **Symmetric equilibrium:** $x = y = V/4$. Each wins with probability ½ and earns $V/2 - V/4 = V/4$.
- **Rent dissipation:** together they spend $V/2$ — half the prize is burnt in the race. With $n$ firms each spends $V(n-1)/n^2$, and total spending $V(n-1)/n$ approaches the entire prize.

## 8.4 Political competition

Two candidates spend on campaigns. Each wins with probability proportional to their spending (a small third-party chance aside), and winning is worth 1. This is the same **contest** as the R&D race: in equilibrium each spends about 1/4 of the value of office, and campaign spending escalates with the stakes. Lobbying for an import licence, a tariff exemption, or a government contract has the same structure — economists call it **rent-seeking**.

## Key takeaways

- Continuous games: **FOC → best-response functions → intersection**. Then compare with the efficient benchmark.
- **Complementary efforts** ⇒ under-investment (hold-up), and sometimes a bad equilibrium at zero.
- **Coordination incentives** pull choices toward each other: equilibrium specs move part-way to the partner's ideal.
- **Contests** (R&D, lobbying, campaigns) dissipate a large share of the prize, more so with more contestants.
