# 1. Strategic Thinking: What Game Theory Studies

> **Teaching rewrite** — built from the topic outline of MIT 14.12 *Economic Applications of Game Theory* (lecture notes ch. 1, MIT OpenCourseWare). All wording, examples, and numbers here are original unless marked as a standard named game.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Explain** why, in a game, your best action depends on what you believe others will do — and on what you believe *they* believe about *you*.
2. **Apply** iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies to a payoff matrix and say which "layer" of rationality each step uses.
3. **Identify** stable outcomes (pure Nash equilibria) in coordination games and explain why a game can have more than one.
4. **Predict** how moving first, or being observed, changes the outcome (backward induction, commitment) — and why having information can *hurt* you.
5. **Use** forward induction to discard an equilibrium that relies on an implausible belief.
6. **Classify** real business situations as a Prisoners' Dilemma, a coordination game, a Hawk–Dove (Chicken) game, or an investment game.

## 1.1 A game is a multi-person decision problem

In ordinary decision theory, you choose and *nature* (luck) determines the result. In a **game**, other *decision-makers* also choose, and **your payoff depends on their choices**. Game theory is better described as **multi-person decision theory**.

A game needs four ingredients:

| Ingredient | Question it answers | Example: two exporters setting prices |
|---|---|---|
| **Players** | Who decides? | Exporter A, Exporter B |
| **Actions / strategies** | What can each do? | Hold the price, Undercut |
| **Payoffs** | How does each rank the outcomes? | Profit in each of the 4 combinations |
| **Information** | Who knows what, when? | Both choose without seeing the other |

The difficulty is **circular reasoning**. A's best price depends on what A thinks B will do. B's choice depends on what B thinks A will do — which includes what B thinks A thinks B will do… Game theory provides tools to cut through this regress.

### Reading a payoff matrix

In a **bimatrix** the row player (Player 1, blue) picks a row, the column player (Player 2, red) picks a column, and each cell lists **(Player 1's payoff, Player 2's payoff)**. Players choose **simultaneously**.

## 1.2 Reasoning with dominance

A strategy is **strictly dominated** if some other strategy gives a strictly higher payoff **no matter what the opponents do**. A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy — no belief can justify it.

Step through the game below. Each click removes one dominated strategy and tells you why.

<GameMatrixLab preset="iesds" />

Walk through the logic, and notice how each step **asks for more knowledge**:

| Step | Removed | What must be true for this step |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Player 1's **Down** (Mid beats it in every column) | Player 1 is **rational** |
| 2 | Player 2's **Right** (once Down is gone, Center beats it) | Player 2 is rational **and knows** Player 1 is rational |
| 3 | Player 1's **Up** (with Right gone, Mid beats it) | Player 1 knows that Player 2 knows that Player 1 is rational |
| 4 | Player 2's **Center** (against Mid, Left is best) | One more layer of "knows that…" |

The prediction **(Mid, Left)** therefore rests on **common knowledge of rationality**: everyone is rational, everyone knows it, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so on.

:::tip What if a player stops thinking about the other?
Suppose Player 1 is rational but believes Player 2 picks a column **uniformly at random**. Then Player 1 simply compares average payoffs: Up = (1 + 0 + 3)/3 = 4/3, Mid = (2 + 1 + 0)/3 = 1, Down = 0. Player 1 now plays **Up**, not Mid. Changing the assumptions about how others reason changes the prediction.
:::

## 1.3 Coordination: when reasoning alone is not enough

Two colleagues want to meet but cannot communicate. They do not care where — only that they meet.

<GameMatrixLab preset="coordination" />

No strategy is dominated: Café is best if the other goes to the Café, Library is best if the other goes to the Library. Dominance arguments are silent.

Instead, look for **stable profiles**: a profile where **no player wants to change her own choice, given the other's choice**. This is a **Nash equilibrium** (named after John Nash). Here both (Café, Café) and (Library, Library) are stable; the mismatches are not — each player would rather switch.

Add a **conflict of interest** and you get the classic *Battle of the Sexes*: both want to coordinate, but they disagree on *where*.

<GameMatrixLab preset="bos" />

Both matching outcomes are equilibria. Each player prefers a different one. In business, this is the structure of a **standards war** (two firms both gain from one common format, but each wants it to be *its* format) or two trading partners choosing an invoicing currency.

:::info Beliefs make the difference
Switch to the **Beliefs → choice** tab. If Player 1 thinks Player 2 goes to the Opera with probability above 1/3, Opera is Player 1's best response. Equilibrium theory is about which beliefs can be *self-fulfilling*.
:::

## 1.4 Who moves first — and who can see it

Take the same Opera/Football payoffs, but let Player 2 **observe** Player 1's choice before choosing. Use the toggle to compare.

<GameTreeLab preset="bos" />

With observation, Player 2 will match whatever Player 1 chose. Player 1 knows this, so he picks Opera and gets 2. This reasoning — **solve the last decision first, then work backwards** — is called **backward induction**. Click *Solve by backward induction* to see it.

Two lessons follow:

1. **Moving first can be an advantage.** By choosing first, Player 1 effectively **commits**. In the simultaneous game Player 2 could hope for her favourite outcome; here she gets only 1.
2. **Information can hurt the player who has it.** Player 2 is worse off *because* she can see Player 1's move — and Player 1 knows she can. She would prefer that Player 1 observe *her*. This is a recurring theme: being known to be well-informed can make you predictable.

Business parallel: a firm that **publicly commits** to a large plant or a fixed export price before rivals decide can steer the market to its preferred outcome — provided the commitment is visible and cannot be undone.

## 1.5 Forward induction: reading meaning into a choice

Now Player 1 can first **Exit** (both get 3/2) or **Play** the simultaneous Opera/Football game.

<GameTreeLab preset="exit" />

Reveal the normal form and turn on best responses. There are several equilibria, including two kinds of story:

- **Pessimistic story:** Player 1 exits because he expects (Football, Football) inside the game, which would give him only 1.
- **Optimistic story:** Player 1 plays and they coordinate on (Opera, Opera), giving him 2.

**Forward induction** argues the first story is not believable. If Player 2 is asked to play, she knows Player 1 **gave up a sure 3/2**. Football can give Player 1 at most 1, so a rational Player 1 who chose *Play* must intend Opera. Player 2 should play Opera — and anticipating that, Player 1 plays. A past choice *signals* future intentions.

## 1.6 A gallery of games you will meet again

### Prisoners' Dilemma

<GameMatrixLab preset="pd" />

Undercutting **strictly dominates** holding the price for each exporter: whatever the rival does, undercutting pays more. Both undercut and earn 1, although both holding would give 3. Individual rationality produces a collectively bad outcome. Think of **tariff wars**, **subsidy races**, or over-fishing of a shared stock.

### Hawk–Dove (Chicken)

Two firms contest a contract worth V. A Hawk insists; a Dove yields. If both insist, they fight and each pays C/2.

<GameMatrixLab preset="hawkDove" />

- If **V > C** (the prize is worth the fight), Hawk dominates — it becomes a Prisoners' Dilemma, and both fight.
- If **V < C** (fighting is ruinous), each wants to do the **opposite** of the other: two equilibria, (Hawk, Dove) and (Dove, Hawk). This is the game of **Chicken**, and of many **wars of attrition** — price wars, strikes, trade standoffs.

### Investment (coordination with risk)

<GameMatrixLab preset="investment" />

Investing pays θ if the other side also invests, and θ − c if you invest alone.

- If **θ − c > 0**, investing is dominant.
- If **θ < 0**, not investing is dominant.
- If **0 < θ < c**, both (Invest, Invest) and (Don't, Don't) are equilibria. In the *Beliefs* tab you will find that investing is a best response only if the other invests with probability at least $1 - \theta/c$. With θ = 3 and c = 4 that threshold is 1/4.

## Key takeaways

- A game = **players, strategies, payoffs, information**. Your best move depends on **beliefs** about others.
- **Strictly dominated** strategies are never played; eliminating them repeatedly needs **common knowledge of rationality**.
- **Nash equilibrium** = no player gains by deviating alone. Games can have **several** — beliefs decide which one is played.
- **Order and observability** matter: moving first can be a commitment advantage; being known to be informed can hurt.
- **Backward induction** solves sequential games from the end; **forward induction** reads intentions from past choices.
- Keep the gallery in mind: **Prisoners' Dilemma, coordination / Battle of the Sexes, Hawk–Dove, investment**.
