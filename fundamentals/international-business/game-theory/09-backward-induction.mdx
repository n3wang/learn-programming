# 9. Backward Induction: Sequential Rationality and Commitment

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 9 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Games, stories, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Solve** finite perfect-information games by backward induction.
2. **Explain** why the backward-induction outcome is always a Nash equilibrium, but some Nash equilibria rely on **non-credible threats**.
3. **Show** how removing your own options (commitment) can raise your payoff.
4. **Recognise** when ties create multiple backward-induction solutions.
5. **Derive** the Stackelberg leader–follower outcome and compare it with Cournot.

---

## 9.1 The procedure

In a game of **perfect information** every information set is a single node, so every player knows the full history when moving. **Sequential rationality** says a player chooses optimally **at every node, including those off the equilibrium path**.

**Backward induction:**

1. Go to the decision nodes whose moves lead only to terminal nodes. Pick the mover's best move there; replace the node with the resulting payoffs.
2. Repeat on the smaller tree until the root is reached.

The result is a strategy profile — a move at *every* node — and its path is the predicted play.

<GameTreeLab preset="centipede" />

In the take-or-pass game, backward induction says Player 1 takes immediately (2, 0), even though passing twice would give both players more. Experiments show many people pass at first — another reminder that common knowledge of rationality is a strong assumption.

## 9.2 Backward induction and Nash equilibrium

> **Proposition.** In a finite game of perfect information, backward induction always produces a (pure-strategy) Nash equilibrium. If no player is ever indifferent, it produces a unique one.

The reverse fails. In the entry game, the incumbent's plan "Fight if entry happens" together with the entrant's "stay Out" is a Nash equilibrium: given the threat, staying out is optimal, and given that nobody enters, the threat costs nothing. But the threat is **not credible** — once entry has happened, accommodating pays more than fighting. Backward induction discards such equilibria.

<GameTreeLab preset="entry" />

Reveal the normal form: there are three pure Nash equilibria, but only (In·Big, Accommodate) survives backward induction.

## 9.3 Commitment: the value of fewer options

In ordinary decision problems more options can never hurt. In games they can — because **others react to what you could do**.

<GameTreeLab preset="commitment" />

If the incumbent stays flexible, the entrant knows the incumbent would accommodate after entry, so it enters (incumbent gets 2). By signing a binding contract that commits it to high output, the incumbent **removes** its own ability to accommodate — and the entrant stays out (incumbent gets 4). The flexible branch contains the best outcome of all (5), but it is not reachable: flexibility invites entry.

Real commitment devices: irreversible capacity investment, long-term supply contracts, most-favoured-customer clauses, public announcements that would be costly to retract, delegating to a hawkish negotiator.

## 9.4 Multiple solutions

If a player is **indifferent** at some node, backward induction branches.

<GameTreeLab preset="tie" />

Player 2 gets 1 whether she plays x or y. If she plays x, Player 1 picks Left (2); if y, Player 1 picks Right (1). Both (Left, x) and (Right, y) are backward-induction solutions. The lab breaks ties by taking the first listed move — real analysis must consider each branch.

## 9.5 Stackelberg duopoly

Firm 1 (the **leader**) chooses its quantity first; firm 2 (the **follower**) observes it, then chooses. Demand $P = a - Q$, unit cost $c$.

1. **Follower:** best response $q_2^*(q_1) = (a - c - q_1)/2$ — the Cournot best response.
2. **Leader** anticipates it: maximise $q_1\big(a - q_1 - \tfrac{a - c - q_1}{2} - c\big) = q_1 \tfrac{a - c - q_1}{2}$ ⇒ $q_1 = (a - c)/2$.
3. Then $q_2 = (a - c)/4$, total $3(a - c)/4$, price $c + (a - c)/4$.

| | Cournot (simultaneous) | Stackelberg (leader moves first) |
|---|---|---|
| Leader output | $(a-c)/3$ | $(a-c)/2$ |
| Follower output | $(a-c)/3$ | $(a-c)/4$ |
| Leader profit | $(a-c)^2/9$ | $(a-c)^2/8$ |
| Follower profit | $(a-c)^2/9$ | $(a-c)^2/16$ |

Moving first is valuable **because it is a commitment**: the leader's large output is already sunk when the follower decides, so the follower accommodates.

## Key takeaways

- **Backward induction** = sequential rationality in perfect-information games: optimal play at every node, solved from the end.
- It always yields a Nash equilibrium, and eliminates equilibria built on **non-credible threats**.
- **Commitment** — visibly removing options — can raise your payoff by changing others' expectations.
- **Ties** can create multiple solutions.
- **Stackelberg**: the leader produces more and earns more than in Cournot; the follower is squeezed.
