# 3. Representing Games: Trees, Information, and Strategies

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 3 and Problem Sets 1–2 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Wording, game trees, stories, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **List** what a complete game description must contain: players, moves, information, payoffs — and chance (Nature).
2. **Read and draw** an extensive-form game: decision nodes, terminal nodes, information sets; tell **perfect** from **imperfect** information.
3. **Define** a strategy as a *complete contingent plan* and **count** a player's strategies from the tree.
4. **Compute** the outcome and (expected) payoffs of any strategy profile, including games with Nature.
5. **Convert** an extensive-form game into its normal (strategic) form, and explain why the reverse is not unique.
6. **Interpret** mixed strategies as other players' beliefs, and compute payoffs against them.

---

## 3.1 What a game must say

To analyse a strategic situation you need to know:

- **who** the players are,
- **which actions** each can take, and **when**,
- **what each knows** when acting,
- **how each values** every possible outcome.

There are two standard formats:

| Format | How it encodes the information | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Extensive form** (game tree) | explicitly: order of moves, information sets, payoffs at the end of each path | sequential play, timing, what is observed |
| **Normal / strategic form** (matrix) | summarised: list of strategies and the payoff of every strategy profile | simultaneous reasoning, dominance, equilibrium |

Throughout, the **structure of the game is common knowledge**: everyone knows it, knows that everyone knows it, and so on.

## 3.2 Game trees

A **tree** is a set of nodes joined by directed edges such that:

1. there is one **initial node** with no incoming edge;
2. every other node has **exactly one** incoming edge;
3. every node can be reached from the initial node by a **unique path** (no loops, no disconnected pieces).

**Terminal nodes** (no outgoing edge) are where the game ends; each carries a payoff for every player — these are vNM utilities from [lesson 2](./decision-theory). **Decision nodes** are assigned to a player, and their outgoing edges are that player's **moves**.

## 3.3 Information sets: what a player knows

An **information set** is a group of nodes such that:

1. the **same player** moves at each of them, and
2. the **same moves** are available at each of them.

When play reaches an information set, the player knows *some* node in the set was reached, but **cannot tell which**. (If the moves differed, the player could tell the nodes apart by looking at the menu.) The initial node is always alone. A game has **perfect information** if every information set contains one node.

Compare the two versions of matching pennies — the only difference is one dashed loop:

<GameTreeLab preset="pennies" />

| | Perfect information | Simultaneous |
|---|---|---|
| Player 2's information sets | 2 (after H, after T) | 1 |
| Player 2's strategies | 4: HH, HT, TH, TT | 2: H, T |
| What it models | 2 moves after seeing the coin | neither sees the other |

In the perfect-information version Player 2 simply plays **HT** ("copy the coin") and always wins. Simultaneously, no pure strategy is safe — that is why the game needs mixed strategies (section 3.7).

## 3.4 Strategies: complete contingent plans

A **strategy** of player $i$ assigns a move to **every** information set of player $i$ — including those the strategy itself prevents from being reached.

Three rules:

1. A move for **every** information set (otherwise we could not say what she would do if it were reached).
2. The move must be **available** at that information set.
3. The **same** move at every node of an information set (she cannot tell them apart).

So the number of strategies is a **product**: $|S_i| = \prod_{h \in H_i} (\text{number of moves at } h)$.

<GameTreeLab preset="entry" />

The entrant has 4 strategies — Out·Big, Out·Small, In·Big, In·Small — even though after **Out** the plant-size choice is never reached. Why bother? Because whether *staying out* is sensible depends on what the entrant *would* do after entering, and the incumbent's best reply depends on it too. Reveal the normal form, turn on best responses, and notice the equilibrium in which the incumbent "threatens" to fight. Backward induction rejects that threat — a theme for later chapters (subgame perfection).

## 3.5 Nature: modelling chance

Uncertainty that no player controls — weather, a lab result, whether a product launch slips — is modelled by a fictitious player, **Nature**. Nature has no payoffs; at each of its nodes we specify **probabilities** for the branches. With Nature in the tree, a strategy profile produces a **lottery** over terminal nodes, and the payoff of the profile is the **expected** payoff.

<GameTreeLab preset="nature" />

For example, (Left, Left) gives (4, 1) with probability 1/2 and (2, 2) with probability 1/2, so its payoff is $(\tfrac12 \cdot 4 + \tfrac12 \cdot 2,\ \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 2) = (3, \tfrac32)$.

### Case study: insider trading and the lemons problem

A chip-maker's new product is either **on track** or **delayed** (probability 1/2 each). The **executive** knows which, and decides whether to offer her share at price 6. The **investor** sees only whether a sale is offered.

| | On track | Delayed |
|---|---|---|
| Trade at 6 | Exec 6, Investor 10 − 6 = 4 | Exec 6, Investor 5 − 6 = −1 |
| No trade | Exec keeps a share worth 8 to her, Investor 0 | Exec keeps a share worth 4 to her, Investor 0 |

<GameTreeLab preset="insider" />

Explore the normal form. The executive's plan **Keep if on track, Sell if delayed** (Keep·Sell) is **weakly dominant**: selling helps her only when the news is bad. The investor anticipates this; against Keep·Sell, buying means paying 6 for a share worth 5. In every pure equilibrium the investor **passes** — **no trade happens**, even though trade at 6 would benefit both sides when the product is on track (the investor values it at 10, the executive at 8). Private information destroys gains from trade: this is the **lemons problem** that also haunts used-equipment markets and export credit.

## 3.6 From extensive form to normal form

The **normal form** of a game is the list $G = (S_1, \dots, S_n; u_1, \dots, u_n)$: each player's strategy set and each player's (expected) payoff for every strategy profile. To build it from a tree:

1. List each player's strategies (complete plans).
2. For every profile, follow the path (averaging over Nature's moves).
3. Record the payoff vector at the end (or its expectation).

Every extensive form has **one** normal form (up to renaming strategies). The reverse is **not** true: many trees produce the same matrix. Any matrix can be drawn as a simultaneous-move tree — and the perfect-information matching-pennies matrix (2 × 4) can also be drawn as a tree in which **Player 2 moves first**, choosing among HH, HT, TH, TT, and Player 1 moves without seeing it.

## 3.7 Mixed strategies: uncertainty about what others do

A **mixed strategy** $\sigma_i$ is a probability distribution over player $i$'s pure strategies: $0 \le \sigma_i(s) \le 1$ and $\sum_s \sigma_i(s) = 1$.

Interpretations range from deliberate randomisation (a tax authority choosing whom to audit) to a population of players using different strategies. In this course the main reading is: **$\sigma_i$ describes what the *other* players believe** about $i$'s choice. A best response to a mixed strategy is just an expected-utility maximisation from [lesson 2](./decision-theory).

## 3.8 Practice problem: guessing the other coin

Two prisoners, Alice and Bob, are in separate cells. The warden flips a fair coin in front of Alice; she must **guess Bob's coin**. Then he flips a fair coin in front of Bob; Bob must **guess Alice's coin**. If **at least one** guess is right, both are released (utility 1); otherwise neither is (utility 0).

Before playing, think about the representation:

- **Extensive form:** Nature flips Alice's coin → Alice guesses (2 information sets: she saw H or T) → Nature flips Bob's coin → Bob guesses (2 information sets: he saw H or T; he does **not** see Alice's coin or guess).
- **Strategies:** each prisoner's strategy maps the coin they see to a guess: 2 × 2 = **4 strategies** each — "say what I see", "say the opposite", "always Heads", "always Tails".
- **Strategic form:** a 4 × 4 matrix of release probabilities.

## Key takeaways

- A game specifies **players, moves, information, payoffs** (plus Nature's probabilities); its structure is **common knowledge**.
- **Information sets** capture what a player cannot distinguish; one dashed loop can turn a trivial game into a hard one.
- A **strategy** is a **complete contingent plan** — a move at every information set, reached or not. Count strategies by **multiplying** moves across information sets.
- With Nature, a profile yields a **lottery**; payoffs are **expected** payoffs.
- Every tree has one **normal form**; a normal form has many trees.
- A **mixed strategy** is a probability distribution over pure strategies — best read as others' **beliefs**.
