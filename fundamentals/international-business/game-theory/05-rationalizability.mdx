# 5. Rationalizability: What Common Knowledge of Rationality Predicts

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 5 and Problem Set 2 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Wording, games, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Define** rationalizable strategies as those that survive iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (with mixed dominators).
2. **Explain** why rationalizability is exactly what common knowledge of rationality implies — no more, no less.
3. **Carry out** the elimination in finite games, including rounds that require mixtures.
4. **Solve** the beauty-contest game and explain why real people rarely play the rationalizable answer.
5. **Apply** iterated elimination to games with continuous strategies (Cournot, coordination of specifications) using best-response functions.

## 5.1 Definition and illustration

Lesson 4 showed that a rational player never uses a strictly dominated strategy. If players also **know** that others are rational, they can delete those strategies from the game and look again; if they **know that others know**, they can delete again, and so on.

**Rationalizability procedure**

1. Delete every strategy that is strictly dominated (by a pure *or mixed* strategy).
2. In the smaller game, delete every strategy that is now strictly dominated.
3. Repeat until nothing more can be deleted. What survives is the set of **rationalizable** strategies.

> **Theorem.** If it is common knowledge that every player is rational (and the game is common knowledge), then every player plays a rationalizable strategy. Conversely, every rationalizable strategy can be justified by a chain of beliefs consistent with common knowledge of rationality.

Two facts make the procedure practical:

- **Order does not matter.** Deleting strictly dominated strategies in any order (or several at once) ends in the same set.
- **Never a best response = strictly dominated** (lesson 4), so each round can equally be phrased as "delete strategies that are not a best response to any belief about what survives".

## 5.2 Example: the beauty contest

Everyone picks a number in [0, 100]. The winner is whoever is **closest to k times the average** (say k = 2/3).

- The average cannot exceed 100, so **any guess above 66.7 is dominated** by 66.7.
- If everyone knows that, the average is at most 66.7, so guesses above 44.4 are dominated…
- Repeating, the only **rationalizable** guess is **0**.

<BeautyContestLab />

In real experiments, average guesses land around 20–35 in first plays: many people stop after one or two rounds of reasoning ("level-k" thinking). The best guess against **real** opponents is not the rationalizable one — common knowledge of rationality is a strong assumption. (The name comes from Keynes, who compared investing to guessing which faces *other judges* will find prettiest.)

### A two-player version with ideal points

Two players choose $x_1, x_2 \in [0, 100]$ with $u_i = -\tfrac12(x_i - \theta_i)^2 - \tfrac12(x_i - x_j)^2$: each wants to be near her own target $\theta_i$ **and** near the other. The best response is the midpoint, $x_i^*(x_j) = (\theta_i + x_j)/2$. Iterated elimination shrinks each player's interval by half every round, so only one strategy survives: $x_1 = (2\theta_1 + \theta_2)/3$, $x_2 = (\theta_1 + 2\theta_2)/3$. ## 5.3 Continuous strategies: Cournot duopoly

Two firms choose quantities $q_1, q_2 \ge 0$; price is $P = a - q_1 - q_2$; unit cost $c$. Firm 1 maximises $q_1(a - q_1 - q_2 - c)$, so its best response is

$$
q_1^*(q_2) = \max\left\{0,\ \frac{a - c - q_2}{2}\right\}.
$$

Iterated elimination:

| Round | Surviving quantities for each firm | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $[0, a - c]$ | more would make price below cost |
| 1 | $[0, (a - c)/2]$ | nothing above the monopoly quantity is ever a best response |
| 2 | $[(a - c)/4, (a - c)/2]$ | the rival produces at most $(a-c)/2$, so you produce at least $(a-c)/4$ |
| 3 | $[(a - c)/4, 3(a - c)/8]$ | … |
| ∞ | $\{(a - c)/3\}$ | the intervals shrink to a single point |

<BestResponseLab preset="cournot" />

With two firms, **rationalizability alone pins down the outcome** — the same point where the best-response curves cross. (In lesson 7 you will see that with three or more firms this is no longer true.)

## Key takeaways

- **Rationalizable** = survives iterated deletion of strictly dominated (incl. mixed) strategies. It is exactly what **common knowledge of rationality** delivers.
- The order of deletion does not matter; mixtures may be needed to delete a strategy.
- Rationalizability can be **sharp** (beauty contest, Cournot duopoly, ideal-point coordination) or **weak** (coordination games leave several outcomes).
- Real players often stop after a few levels of reasoning — the model's prediction depends on how much rationality is truly common knowledge.
