# 7. Imperfect Competition: Cournot, Bertrand, and Search

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 7 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Wording, examples, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Derive** best-response functions and the Nash equilibrium of Cournot (quantity) competition with two or $n$ firms.
2. **Explain** how price and profits move from monopoly toward the competitive level as the number of Cournot firms grows.
3. **Show** that Bertrand (price) competition with identical products drives price to marginal cost with just two firms — and why differentiation changes that.
4. **Contrast** strategic substitutes (quantities) with strategic complements (prices).
5. **Explain** how even tiny search costs can let firms charge monopoly prices (the search-cost paradox).

## 7.1 Cournot (quantity) competition

Firms choose **how much to produce**; the market price adjusts to clear the total. With inverse demand $P = a - Q$ and unit cost $c$, firm $i$ earns $\pi_i = q_i(a - q_i - q_{-i} - c)$, where $q_{-i}$ is the total output of the others.

**Best response.** Setting $\partial \pi_i / \partial q_i = a - 2q_i - q_{-i} - c = 0$:

$$
q_i^*(q_{-i}) = \max\left\{0, \frac{a - c - q_{-i}}{2}\right\}.
$$

More output from rivals lowers your best output — quantities are **strategic substitutes** (downward-sloping best responses).

### Duopoly equilibrium

Solve $q_1 = (a - c - q_2)/2$ and $q_2 = (a - c - q_1)/2$: $q_1 = q_2 = (a - c)/3$, price $P = (a + 2c)/3$, each profit $(a - c)^2/9$. As lesson 5 showed, iterated elimination reaches the same point.

### Oligopoly: n firms

In a symmetric equilibrium each firm produces $q$ with $q = (a - c - (n-1)q)/2$, so

$$
q = \frac{a - c}{n + 1}, \qquad Q = \frac{n(a - c)}{n + 1}, \qquad P = \frac{a + nc}{n + 1} \xrightarrow{n \to \infty} c.
$$

With one firm this is the monopoly; as $n$ grows the price approaches cost. Each firm ignores the **negative effect of its output on rivals' revenue**, so together they produce more than a cartel would.

:::info Rationalizability with three or more firms
With $n \ge 3$ the elimination argument stalls: if two rivals together might produce anything in $[0, a - c]$, every $q_i \in [0, (a - c)/2]$ is a best response to some belief. Only the equilibrium assumption — correct beliefs — delivers the $(a - c)/(n + 1)$ prediction.
:::

## 7.2 Bertrand (price) competition

Now firms choose **prices**; consumers buy from the cheapest (splitting equally at a tie). Identical products, unit cost $c$.

**Theorem.** With two or more firms, the only Nash equilibrium has **price = marginal cost** and zero profit.

Reasoning: if the lowest price is above $c$, a firm charging it (or a rival) can undercut slightly and capture the whole market — almost doubling its profit. If one firm prices at $c$ and the other above, the low firm can raise its price a little and still win. Only $p_1 = p_2 = c$ survives. Two firms are enough for the competitive outcome: the **Bertrand paradox**.

With **discrete prices** (e.g. whole cents), the argument works through iterated dominance: the highest prices are dominated by slightly lower ones, and equilibrium ends at the lowest price above cost (or at cost).

### Differentiated products

If products are imperfect substitutes, demand for firm $i$ is, say, $a - p_i + b\,p_j$. Now a higher rival price raises your best price — prices are **strategic complements** (upward-sloping best responses) — and equilibrium prices stay above cost.

## 7.3 Price competition with search costs

Bertrand assumes consumers know all prices. Suppose instead a buyer (value 6 for one unit) must pay a small cost $s$ to **check** prices. Firms choose a price of 3 or 5; the buyer simultaneously decides whether to check. If she checks, she buys from the cheaper firm; if not (or if prices tie), she picks a firm at random.

- If the buyer **does not check**, each firm sells with probability ½ whatever it charges, so each prefers the **high price** 5 (5/2 > 3/2).
- If both firms charge 5, checking reveals nothing useful and costs $s$, so the buyer **does not check**.
- So (5, 5, don't check) is an equilibrium. Can (3, 3) be one? If the buyer does not check, either firm gains by raising to 5; if she does check, she would have wasted $s$ since prices tie — so she wouldn't. **The only pure equilibrium is the monopoly price**, no matter how small $s > 0$ is.

A tiny friction overturns the Bertrand paradox. Price-comparison sites, price-transparency rules, and "price-match guarantees" all change this search game.

## Key takeaways

- **Cournot**: best responses slope down; equilibrium output $(a-c)/(n+1)$ per firm; price falls toward cost as $n$ grows.
- **Bertrand** with identical products: price = marginal cost with only two firms. Differentiation (strategic complements) keeps prices above cost.
- How firms compete — **quantities or prices** — matters as much as how many there are.
- **Search costs**, however small, can restore monopoly pricing.
