## 11.1 Subgames

Backward induction needs perfect information. When some moves are simultaneous, we instead solve **subgames** — self-contained parts of the tree.

A **subgame** is the part of the tree that starts at a single node $x$ and includes everything after it, such that:

1. $x$ is alone in its information set, and
2. no information set is cut: if a node is in the subgame, so is every node in its information set.

The whole game is always a subgame of itself. A subgame can be **played as a game on its own** — everyone knows they are in it.

**Definition.** A strategy profile is a **subgame-perfect equilibrium** if it induces a Nash equilibrium **in every subgame**.

In a perfect-information game every node starts a subgame, so SPE = backward induction. In general: **solve the last subgames, replace them by their equilibrium payoffs, and repeat.**

### Example: entry with post-entry competition

After entry, the entrant (pricing Soft or Aggressive) and the incumbent (Fight or Accommodate) choose **simultaneously** — that stage is a subgame.

<GameTreeLab preset="entrySub" />

1. **Solve the post-entry subgame.** Accommodate is dominant for the incumbent (2 > 0 against Soft, 1 > −1 against Aggressive). Against Accommodate, the entrant prefers Soft (2 > 1). Subgame equilibrium: (Soft, Accommodate), payoffs (2, 2).
2. **Replace it** by (2, 2). The entrant compares In (2) with Out (0): **In**.
3. **SPE:** entrant plays In and Soft; incumbent Accommodates.

Reveal the normal form: (Out, Fight)-type profiles are Nash equilibria too, but they ask the incumbent to Fight in a subgame where Fight is not a best response — they are not subgame perfect.

## 11.2 The single-deviation principle

Checking every possible deviation in long (or infinite) games is impractical. Fortunately:

> **Single-deviation principle.** In a multistage game that is *continuous at infinity* (far-future payoffs matter little, e.g. with discounting), a strategy profile is subgame perfect **if and only if** no player can gain by deviating at **one** information set only and following the strategy afterwards.

So to verify an SPE you only ask, at every history: "would changing **this one move**, then reverting to the plan, help?" This is how we will check cooperation in repeated games (lesson 12) and cartels (lesson 13).

## 11.3 Application: infinite-horizon bargaining

Return to alternating offers over a pie of 1 — now **with no deadline**. Player 1 offers in odd rounds, Player 2 in even rounds, with discount factors $\delta_1, \delta_2 < 1$.

**Claim (Rubinstein).** There is a unique SPE: each player, when proposing, offers the other exactly what the other would get by rejecting and proposing next round; the offer is accepted at once.

Look for a **stationary** solution: proposer 1 always keeps $x$, proposer 2 always keeps $y$.

- Player 2 accepts Player 1's offer iff $1 - x \ge \delta_2 y$ ⇒ in equilibrium $1 - x = \delta_2 y$.
- Player 1 accepts Player 2's offer iff $1 - y \ge \delta_1 x$ ⇒ $1 - y = \delta_1 x$.

Solving:

$$
x = \frac{1 - \delta_2}{1 - \delta_1\delta_2}, \qquad 1 - x = \frac{\delta_2(1 - \delta_1)}{1 - \delta_1\delta_2}.
$$

The single-deviation principle confirms it: rejecting an equilibrium offer and countering next period leaves the deviator no better off. With equal patience $\delta$, the first mover gets $1/(1 + \delta)$ — slightly more than half, approaching ½ as offers become frequent ($\delta \to 1$).

## Key takeaways

- A **subgame** starts at a singleton information set and never splits an information set.
- **SPE** = Nash equilibrium in every subgame. Solve from the last subgames back; it removes non-credible threats even with simultaneous moves.
- The **single-deviation principle** makes checking SPE tractable: test one-shot deviations only.
- **Rubinstein bargaining**: immediate agreement, Player 1 gets $(1 - \delta_2)/(1 - \delta_1\delta_2)$ — patience is bargaining power.
