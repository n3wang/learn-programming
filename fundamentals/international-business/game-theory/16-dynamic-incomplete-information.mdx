# 16. Dynamic Games with Incomplete Information: Beliefs, Signaling, and Reputation

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 16 (MIT OpenCourseWare). The Beer–Quiche game is a standard named example; the other stories and all numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Explain** why dynamic games with private information need **beliefs** at information sets, and state sequential rationality and consistency (Bayes' rule).
2. **Find** pooling and separating equilibria of a signaling game and explain the role of **off-path beliefs**.
3. **Apply** Bayes' rule to update beliefs after an observed action.
4. **Show** how a small chance of a "committed" type can make a normal player build a **reputation**.
5. **Explain** why a seller without commitment cuts prices over time when the buyer's value is private (bargaining with incomplete information).

## 16.1 Sequential equilibrium

When a player moves at an information set with several nodes, she must form a **belief** — a probability over those nodes — to evaluate her moves. A **sequential (perfect Bayesian) equilibrium** is a pair (strategies, beliefs) such that:

1. **Sequential rationality:** at every information set, the mover's strategy is optimal given her beliefs and the others' strategies.
2. **Consistency:** beliefs are derived from the strategies by **Bayes' rule** wherever possible (on the equilibrium path). At information sets that are never reached, beliefs are not pinned down by Bayes' rule (sequential equilibrium adds a limiting-consistency requirement).

The extra flexibility off the path is where most of the action is: equilibria can be **supported by what players believe about deviations**.

**Bayes' rule.** If type $H$ has prior $\mu$ and sends a signal with probability $\sigma_H$, while type $L$ sends it with $\sigma_L$:

$$
\Pr(H \mid \text{signal}) = \frac{\mu\,\sigma_H}{\mu\,\sigma_H + (1 - \mu)\,\sigma_L}.
$$

## 16.2 Beer or Quiche

The Sender is **Strong** (prob. 0.9) or **Weak** (0.1). He chooses breakfast — Beer or Quiche. The Receiver sees the breakfast (not the type) and decides whether to duel. The Sender gets +1 for his preferred breakfast (Strong likes beer, Weak likes quiche) and +2 if there is no duel. The Receiver wants to duel only the Weak type.

<GameTreeLab preset="beerQuiche" />

**Pooling on Beer.** Both types drink beer.

- On the path, Bayes' rule gives $\Pr(\text{Weak} \mid \text{Beer}) = 0.1$, so the Receiver does not duel (0.9 > 0.1).
- Quiche is off the path. Suppose the Receiver believes a quiche-eater is Weak with probability ≥ ½, so she would duel after quiche.
- Strong: beer and no duel = 3, vs quiche and duel = 0 → stays. Weak: beer and no duel = 2, vs quiche and duel = 1 → stays. ✓ An equilibrium.

**Pooling on Quiche** can also be supported — by the belief that a beer-drinker is likely Weak. But that belief is odd: the Weak type could never gain from switching to beer (at best 2 < 3), while the Strong type could. Refinements such as the **intuitive criterion** eliminate it by requiring off-path beliefs to put weight on types who *could* benefit from the deviation.

## 16.3 Reputation formation

Complete-information backward induction can be fragile. Suppose an exporter sells to a buyer for two periods. A **normal** exporter would cut quality in the last period (no future to protect). But with prior probability μ the exporter is an **honest** type who always delivers quality. The buyer keeps ordering only if she believes quality will be delivered with probability at least 2/3.

- If the normal exporter always delivered in period 1, delivery would reveal nothing: the posterior on "honest" stays at μ. With μ = 0.4 < 2/3, the buyer would stop ordering — so the normal type would have no reason to deliver. Contradiction.
- If the normal type never delivered, a delivery would reveal "honest" (posterior 1), and the buyer would order again — so the normal type would want to deliver. Contradiction.
- So the normal type **mixes**: it delivers with probability σ that makes the buyer exactly indifferent after a delivery: $\frac{0.4}{0.4 + 0.6\sigma} = \frac23 \Rightarrow \sigma = \frac13$.

A small chance of being a committed type lets normal players **build a reputation** by imitating it — the logic behind brand investment, warranties, and why new suppliers often over-deliver early.

## 16.4 Bargaining with incomplete information

A seller faces a buyer whose value is **10** (prob. ½) or **4** (prob. ½); only the buyer knows. With one take-it-or-leave-it offer, the seller asks 10 (expected revenue 5 > 4) and loses the low-value buyer.

With **two periods** (discount δ), the seller is tempted to cut the price to 4 after a rejection. Anticipating that, the high-value buyer will wait unless the first price leaves him as well off as waiting: $10 - p_1 = \delta(10 - 4)$ ⇒ $p_1 = 10 - 6\delta$. Lack of **commitment** not to cut prices erodes the seller's power — taken to the limit of frequent offers, prices fall almost immediately to the low value (the **Coase conjecture**). Durable-goods makers fight this with rentals, "lowest price guarantees", and limited editions.

## Key takeaways

- Dynamic games with private information need **beliefs**; equilibrium = **sequential rationality + Bayes-consistent beliefs**.
- **Off-path beliefs** can support many equilibria; refinements (intuitive criterion) discard implausible ones.
- **Signaling** works when the signal is cheaper for the type that wants to be recognised.
- A small probability of a committed type creates **reputation** effects that overturn backward-induction logic.
- Without commitment, a seller facing private values **cuts prices over time** (Coase conjecture).
