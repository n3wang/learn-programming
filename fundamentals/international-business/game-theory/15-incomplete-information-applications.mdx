# 15. Auctions and Trade with Private Information

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 15 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Derivations are restated in original wording; numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Solve** a Cournot duopoly in which one firm's cost is private information.
2. **Derive** the symmetric linear equilibrium of a first-price auction with uniform values and explain **bid shading**.
3. **State** revenue equivalence for first- and second-price auctions and check it numerically.
4. **Analyse** a double auction between a buyer and a seller with private values and explain why some efficient trades fail.
5. **Explain** why private information makes coordination (joint investment) fragile.

## 15.1 Cournot duopoly with incomplete information

Demand $P = a - q_1 - q_2$. Firm 1's unit cost $c$ is common knowledge. Firm 2's cost $c_2$ is its private information: $c_H$ with probability θ, $c_L$ otherwise.

- **Firm 2, each type:** $q_2(c_2) = (a - c_2 - q_1)/2$. The high-cost type produces less.
- **Firm 1** does not know which type it faces, so it best-responds to the **expected** rival output: $q_1 = (a - c - \mathbb{E}[q_2])/2$.
- Solving: $q_1 = \dfrac{a - 2c + \mathbb{E}[c_2]}{3}$, and $q_2(c_2) = \dfrac{a - c_2 - q_1}{2}$.

Compared with complete information, the **low-cost** type of firm 2 produces less than it would if firm 1 knew its cost (firm 1 is producing "as if" facing an average rival), and the **high-cost** type produces more.

## 15.2 First-price auction

Two bidders with values $v_1, v_2$ independent and uniform on [0, 1]. Highest bid wins and **pays its own bid**.

**Guess a linear equilibrium** $b(v) = kv$. Bidder 1 with value $v$ bidding $b$ wins if $k v_2 < b$, i.e. with probability $b/k$. She maximises

$$
(v - b)\,\frac{b}{k} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v - 2b = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad b = \frac{v}{2}.
$$

So $k = 1/2$: each bidder **shades** her bid to half her value. With $n$ bidders the same logic gives $b(v) = \frac{n-1}{n}\,v$ — more competition, less shading.

**Revenue equivalence.** The expected price is the expected highest bid: $\frac{n-1}{n}\,\mathbb{E}[v_{(1)}] = \frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1} = \frac{n-1}{n+1}$ — exactly the expected **second-highest value**, which is what a second-price auction (truthful bids) collects. Bidders shade by just enough to offset paying their own bid.

## 15.3 Double auction

A seller (value $v_s$) and a buyer (value $v_b$), values private and uniform on [0, 1], simultaneously name an ask $p_s$ and a bid $p_b$. If $p_b \ge p_s$ they trade at $(p_b + p_s)/2$.

- **Many equilibria**: e.g. a **one-price** equilibrium — both name the same $X$ whenever trade at $X$ is acceptable to them.
- **Linear equilibrium:** $p_b = \tfrac23 v_b + \tfrac1{12}$ and $p_s = \tfrac23 v_s + \tfrac14$. Trade happens iff $v_b \ge v_s + \tfrac14$.

Each side **shades** to get a better midpoint price — the buyer understates, the seller overstates — so trades with a small gain ($0 < v_b - v_s < \tfrac14$) are lost. This is an instance of a deep result (Myerson–Satterthwaite): with two-sided private information **no** bargaining procedure realises every efficient trade.

## 15.4 Investment in a joint project

Two partners each decide whether to invest in a project that succeeds only if both invest, and each has private information about her own cost or return. Equilibria take a **threshold** form: invest iff your private signal is good enough. Each partner must also guess the other's signal, and the other's guess of hers… When information is noisy, even partners who would both like to invest may fail to coordinate. The right institutions (communication, sequential commitments, contracts that pay off in the "only one invested" state) help secure coordination.

## Key takeaways

- With private costs, firms best-respond to **expected** rival behaviour; each type adjusts its own output.
- **First-price auctions**: bid $\frac{n-1}{n}v$ (uniform values) — shading falls with competition.
- **Revenue equivalence**: first- and second-price auctions raise the same expected revenue (under these assumptions).
- **Double auctions** with private values lose some efficient trades; no mechanism can avoid that entirely.
- Private information makes **coordination** harder.
