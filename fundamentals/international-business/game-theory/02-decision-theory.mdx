# 2. Decision Theory: Preferences, Expected Utility, and Risk

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 2 and Problem Set 1 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Wording, stories, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Test** whether a relation is a *preference relation* (complete and transitive) and explain what goes wrong when it is not.
2. **Distinguish** ordinal utility (only the ranking matters) from cardinal vNM utility (the numbers matter up to $au + b$ with $a > 0$).
3. **Model** a choice under uncertainty as a choice between **lotteries** and compute expected utility.
4. **Recover** payoff numbers from stated indifferences — the step that turns verbal preferences into a game's payoffs.
5. **Link** the shape of a utility function to risk aversion; compute **certainty equivalents**, **risk premia**, and the range of mutually acceptable **insurance premiums**.
6. **Explain** why pooling independent risks creates value, and why correlation destroys that value.

## 2.1 Preferences: the raw material of choice

Start with a set $X$ of **alternatives** that are **mutually exclusive** (you get exactly one) and **exhaustive** (you always get one). Modelling matters: if you can order coffee, tea, both, or neither, the alternatives are *four* bundles, not two drinks.

Write $x \succeq y$ for "$x$ is at least as good as $y$". From it we define strict preference ($x \succ y$: $x \succeq y$ but not $y \succeq x$) and indifference ($x \sim y$: both hold).

A relation $\succeq$ is a **preference relation** if it is:

- **Complete** — for every $x, y$: $x \succeq y$ or $y \succeq x$ (you can always compare).
- **Transitive** — $x \succeq y$ and $y \succeq z$ imply $x \succeq z$ (no cycles).

:::info Why transitivity matters — the money pump
Suppose a buyer strictly prefers supplier A to B, B to C, and C to A. Offer to swap her current contract with C for B at a small fee; then B for A for a fee; then A for C for a fee… She pays at every step and ends where she started. Intransitive preferences can be exploited without limit.
:::

## 2.2 Utility as a scoreboard (ordinal)

A function $u: X \to \mathbb{R}$ **represents** $\succeq$ if $x \succeq y \iff u(x) \ge u(y)$. The player then behaves *as if* maximising $u$.

**Theorem (finite X).** A relation can be represented by a utility function **if and only if** it is complete and transitive. If $u$ represents it, so does $f \circ u$ for **any strictly increasing** $f$.

Under certainty only the **ranking** carries information: $u$, $2u + 7$, $u^3$, and $e^u$ all describe the same choices. Such utilities are **ordinal**.

## 2.3 Choice under uncertainty: lotteries

Real decisions — and every game, because you do not know what the other player will do — involve uncertainty. Let $Z$ be a finite set of **prizes** (outcomes). A **lottery** $p$ assigns a probability $p(z)$ to each prize, with $\sum_z p(z) = 1$.

**Expected utility.** A preference over lotteries has a **von Neumann–Morgenstern (vNM)** representation if there is $u: Z \to \mathbb{R}$ with

$$
p \succeq q \iff U(p) = \sum_{z \in Z} u(z)\,p(z) \;\ge\; \sum_{z \in Z} u(z)\,q(z) = U(q).
$$

The player acts as if maximising the **expected value of $u$**.

### Worked example: to study or not to study

A student in a pass/fail course chooses whether to study. She passes with probability $p_S$ if she studies and $p_N$ if she does not ($0 < p_N < p_S < 1$).

- **Prizes:** $Z$ has four elements: pass & studied, pass & didn't, fail & studied, fail & didn't.
- **Utility (one valid choice):** $u = 10\cdot[\text{pass}] - c\cdot[\text{studied}]$, where $c > 0$ is the effort cost.
- **Lotteries induced:** studying gives (pass & studied) with prob $p_S$, (fail & studied) with prob $1 - p_S$. Not studying gives (pass & didn't) with $p_N$, (fail & didn't) with $1 - p_N$.
- **Expected utilities:** $U(\text{study}) = 10p_S - c$, $U(\text{not}) = 10p_N$.
- **Decision:** study iff $10(p_S - p_N) > c$; indifferent at equality.

:::warning A tempting fallacy
A friend argues: "Whatever your chance of passing is, you'd rather have that chance *without* the effort. So don't study." The error: the passing probability is **not fixed** — studying *changes which lottery you face*. Comparing "same lottery, with and without effort" compares options that are not on the menu.
:::

## 2.4 The axioms behind expected utility

Expected-utility maximisation is not an assumption pulled from the air — it follows from three axioms on preferences over lotteries:

1. **Completeness & transitivity** (as in 2.1).
2. **Independence.** For any lotteries $p, q, r$ and $a \in (0, 1]$: $ap + (1-a)r \succ aq + (1-a)r \iff p \succ q$. Mixing both options with the same third lottery should not flip the ranking — a kind of *dynamic consistency*: if a coin decides first whether you get $r$, your ranking of $p$ vs $q$ should not depend on it.
3. **Continuity.** No prize is infinitely good or bad: a small enough chance of a worse lottery can always be compensated.

**Theorem (von Neumann–Morgenstern).** $\succeq$ has an expected-utility representation **iff** it satisfies these axioms. Moreover $u$ and $\tilde u$ represent the same preferences **iff** $\tilde u = au + b$ for some $a > 0$ and $b$.

So vNM utility is **cardinal**: you may rescale ($a > 0$) and shift ($b$), but **not** bend it. Under certainty $\sqrt{u}$ and $u$ are the same person; under uncertainty they are different people.

Geometrically (three prizes): independence makes indifference curves in the probability triangle **straight and parallel** — which is exactly what a linear function $u_1 p(z_1) + u_2 p(z_2)$ produces.

## 2.5 From beliefs to game payoffs

In a game, a player's strategies are lotteries over outcomes: if Alice plays T and believes Bob plays L with probability $q$, she gets outcome (T, L) with probability $q$ and (T, R) with $1 - q$. Thanks to vNM, we only need **one number per outcome** — the payoff $u_A(s_A, s_B)$ — not a full ranking of all lotteries for every belief.

**Calibrating from behaviour.** Suppose Alice is indifferent between (B, L) and (B, R), and she prefers T exactly when $q > 1/4$. Normalise $u_A(B, \cdot) = 0$. Then T must satisfy $\tfrac14 u_A(T,L) + \tfrac34 u_A(T,R) = 0$ with $u_A(T,L) > u_A(T,R)$, i.e. $u_A(T,L) = -3\,u_A(T,R) > 0$. One valid choice: $u_A(T,L) = 3$, $u_A(T,R) = -1$. ## 2.6 Attitudes toward risk

For **money** prizes, the shape of $u$ encodes risk attitude. A **fair gamble** has expected value 0.

| Attitude | Definition | Shape of u |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk neutral** | indifferent to every fair gamble | linear: $u(x) = ax + b$ |
| **Strictly risk averse** | rejects every non-trivial fair gamble | strictly **concave** |
| **Risk seeking** | accepts fair gambles | **convex** |

For a gamble paying $W_1$ with probability $p$ and $W_2$ otherwise, risk aversion means $p\,u(W_1) + (1-p)\,u(W_2) < u(pW_1 + (1-p)W_2)$ — the chord lies **below** the curve.

- **Certainty equivalent (CE):** the sure amount with the same utility as the gamble, $u(\text{CE}) = E[u]$.
- **Risk premium:** $\text{EV} - \text{CE}$ — what you would give up to replace the gamble by its average.

<RiskAttitudeLab />

### Insurance as a trade between risk attitudes

A risk-averse owner ($u = \sqrt{x}$) holds an asset worth 100 or 0 with equal probability. A risk-neutral insurer offers to pay 100 whenever the asset pays 0, for a premium $P$ paid in all cases.

- **Owner accepts** iff $\sqrt{100 - P} \ge \tfrac12\sqrt{0} + \tfrac12\sqrt{100} = 5$, i.e. $P \le 75$.
- **Insurer accepts** iff $P \ge \tfrac12 \cdot 100 = 50$.
- Any $P \in [50, 75]$ makes both better off. **Risk is transferred to whoever bears it more cheaply** — the basic logic behind cargo insurance, credit insurance, and hedging.

## 2.7 Risk sharing and the securitisation lesson

Two identical owners each hold an independent asset paying 100 or 0 with probability 1/2 ($u = \sqrt{x}$). Alone, each has $E[u] = 5$. If they **pool** and split the total, each share pays 100 (prob 1/4), 50 (prob 1/2), or 0 (prob 1/4):

$$
E[u] = \tfrac14\sqrt{100} + \tfrac12\sqrt{50} + \tfrac14\sqrt{0} \approx 6.036 > 5.
$$

The expected payout is unchanged; only the **spread** shrinks — and risk-averse owners value that. Push $n$ higher and the certainty equivalent climbs toward the expected value 50. Then raise the **common-shock** slider.

<RiskPoolingLab />

This is the logic — and the trap — of **mortgage-backed securities**. Pooling many mortgages makes each slice almost riskless **if defaults are independent**, so investors pay close to expected value. When defaults are driven by a common factor (a national housing slump), the pool behaves like a single mortgage. Securities priced under the independence assumption were therefore **over-priced**.

## Key takeaways

- Rational preferences are **complete and transitive**; then — and only then — they have a utility representation.
- Under certainty utility is **ordinal**; under uncertainty, vNM utility is **cardinal**: unique up to $au + b$, $a > 0$.
- A choice under uncertainty is a choice of **lottery**; the agent maximises **expected utility**. Actions often change *which* lottery you face.
- Game payoffs are vNM utilities: one number per outcome, calibrated from indifferences.
- **Concave u ⇔ risk aversion.** CE < EV; the gap is the risk premium. Insurance and risk sharing create value; **correlation** destroys diversification.
