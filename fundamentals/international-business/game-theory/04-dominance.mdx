# 4. Dominance: What Rationality Alone Rules Out

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 4 and Problem Set 2 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Wording, games, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **Define** a rational player as one who best-responds to *some* belief, and explain why that is weaker than "knows what the others will do".
2. **Test** whether a strategy is strictly dominated — by a pure strategy **or by a mixture** — and connect this to "never a best response".
3. **Distinguish** strict from weak dominance and explain why weakly dominated strategies can survive in equilibrium.
4. **Find** dominant strategies and dominant-strategy equilibria.
5. **Prove** that bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy in a second-price auction.

## 4.1 Rationality means best-responding to a belief

A player does not know what others will do, but she has a **belief** — a probability distribution over their strategies. She is **rational** if she plays a strategy that maximises expected payoff **given that belief**.

Which strategies could a rational player *ever* choose? Those that are a **best response to at least one belief**. The key result:

> **Theorem.** A strategy is a best response to some belief **if and only if** it is **not strictly dominated** — where the dominating strategy is allowed to be a **mixed** strategy.

A strategy $s_i$ is **strictly dominated** by $\sigma_i$ if $u_i(\sigma_i, s_{-i}) > u_i(s_i, s_{-i})$ for **every** profile $s_{-i}$ of the others.

## 4.2 Dominant strategies

A strategy is **strictly dominant** if it strictly dominates **every** other strategy: it is the unique best reply to every belief. If **every** player has a dominant strategy, the profile of dominant strategies is a **dominant-strategy equilibrium** — the most robust prediction game theory offers, because it needs only rationality, not knowledge of others' rationality.

The Prisoners' Dilemma is the famous case: Defect dominates Cooperate for both players, even though both cooperating is better for both. Rationality does not guarantee efficiency.

### Weak dominance

$s_i$ **weakly dominates** $s_i'$ if it is **never worse** and **sometimes strictly better**. Weak dominance is a weaker argument: a weakly dominated strategy can still be a best response — to beliefs that put all weight where the two tie.

<GameMatrixLab preset="weakDom" />

Up weakly dominates Down, and Left weakly dominates Right — yet (Down, Right) is stable: if you are sure the other will play Right (or Down), you lose nothing by playing Down (or Right). Strict elimination removes nothing here. Lesson: weak dominance is a good **tie-breaker** but not a consequence of rationality alone.

## 4.3 Example: the second-price auction

An object is sold to $n$ bidders. Bidder $i$ values it at $v_i$ (known only to her). Bids are sealed; the **highest bid wins** and the winner **pays the second-highest bid**. (Ties: split at random.)

**Claim.** Bidding $b_i = v_i$ weakly dominates every other bid.

Let $r$ be the highest rival bid. Your bid only decides **whether** you win; the **price** is $r$ either way.

| Case | Bid v | Shade to b below v | Overbid to b above v |
|---|---|---|---|
| r < b, r < v | win, gain v − r | same | same |
| b < r < v | — | **lose a profitable win** | — |
| v < r < b | — | — | **win at a loss, v − r < 0** |
| r > v, r > b | lose, 0 | same | same |

Deviating never helps and sometimes hurts. Try it:

This is why platforms that sell ad slots or spectrum often use second-price logic: bidders need **no strategy and no information about rivals** — just bid what the item is worth to them.

## Key takeaways

- **Rational** = best response to *some* belief. Exactly the strategies that are **not strictly dominated** (allowing mixed dominators) can be rational.
- A **dominant strategy** is best whatever others do; if all players have one, we have a **dominant-strategy equilibrium** — robust, but possibly inefficient (Prisoners' Dilemma).
- **Weak** dominance allows ties; weakly dominated strategies can appear in equilibrium, so treat weak dominance as a refinement, not a rationality requirement.
- In a **second-price auction**, truthful bidding is weakly dominant: your bid determines whether you win, not what you pay.
