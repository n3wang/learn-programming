# 6. Nash Equilibrium: Pure and Mixed

> **Teaching rewrite** — follows the topic outline of MIT 14.12 lecture notes ch. 6 (MIT OpenCourseWare). Wording, games, and numbers are original.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you should be able to:

1. **State** the definition of a Nash equilibrium and explain the "no profitable unilateral deviation" test.
2. **Find** all pure-strategy equilibria of a matrix game by marking best responses.
3. **Place** Nash equilibrium between the other solution concepts: dominant-strategy equilibrium ⊂ Nash equilibrium ⊂ rationalizable.
4. **Compute** mixed-strategy equilibria with the **indifference principle**, and explain why each player's mix depends on the *other* player's payoffs.
5. **Interpret** a mixed equilibrium as the stable composition of a population (Hawk–Dove evolution).

## 6.1 Definition

A strategy profile $s^* = (s_1^*, \dots, s_n^*)$ is a **Nash equilibrium** if every player's strategy is a best response to the others':

$$
u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \text{for every player } i \text{ and every strategy } s_i.
$$

Two readings:

- **Stability:** if everyone expects $s^*$, nobody wants to deviate alone.
- **Self-fulfilling beliefs:** each player's belief about the others is *correct*, and each best-responds to it.

Rationalizability asks only that beliefs be *consistent with rationality*; Nash equilibrium additionally asks them to be **correct**.

**Finding pure equilibria.** For each column, mark Player 1's best payoff; for each row, mark Player 2's best payoff. A cell with both marks is an equilibrium.

## 6.2 How it relates to earlier concepts

| If… | then… | but not conversely, e.g. |
|---|---|---|
| $s^*$ is a **dominant-strategy equilibrium** | it is a Nash equilibrium (dominant strategies are best responses to everything) | coordination games have equilibria but no dominant strategies |
| $s^*$ is a **Nash equilibrium** | every $s_i^*$ is **rationalizable** (each is a best response to beliefs that are themselves best responses…) | in the 3 × 3 game of lesson 5, (a, y) is rationalizable but not an equilibrium |

A game can have a **unique rationalizable outcome** (then it is the unique equilibrium), **several equilibria** (coordination), or **no pure equilibrium** (matching pennies). The last case is why we need mixed strategies.

## 6.3 Mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium

Allow each player to randomise. A profile of mixed strategies $\sigma^*$ is a Nash equilibrium if each $\sigma_i^*$ is a best response to $\sigma_{-i}^*$.

**Indifference principle.** If a player puts positive probability on two pure strategies in equilibrium, they must give her the **same** expected payoff (otherwise she would drop the worse one). So:

> To find Player 1's equilibrium mix, make **Player 2 indifferent**. To find Player 2's mix, make **Player 1 indifferent**.

**Nash's theorem.** Every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

### Worked example: customs inspection

A customs office chooses Inspect (cost 1) or Skip; a trader chooses Smuggle or Comply. Catching smuggling earns the office 4 and costs the trader 6; undetected smuggling costs the office 5 and earns the trader 3.

- **No pure equilibrium**: if the trader smuggles, inspect; if the office inspects, comply; if the trader complies, skip; if the office skips, smuggle.
- **Trader's mix q (prob. of Smuggle)** makes the office indifferent: $4q - 1\cdot(1-q) = -5q \Rightarrow q = 0.1$.
- **Office's mix p (prob. of Inspect)** makes the trader indifferent: $-6p + 3(1-p) = 0 \Rightarrow p = 1/3$.

A surprising comparative static: a **bigger fine** changes the trader's payoffs, so it lowers the office's *inspection rate* — the smuggling rate (pinned down by the *office's* payoffs) stays at 10%.

## 6.4 Evolution of hawks and doves

Mixed equilibria have a second interpretation. Imagine a large population in which each individual is **hard-wired** as Hawk or Dove and meets random opponents. Types that earn more than average reproduce (or are imitated) faster.

<HawkDoveEvolutionLab />

When fighting is costly ($V < C$), the hawk share converges to $V/C$ from any starting point: if hawks are rarer than that, they meet mostly doves and prosper; if more common, they fight each other and decline. The rest point is exactly the mixed equilibrium — and it is **evolutionarily stable**. The same logic describes markets where "aggressive" and "accommodating" business strategies coexist.

## Key takeaways

- **Nash equilibrium**: each player best-responds to the others; beliefs are correct. Test: no profitable *unilateral* deviation.
- Dominant-strategy equilibrium ⇒ Nash equilibrium ⇒ rationalizable (none of the converses hold).
- **Mixed equilibria** always exist in finite games. Find them by making the **other** player indifferent.
- A mixed equilibrium can describe deliberate randomisation (inspections, audits, penalty kicks) or the stable **mix of types** in a population.
