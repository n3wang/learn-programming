# Ordering Guarantees

> **Adapted from:** Martin Kleppmann, *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* (O'Reilly, 2017), Chapter 9 — rewritten and condensed for teaching.

[Linearizability](./linearizability) defined its guarantee entirely in terms of **order**: every operation appears to take effect atomically, at some single point along one shared timeline. That's not a coincidence — ordering has been a recurring, load-bearing idea throughout this whole series: a replication leader's whole job is fixing the order writes get applied in; serializability means transactions behave *as if* run in some sequential order; even physical clocks and timestamps are, underneath it all, just another attempt to impose order on a genuinely disorderly world. This lesson pulls that thread all the way through — from **causality** (a weaker, more achievable notion of order) up to **total order broadcast** (a genuinely powerful abstraction that turns out to be equivalent to consensus itself).

---

## Ordering and Causality

**Causality** has quietly shown up throughout this series already:

- [Problems with Replication Lag](./problems-with-replication-lag) covered **consistent prefix reads** — an observer seeing an *answer* before the *question* it responds to, purely because the two happened to replicate at different speeds. That's jarring precisely because it violates cause-and-effect: an answer can't exist before its question.
- [Multi-Leader Replication](./multi-leader-replication) covered writes **overtaking** each other across replicas — a replica seeing an *update* to a row that, from its own point of view, doesn't even exist yet, because the row's original *creation* hasn't arrived over that particular network path yet.
- [Leaderless Replication](./leaderless-replication) covered the **happens-before** relationship directly: for any two operations A and B, either A happened before B, B happened before A, or the two are genuinely **concurrent** (neither aware of the other) — happens-before *is* causality, stated formally.
- **Snapshot isolation** (transactions) reads from a snapshot that's *consistent with causality*: if the snapshot shows you an answer, it must also show you the question that answer responds to — the effects of everything that happened causally *before* that snapshot point are visible, nothing that happened causally *after* it leaks in.
- **Write skew** between transactions is, underneath, also a causality problem: an action taken based on an *observation* is causally dependent on that observation — Serializable Snapshot Isolation catches write skew precisely by tracking those causal dependencies between transactions directly.

Causality imposes order: cause precedes effect, a message is sent before it's received, a question precedes its answer. Chains of causally-dependent operations — one node reads something, writes a result, another node reads *that*, writes something else in turn — define what actually **happened before what** across an entire system. A system that respects this ordering is **causally consistent**. Snapshot isolation is a concrete example: whenever you read some piece of data, you're guaranteed to also be able to see everything that causally preceded it (barring deletion in the meantime).

## The Causal Order Is a *Partial* Order, Not a Total One

A **total order** lets you compare *any* two elements and always say which is bigger — natural numbers work this way: given any two numbers, one is always strictly greater than the other. Mathematical **sets**, by contrast, only support a **partial order**: `{a, b}` and `{b, c}` genuinely can't be compared — neither is a subset of the other — they're simply **incomparable**.

This exact distinction separates two consistency models covered in this series:

- **Linearizability** imposes a **total order**: since the system behaves as if there's a single copy of the data with every operation atomic, for *any* two operations you can always say definitively which one happened first. There is no such thing as two genuinely "incomparable" operations in a linearizable system.
- **Causality** imposes only a **partial order**: two operations are ordered *only* if one causally depends on the other; if they're concurrent, they're simply **incomparable** — neither before nor after the other, structurally the same as `{a, b}` versus `{b, c}`.

Put differently: a linearizable datastore has **no concurrency** at all, in the causal sense — there's one single timeline every operation gets slotted into, atomically, one at a time. Concurrency, by contrast, means the timeline genuinely **branches and merges** — exactly the shape of a distributed version-control history (Git, say): usually one commit follows another in a straight line, but concurrent work by different people produces branches, and merges reconcile them back together. That branching graph of causal dependencies is precisely what causal ordering actually looks like in practice.

### Linearizability Is Strictly Stronger Than Causal Consistency

**Any linearizable system automatically preserves causality** — this is a real, useful theorem, not a coincidence. In particular, if a system has multiple independent communication channels (the file-storage-plus-message-queue example from [Linearizability](./linearizability#where-linearizability-actually-matters)), linearizability preserves causal ordering across them **automatically**, with no extra bookkeeping (no timestamps to pass around, no explicit dependency tracking) required at all.

That automatic simplicity is exactly what makes linearizable systems so appealing — but [Linearizability](./linearizability#the-cost-of-linearizability) already established the real cost: linearizability can meaningfully hurt both performance and availability, especially across real network delay. The genuinely good news: **linearizability isn't the only way to preserve causality.** A system can be causally consistent *without* paying linearizability's full cost — and unlike full linearizability, causal consistency is **not** subject to the CAP theorem's forced trade-off at all. In fact, causal consistency is provably the **strongest** consistency model that can be maintained without incurring network-delay-driven slowdowns, and it can remain fully available even during a network partition. A great many systems that superficially seem to *need* full linearizability actually only need causal consistency underneath — which opens the door to implementations with performance and availability characteristics much closer to eventually consistent systems, while still ruling out the genuinely confusing anomalies linearizability was invoked to prevent in the first place. (This is an active, comparatively recent research area — not yet mainstream in production systems, but a promising direction.)

## Sequence Number Ordering

Tracking every explicit causal dependency for real, complex applications gets impractical fast — most applications read a lot of data before any given write, and it's rarely obvious which of those prior reads the write is actually causally dependent on. The practical alternative: **sequence numbers** (or timestamps) — not necessarily from a physical clock, but often from a **logical clock**: an algorithm that just generates an increasing sequence of numbers to identify operations, typically nothing more than a counter incremented on every operation.

Done right, these sequence numbers give you a genuine **total order** that's still **consistent with causality**: if operation A causally happened before B, A is guaranteed to get a lower number than B (concurrent operations can land in either order — that's fine, since causality doesn't constrain them anyway). Single-leader replication already gives you exactly this, essentially for free: the leader just increments a counter for every write in its replication log, and any follower applying that log in order ends up causally consistent by construction, even while lagging behind.

### When There's No Single Leader, Naive Sequence Generation Breaks Causality

Without a single leader (multi-leader, leaderless, or a partitioned database), a few tempting shortcuts turn out to be broken in the same way:

- **Per-node independent counters** (node A generates only even numbers, node B only odd) — but different nodes process operations at different rates, so one counter can drift meaningfully ahead of the other, and comparing an even-numbered operation against an odd-numbered one tells you **nothing** reliable about which genuinely happened first.
- **Physical clock timestamps** — subject to exactly the clock-skew problems already covered in [Unreliable Clocks](./unreliable-clocks#relying-on-synchronized-clocks-is-genuinely-dangerous), where a causally-*later* write can end up with a numerically *smaller* timestamp purely due to ordinary skew between two nodes' clocks.
- **Preallocated number blocks** (node A claims 1–1000, node B claims 1001–2000) — an operation from A's block can easily be causally **later** than one from B's block, while still carrying a numerically smaller sequence number.

All three genuinely do scale better than funneling every operation through one leader's counter — but all three share the identical flaw: **the numbers they generate aren't consistent with causality.**

### Lamport Timestamps: A Genuine Fix

There's an elegant, surprisingly simple fix, proposed by Leslie Lamport in 1978, in what's since become one of the most-cited papers in distributed systems: the **Lamport timestamp**. Every node keeps a unique ID and its own local counter. A **Lamport timestamp** is simply the pair `(counter, node ID)` — two timestamps compare by counter first, and by node ID only as a tiebreaker when counters happen to match, which is enough to make every timestamp strictly, totally orderable.

The genuinely clever part — the piece that actually makes this **causality-consistent**, not just another naive counter scheme: **every node and every client tracks the highest counter value it has ever seen, anywhere, and attaches that maximum to every request it sends.** The moment any node receives a message carrying a counter value **higher** than its own, it immediately jumps its own counter forward to match that maximum before continuing. As long as this "carry the max forward" rule is followed on *every* single message, every genuine causal dependency necessarily produces a strictly increasing timestamp along the way — which is exactly what makes the resulting total order provably consistent with causality.

:::note Not the same tool as version vectors
Lamport timestamps are easy to confuse with the **version vectors** from [Leaderless Replication](./leaderless-replication#detecting-concurrent-writes) — but they solve different problems. Version vectors can tell you whether two operations are genuinely **concurrent** or one causally depends on the other. Lamport timestamps enforce a strict **total order** on everything — from the timestamps alone, you genuinely cannot tell whether two operations were causally related or merely concurrent; that distinction is thrown away. In exchange, Lamport timestamps are far more compact — a single `(counter, ID)` pair, rather than one counter per node.
:::

## Timestamp Ordering Alone Isn't Enough

Here's the catch, even with a perfectly causality-consistent total order in hand: it's still not sufficient to solve a problem like the username-uniqueness constraint from [Linearizability](./linearizability#where-linearizability-actually-matters). It's tempting to think a total order alone settles it — if two users concurrently claim the same username, just keep whichever claim carries the **lower** timestamp and reject the other, since timestamps are always comparable.

That reasoning works fine **after the fact** — once you've collected every username-creation operation that will ever happen, comparing their timestamps to pick a winner is trivial. But it does **not** work at the actual moment a node needs to make a **real-time decision**: right now, should *this* particular claim succeed or fail? At that instant, the node has no way to know whether some *other* node is, at this very moment, concurrently processing a claim for the identical username — and, crucially, no way to know what timestamp that other operation might end up carrying. Being fully sure no other node could still insert a **lower**-timestamped, conflicting claim would require actively checking in with literally *every* other node before deciding anything — and if even one of those nodes is temporarily unreachable, the whole system grinds to a halt exactly the way [Faults, Partial Failures, and Unreliable Networks](./faults-partial-failures-and-unreliable-networks) already warned against.

The real problem: a total order **only fully emerges after you've collected every operation** — but a node making a live decision right now has no way to know about operations from other nodes that **haven't happened yet, or haven't arrived yet**. So a total order alone, however causality-consistent, is not enough — you additionally need to know **when that order is actually finalized**. That specific idea — a total order you can trust *has fully arrived*, not merely one that's internally consistent — is exactly what **total order broadcast** provides.

## Total Order Broadcast

On a single CPU core, defining a total order of operations is trivial — it's just whatever order the CPU actually executed them in. Getting **every node in a distributed system** to agree on one shared total order is the genuinely hard version of that same problem — and it turns out to be **more powerful** than either of the approaches covered so far in this lesson: sequence-number/timestamp ordering alone (not fault-tolerant enough — as just shown) or single-leader replication alone (not fault-tolerant to leader failure without a real recovery story).

**Total order broadcast** is a protocol for reliably passing messages between nodes, satisfying exactly two safety properties, always:

- **Reliable delivery** — no message is ever silently lost: if a message is delivered to *any* one node, it must eventually be delivered to *every* node.
- **Totally ordered delivery** — every node delivers every message in the exact **same** order as every other node.

A network fault will obviously still delay delivery — total order broadcast doesn't defy physics — but a correct implementation must keep retrying until the message genuinely gets through, and once it does, it must land in the correct, agreed-upon position relative to everything else, on every node, without exception.

### What Total Order Broadcast Actually Buys You

**Database replication.** If every message represents one write, and every replica applies the exact same messages in the exact same order, the replicas stay consistent with each other (aside from ordinary, temporary lag) — this is called **state machine replication**, and it's essentially the general-purpose engine underneath everything covered in [Leaders and Followers](./leaders-and-followers).

**Serializable transactions.** If every message represents one deterministic transaction (a stored procedure, say), and every partition and replica processes those messages in the identical order, the whole database stays consistent everywhere — genuine serializability, achieved without any locking at all.

**Fencing tokens.** [Knowledge, Truth, and Lies](./knowledge-truth-and-lies#the-leader-and-the-lock) already covered fencing tokens as the fix for stale-leader lock disputes — total order broadcast is a natural way to *generate* them: append every lock-acquisition request as a message to the log, number messages sequentially in the exact order they land in the log, and that number **is** a valid, monotonically-increasing fencing token, by construction. (This is precisely what ZooKeeper's `zxid` already is.)

A critical structural property, worth calling out explicitly: total order broadcast's order is **fixed at delivery time** — a node is never permitted to retroactively splice a message into an earlier slot after later messages have already been delivered. That's exactly what makes total order broadcast strictly **stronger** than plain timestamp ordering — a total order that's genuinely, permanently settled the instant it's delivered, not merely internally consistent after the fact. You can also think of total order broadcast as, in effect, a general mechanism for building a **log** (a replication log, a transaction log, a write-ahead log): delivering a message is exactly equivalent to appending to that log, and since every node delivers the identical messages in the identical order, every node ends up looking at the exact same sequence.

## Linearizability and Total Order Broadcast Are Deeply Connected

Given total order broadcast's guarantee of one shared, totally ordered log across every node, it's natural to wonder whether that's just linearizability under another name. It isn't — but the two are tightly linked, in a way worth understanding precisely.

**Total order broadcast is asynchronous**: messages are guaranteed to be delivered reliably, in the same fixed order everywhere — but with **no** guarantee about exactly *when* any given message is delivered, so one node can genuinely lag behind others. **Linearizability**, by contrast, is fundamentally a **recency** guarantee: a read must reflect the *most recent* write, right now, not eventually.

### Building Linearizable Storage on Top of Total Order Broadcast

Despite that difference, total order broadcast is enough to **build** genuinely linearizable storage on top of it. Take the username uniqueness problem once more: imagine a linearizable register per possible username, each starting `null`, with an atomic compare-and-set available on each. Using total order broadcast as an append-only log:

1. Append a message tentatively claiming the desired username.
2. Read the log until your own just-appended message comes back around to you.
3. Scan the log for every message claiming that same username. If **yours** is the first one for that username, you win — commit the claim. If someone else's message beat you to that same username, you lose — abort.

Because every node sees log entries delivered in the identical order, if several claims for the same username land concurrently, **every node independently agrees** on which one arrived first — full agreement, with zero extra coordination required.

This procedure alone guarantees **linearizable writes** — but *not* automatically linearizable **reads**: reading straight from a store that's only asynchronously kept up to date from the log can still hand back stale data (this weaker guarantee is called **sequential consistency**, a real step below full linearizability). To make **reads** linearizable too, three concrete options exist: **sequence reads through the log itself** (append a marker message, wait for it to come back around, and only *then* perform the actual read — its position in the log defines exactly when the read "happens"); **fetch the log's current tail position in a linearizable way**, wait until every entry up to that exact position has been delivered locally, and *then* read; or simply **read from a replica that's updated synchronously** on every write, guaranteeing it's never behind at all.

### Building Total Order Broadcast on Top of Linearizable Storage

The relationship runs the other direction too. Given a single **linearizable integer register** supporting an atomic **increment-and-get**, total order broadcast falls out almost immediately: for every message you want to broadcast, atomically increment-and-get the register, attach the resulting value as that message's sequence number, then send the tagged message to every node — recipients simply deliver messages consecutively, strictly in order of that number.

The genuinely important detail here: unlike Lamport timestamps, values from a linearizable increment-and-get form a sequence with **no gaps whatsoever**. If a node has already delivered message 4 and then receives message 6, it *knows*, with certainty, that message 5 must still be somewhere in flight — and can correctly wait for it before delivering 6. Lamport timestamps offer no such guarantee; **gap-free-ness is precisely the structural difference** between total order broadcast and mere timestamp ordering.

So — how hard could a linearizable integer with atomic increment-and-get actually be to build? Trivial, if nothing ever fails: just keep it as a plain variable on one node. The real difficulty, unsurprisingly, is exactly the same one that's run through this entire series: handling what happens when the network connection to that node breaks, or the node itself dies, and correctly recovering its true value afterward. Push hard enough on "how do I build a fault-tolerant, linearizable sequence number generator?" and you inevitably land right back on: a **consensus algorithm**.

That's not a coincidence — it's provable: a linearizable compare-and-set (or increment-and-get) register and total order broadcast are **both**, formally, equivalent to solving **consensus**. Solve any one of these three problems, and you can mechanically transform that solution into a working solution for either of the other two. It's a genuinely striking, unifying result — and it sets up exactly where this series goes next.

:::important Key takeaways
- **Causality** imposes a **partial order** (some operations are ordered, some are genuinely incomparable/concurrent); **linearizability** imposes a **total order** (every operation is comparable to every other). Linearizability implies causal consistency, but the reverse doesn't hold — and causal consistency alone is achievable without paying linearizability's network-delay cost or losing partition availability.
- **Lamport timestamps** — a `(counter, node ID)` pair, with every node carrying forward the maximum value it's ever seen — give you a compact total order that's genuinely consistent with causality, unlike naive per-node counters or physical-clock timestamps.
- Timestamp ordering alone can't solve real-time decisions like uniqueness constraints, because the total order only fully **emerges after the fact** — a live node has no way to know about not-yet-arrived operations from elsewhere.
- **Total order broadcast** (reliable delivery + identical order everywhere, fixed permanently at delivery time) is strictly stronger than timestamp ordering, and underlies state machine replication, serializable transactions, and fencing tokens.
- **Linearizable storage, total order broadcast, and consensus are all formally equivalent** — solve any one, and you can mechanically build the other two on top of it. That equivalence is exactly what makes consensus — the subject of the next two lessons — the true, unifying core of this whole topic.
:::
