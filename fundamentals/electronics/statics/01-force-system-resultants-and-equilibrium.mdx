> Study notes on Chapter 1 of *The Electrical Engineering Handbook Series* — "Force-System Resultants and Equilibrium" (R. C. Hibbeler). Figures are reproduced from the handbook.

**Statics** studies bodies in equilibrium — at rest or moving at constant velocity. Before applying the equilibrium laws you need two tools: simplifying a force system, and computing the moment of a force.

## 1.1 Force-system resultants

### Concurrent forces

A force is a vector: magnitude, direction, and point of application. Two forces acting through the same point add by the **parallelogram law** to give a resultant $\mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2$.

For more than two forces it is easier to resolve each one into Cartesian components, $\mathbf{F} = F_x\mathbf{i} + F_y\mathbf{j} + F_z\mathbf{k}$, and add component-wise:

$$
\mathbf{F}_R = \sum F_x\,\mathbf{i} + \sum F_y\,\mathbf{j} + \sum F_z\,\mathbf{k} \tag{1.1}
$$

### Moment of a force

Where a force acts matters. Sliding $\mathbf{F}$ along its own line of action (A → B in Figure 1.3) leaves the body's motion unchanged — the **principle of transmissibility** — though internal stresses differ. Moving it off that line (to C) changes the rotation, because the lines of action are now separated by a distance $d$.

That turning tendency about a point O is the **moment**:

$$
M_O = F d \tag{1.2}
$$

where $d$ is the perpendicular distance from O to the line of action. Its direction follows the **right-hand rule**: curl your fingers with the rotation, and your thumb points along $\mathbf{M}_O$.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| ![Figure 1.3 — Transmissibility.](/img/statics/fig-1-3.png) | ![Figure 1.4 — Moment of a force.](/img/statics/fig-1-4.png) |
| *Figure 1.3 — Same force at A, B (same line) and C (offset by d).* | *Figure 1.4 — Moment of a force about O.* |

When $d$ is awkward to find, use the **principle of moments**: the moment of a force equals the sum of the moments of its components, so $M_O = F_x y + F_y x$ (Figure 1.5).

In 3D, write it as a cross product, with $\mathbf{r}$ running from O to *any* point on the line of action:

$$
\mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \tag{1.3}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{M}_O &=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\
x & y & z \\
F_x & F_y & F_z
\end{vmatrix} \\
&= (yF_z - zF_y)\,\mathbf{i} + (zF_x - xF_z)\,\mathbf{j} + (xF_y - yF_x)\,\mathbf{k}
\end{aligned} \tag{1.4}
$$

### Couples

A **couple** is two equal, opposite, parallel forces a distance $d$ apart. Its moment is the same about every point:

$$
\mathbf{M}_C = \mathbf{r}_1 \times \mathbf{F} + \mathbf{r}_2 \times (-\mathbf{F}) = (\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2) \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F} \tag{1.5}
$$

Here $\mathbf{r}$ goes from any point on $-\mathbf{F}$'s line to any point on $\mathbf{F}$'s line. A couple's resultant force is zero, so it rotates a body without translating it.

![Figure 1.8 — A couple.](/img/statics/fig-1-8.png)

*Figure 1.8 — A couple: the moment depends only on the separation r.*

### Resultant of a force and couple system

Any set of forces and couples can be replaced by one force and one couple moment at a chosen point O:

$$
\mathbf{F}_R = \sum \mathbf{F} \tag{1.6}
$$

$$
\mathbf{M}_{R_O} = \sum \mathbf{M}_O \tag{1.7}
$$

$\sum \mathbf{M}_O$ includes $\mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{F}_i$ for every force **plus** every couple moment.

You can go one step further. Split $\mathbf{M}_{R_O}$ into a part parallel to $\mathbf{F}_R$ ($M_\parallel$) and a part perpendicular to it ($M_\perp$). Shifting $\mathbf{F}_R$ sideways by $d = M_\perp / F_R$ absorbs $M_\perp$, leaving a **wrench**: a force plus a collinear moment — a push and a twist together.

If $\theta = 90^\circ$ then $M_\parallel = 0$ and everything reduces to a single force on a specific line of action. That always happens for **concurrent, parallel, or coplanar** systems.

![Figure 1.9 — Reducing to a wrench.](/img/statics/fig-1-9.png)

*Figure 1.9 — (a) general system → (b) force + couple at O → (c) wrench.*

### Distributed loadings

Contact loads are really spread over an area. If that area is small, treat it as a point force; if it's large (wind, fluid pressure), describe it with a **load-intensity** function $p = p(x, y)$. Its resultant and line of action are:

$$
F_R = \int_A p(x,y)\,dA \tag{1.8}
$$

$$
\bar{x} = \frac{\int_A x\,p\,dA}{\int_A p\,dA}, \qquad
\bar{y} = \frac{\int_A y\,p\,dA}{\int_A p\,dA} \tag{1.9}
$$

Geometrically, $F_R$ is the **volume** under the load diagram and it acts through that volume's centroid. For a line load $w = w(x)$, $F_R$ is the **area** under the curve, acting through the area's centroid.

**Body forces** act through the volume without contact — gravity is the usual one. Its resultant is the **weight**, acting through the center of gravity.

## 1.2 Equilibrium

### Equations of equilibrium

Treat the body as **rigid** (fine when deformations are small). It is in equilibrium iff the resultant force and moment both vanish:

$$
\sum \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0} \tag{1.10}
$$

$$
\sum \mathbf{M}_O = \mathbf{0} \tag{1.11}
$$

In components that is six scalar equations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum F_x &= 0, & \sum F_y &= 0, & \sum F_z &= 0 \\
\sum M_x &= 0, & \sum M_y &= 0, & \sum M_z &= 0
\end{aligned} \tag{1.12}
$$

Any three non-coplanar, non-parallel axes work for the sums. For a **coplanar** system only three remain:

$$
\sum F_x = 0,\quad \sum F_y = 0,\quad \sum M_O = 0 \tag{1.13}
$$

O can be anywhere — on or off the body.

### Free-body diagram

Sketch the body isolated ("free") from everything around it and draw **every** force and couple on it: applied loads, support and contact reactions, and weight. Add the dimensions you'll need for moments. Once the FBD and axes are set, the equilibrium equations are mechanical.

### Support reactions

Rule of thumb: **a support that blocks translation in a direction exerts a force in that direction; one that blocks rotation exerts a couple moment.**

### Friction

A rough surface resists slipping with a force tangent to the surface and opposing the (possible) motion. For dry surfaces:

$$
F \le \mu_s N \tag{1.14}
$$

Equality holds only when slipping is **impending**. $N$ is the normal force and $\mu_s$ the coefficient of static friction. Once sliding, $F = \mu_k N$, with $\mu_k$ roughly 25% below $\mu_s$.

| Materials | $\mu_s$ |
| --- | --- |
| Metal on ice | 0.03 – 0.05 |
| Wood on wood | 0.30 – 0.70 |
| Leather on wood | 0.20 – 0.50 |
| Leather on metal | 0.30 – 0.60 |
| Aluminum on aluminum | 1.10 – 1.70 |

*Table 1.2 — Typical coefficients of static friction.*

### Constraints

Satisfying the equations is not enough; the body must also be properly held.

- **Statically indeterminate** — more supports than needed, so more unknowns than equations. The beam in Figure 1.11 has four unknowns ($A_x, A_y, M_A, B_y$) but only three equations; the extra one comes from deformation (mechanics of materials).
- **Improperly constrained** — the body can move. This happens when the reactions are all **parallel** (Figure 1.12, nothing resists $\sum F_x$) or all **concurrent** (Figure 1.13, nothing resists rotation about that point).

If the number of reactions is the minimum and they are neither parallel nor concurrent, the problem is **statically determinate**.

### Internal loadings

The **method of sections**: if a body is in equilibrium, so is any piece of it. Cut it, draw the FBD of one piece, and the internal resultants become external loads you can solve for.

- In 3D there are six: normal force $N_y$, shears $V_x, V_z$, torque $T_y$, and bending moments $M_x, M_z$ (Figure 1.14).
- For coplanar loads only three: normal force $N$, shear $V$, bending moment $M$ (Figure 1.15).

How those resultants are distributed over the cut (**stress**) is mechanics of materials.

## Worked examples

The recipe is always the same: pick axes, draw the FBD, apply the equations.

### Example 1.1 — Parallel forces on a plate

Reduce the three parallel forces on the plate to one resultant and find where it acts.

![Figure 1.16](/img/statics/fig-1-16.png)

*Figure 1.16*

$$
\mathbf{F}_R = -300\mathbf{k} - 400\mathbf{k} - 200\mathbf{k} = \{-900\mathbf{k}\}\ \text{N}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{M}_{R_O} &= (2\mathbf{i}+1.5\mathbf{j})\times(-300\mathbf{k}) + (2.5\mathbf{j})\times(-400\mathbf{k}) + (-0.5\mathbf{i}+2.5\mathbf{j})\times(-200\mathbf{k}) \\
&= \{-1950\mathbf{i} + 500\mathbf{j}\}\ \text{N·m}
\end{aligned}
$$

As expected for parallel forces, $\mathbf{F}_R \perp \mathbf{M}_{R_O}$. Place $\mathbf{F}_R$ at $(\bar x, \bar y)$ so that $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{M}_{R_O}$:

$$
\bar x = \frac{M_{O_y}}{F_R} = \frac{500}{900} = 0.556\ \text{m}, \qquad
\bar y = \frac{M_{O_x}}{F_R} = \frac{1950}{900} = 2.17\ \text{m}
$$

### Example 1.2 — Beam with a pin and a smooth support

Find the reactions on the beam.

![Figure 1.17](/img/statics/fig-1-17.png)

*Figure 1.17 — (a) beam, (b) free-body diagram.*

From Table 1.1, A is a pin ($A_x, A_y$) and B a smooth contact ($N_B$, normal to the 60° member). Three unknowns, three equations — determinate. Sum moments about A to drop $A_x$ and $A_y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum M_A = 0:\quad & -500\left(\tfrac35\right)(2) + N_B\cos 60^\circ\,(4 + 2\cos 60^\circ) \\
& + N_B\sin 60^\circ\,(2\sin 60^\circ) = 0
\;\Rightarrow\; N_B = 150\ \text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\sum F_x = 0:\quad A_x - 500\left(\tfrac45\right) - 150\sin 60^\circ = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A_x = 530\ \text{N}
$$

$$
\sum F_y = 0:\quad A_y - 500\left(\tfrac35\right) + 150\cos 60^\circ = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A_y = 225\ \text{N}
$$

All positive, so the assumed directions on the FBD are correct. (The figure labels the load "500 kN"; the handbook's solution works in N.)

### Example 1.3 — Compound beam

Segments AB and BC are pinned at B. Find the support reactions.

![Figure 1.18](/img/statics/fig-1-18.png)

*Figure 1.18 — (a) compound beam, (b) FBDs of each segment.*

Split at B; by Newton's third law, $B_x$ and $B_y$ act equal and opposite on the two pieces. Replace each distributed load with its resultant: the uniform 400 N/m over 4 m → 1600 N at the midpoint; the triangle over 3 m → $\tfrac12(400)(3) = 600$ N at 1 m from B.

**Segment BC:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum F_x = 0&:\quad B_x = 0 \\
\sum M_B = 0&:\quad -600(1) + C_y(3) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; C_y = 200\ \text{N} \\
\sum F_y = 0&:\quad B_y - 600 + 200 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; B_y = 400\ \text{N}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Segment AB:**

$$
\sum F_x = 0:\ A_x = 0 \qquad
\sum F_y = 0:\ A_y - 1600 - 400 = 0 \Rightarrow A_y = 2000\ \text{N}
$$

$$
\sum M_A = 0:\ M_A - 1600(2) - 400(4) = 0 \Rightarrow M_A = 4800\ \text{N·m}
$$

### Example 1.4 — Table on a rough floor

The table rests on a floor with $\mu_s = 0.3$ and carries 500 N. Find the largest $P$ before it moves.

![Figure 1.19](/img/statics/fig-1-19.png)

*Figure 1.19 — (a) table, (b) free-body diagram with impending slip.*

At the maximum, slipping impends at both legs, so $F = 0.3N$ at A and B. Unknowns: $P, N_A, N_B$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum M_B = 0&:\quad -N_A(6) + 500(3) + P\cos 30^\circ\,(2) = 0 \\
\sum F_x = 0&:\quad 0.3N_A + 0.3N_B - P\cos 30^\circ = 0 \\
\sum F_y = 0&:\quad N_A + N_B - 500 - P\sin 30^\circ = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
P = 209\ \text{N}, \qquad N_A = 310\ \text{N}, \qquad N_B = 294\ \text{N}
$$

Both normal forces are positive, so both legs stay on the floor.

## Key terms

- **Concurrent forces** — forces whose lines of action pass through one point.
- **Couple** — two equal, opposite, non-collinear forces; produces rotation only.
- **Free-body diagram** — the body drawn isolated, with every load and needed dimension.
- **Friction** — tangential resistance one surface exerts on another.
- **Method of sections** — any cut-out piece of a body in equilibrium is itself in equilibrium.
- **Parallelogram law** — add two vectors tail-to-tail; the resultant is the parallelogram's diagonal.
- **Principle of moments** — a force's moment equals the sum of its components' moments.
- **Principle of transmissibility** — a force may slide along its line of action without changing external effects.
- **Weight** — Earth's gravitational pull on a body, acting through its center of gravity.
- **Wrench** — a force plus a collinear moment: a push and a twist.
