# Cables, Connectors, and Ports

The office sketch from the last chapter drew a line between a PC and a switch, then another line out toward the internet. Those lines were placeholders. Real networks use **copper** or **glass**, a **connector** on each end, and a **port** on the device. Ethernet is the family of rules that makes most of those wired links interchangeable.

Ethernet is not “one cable.” It is a set of **standards**: physical plugs and media on one side, and rules for how bits are grouped into messages on the other.

## Why standards exist

A person speaking only English and a person who only knows Japanese do not have a conversation. They failed to share a **language**. If both know English but one writes on paper while the other keeps their eyes shut and waits for sound, they failed to share a **medium**.

Computers have the same two agreements:

- **Rules** — how to format what you send.
- **Medium** — copper voltage, glass light, or radio.

Those agreements are written down by standards bodies. The one that owns wired Ethernet is the **IEEE** (say “I triple E”). In 1983 it published **IEEE 802.3**, the Ethernet family. The same organization later defined **IEEE 802.11**, the radio LAN people call Wi-Fi — that track is later. This chapter stays on wires.

Because the rules are public, a laptop, a switch, and an ISP’s gear can all speak on the same path even when no two boxes share a vendor.

## Bits on the wire

A **bit** is a binary digit: `0` or `1`. That is the smallest piece of information a computer stores or sends. A **byte** is eight bits.

Everything a screen shows or a speaker plays is bits that some program interpreted. Two PCs on a cable are doing the same thing at a scale that is hard to picture: long runs of `0` and `1`, often **billions per second**.

On copper, those two values are two **voltage levels**. Voltage *x* means `0`. Voltage *y* means `1`. The simulator’s “send a byte” bar is that idea, not a scope trace.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-1.png" alt="A router sends one byte to a switch; two voltage levels stand for 0 and 1." caption="Figure 3.1 — A router sends one byte to a switch; two voltage levels stand for 0 and 1." maxWidth={520} />

We state link speed as **bits per second**, then scale the name:

| Name | Size |
| --- | --- |
| kilobit (kb) | 1,000 bits |
| megabit (Mb) | 1,000,000 bits |
| gigabit (Gb) | 1,000,000,000 bits |
| terabit (Tb) | 1,000,000,000,000 bits |

A **100 Mbps** link is one hundred million bits each second. A **10 Gbps** link is a thousand times that.

:::note 1,000, not 1,024
Networking uses the decimal thousands above. The binary cousins (1,024 and its powers) have other names: kibibit, mebibit, gibibit, tebibit. Exam questions about **link rate** want 1,000.
:::

How to *count* in binary, and how to convert to decimal or hex, is a later chapter. Here you only need the units and the picture of bits on a medium.

## Copper UTP

The cable most people point at and say “Ethernet cable” is **unshielded twisted pair (UTP)** copper.

The plug on the end has eight metal contacts. The accurate name is **8 position 8 contact (8P8C)** — eight wires, eight pins. People also say **RJ45**. That name is slightly wrong as a standards label and completely normal in the field.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-2.png" alt="Two 8P8C ports on a Cisco switch, and the 8P8C plug on a UTP cable." caption="Figure 3.2 — Two 8P8C ports on a Cisco switch, and the 8P8C plug on a UTP cable." maxWidth={480} />

Inside the jacket:

- **Twisted pair** — eight wires are twisted into **four pairs**. The twist cuts **electromagnetic interference (EMI)** *between* the two wires of a pair.
- **Unshielded** — there is no metal foil or braid around the pairs. A **shielded** cousin (STP) exists; it is less common, so this track talks UTP.

A pair is one electrical circuit. For 10 and 100 megabit Ethernet, only **two pairs** are busy. Gigabit and 10-gigabit copper use **all four**.

### Names for the same copper standard

Each IEEE Ethernet variant wears three name tags:

1. A **speed** name people say out loud: Ethernet, Fast Ethernet, Gigabit Ethernet, 10 Gig Ethernet.
2. A **task-group** code such as IEEE 802.3ab (you do not need to memorize the letters).
3. An **informal** name that encodes speed and media. Copper names end in **T**: `10BASE-T`, `100BASE-T`, `1000BASE-T`, `10GBASE-T`.

| Speed | Informal name | Typical category | Max length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 Mbps | 10BASE-T | Cat 3 | 100 m |
| 100 Mbps | 100BASE-T | Cat 5 | 100 m |
| 1 Gbps | 1000BASE-T | Cat 5e | 100 m |
| 10 Gbps | 10GBASE-T | Cat 6a | 100 m |

The **100 meter** cap is the number to keep. Longer copper attenuates; the far end starts guessing bits. The **category** (Cat 5, Cat 5e, Cat 6a, …) is a cable construction standard from other bodies, not from IEEE 802.3. “Ethernet cable” is a nickname for a cable Ethernet *uses*.

### Straight-through and crossover

On **10BASE-T** and **100BASE-T**, one pair is transmit and one pair is receive:

- Pins **1 and 2** are one pair.
- Pins **3 and 6** are the other (not 3 and 4).

A **straight-through** cable joins pin 1 to pin 1, 2 to 2, 3 to 3, 6 to 6. That is what you want when the two devices **disagree** about which pair is Tx:

| Device | Tx pins | Rx pins |
| --- | --- | --- |
| PC, server, router, firewall | 1–2 | 3–6 |
| Switch | 3–6 | 1–2 |

A PC to a switch works on straight-through: the PC’s 1–2 land on the switch’s 1–2, and the switch is listening there.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-3.png" alt="PC to switch on a straight-through cable: the PC transmits on 1–2, where the switch listens." caption="Figure 3.3 — PC to switch on a straight-through cable: the PC transmits on 1–2, where the switch listens." maxWidth={560} />

Two routers, two PCs, or two switches **agree** on Tx. Straight-through then aims Tx at Tx. A **crossover** cable swaps 1–2 with 3–6 so each sender lands on a listener.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-4.png" alt="Router to router on straight-through: both transmit on 1–2, so it fails." caption="Figure 3.4 — Router to router on straight-through: both transmit on 1–2, so it fails." maxWidth={560} />

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-5.png" alt="Router to router on a crossover cable: each Tx pair lands on the other Rx pair." caption="Figure 3.5 — Router to router on a crossover cable: each Tx pair lands on the other Rx pair." maxWidth={560} />

The unused pairs in the jacket just sit there on 10/100. They become live on **1000BASE-T** and **10GBASE-T**: pairs 4–5 and 7–8 join in, and each pair can send **and** receive at once. A gigabit crossover also crosses 4–5 with 7–8. You still almost never pick that cable by hand.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-7.png" alt="1000BASE-T and 10GBASE-T use all four pairs, each in both directions." caption="Figure 3.7 — 1000BASE-T and 10GBASE-T use all four pairs, each in both directions." maxWidth={560} />

### Auto MDI-X

Modern ports run **Auto MDI-X**: a device may **swap its own Tx and Rx** to match whatever pin-out you plugged in. Straight-through between two routers then still comes up.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-6.png" alt="Auto MDI-X: the right-hand router swaps its own Tx/Rx so straight-through works." caption="Figure 3.6 — Auto MDI-X: the right-hand router swaps its own Tx/Rx so straight-through works." maxWidth={560} />

Know the old pin story for the exam. In a closet full of current gear, grab a straight-through and move on.

## Fiber

Copper’s 100 m wall is why **fiber-optic** cable exists in the same building, and why it owns most links between buildings.

Fiber carries **light** down a **glass core**, not voltage down metal. The glass is more flexible than window glass and still hates a sharp bend: the core can crack, or light can leak and the signal dies.

A typical fiber Ethernet hop is **two strands**: one for transmit, one for receive. Those strands land on a modular **small form-factor pluggable (SFP)** transceiver that sits in an **SFP port**. The SFP is a separate purchase from the switch. Cross the two strands the wrong way (Tx to Tx) and you have the fiber version of a bad crossover.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-8.png" alt="An SFP transceiver in a switch port, with a spare SFP on top." caption="Figure 3.8 — An SFP transceiver in a switch port, with a spare SFP on top." maxWidth={420} />

Layers you will hear named, from the inside out: **core**, reflective **cladding**, **buffer**, **jacket**. Cladding keeps the light in the core.

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-9.png" alt="Fiber from the inside out: glass core, cladding, buffer, outer jacket." caption="Figure 3.9 — Fiber from the inside out: glass core, cladding, buffer, outer jacket." maxWidth={420} />

### Multimode and single-mode

| | Multimode (MMF) | Single-mode (SMF) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core | Wider | Very narrow |
| Source | LED, many angles (“modes”) | Laser, one angle |
| Reach | Hundreds of meters | Tens of kilometers |
| Cost | Cheaper optics | More expensive SFPs |

<LessonFigure src="/img/ccna/fig-3-10.png" alt="Multimode: an LED and many light angles. Single-mode: a laser and one angle." caption="Figure 3.10 — Multimode: an LED and many light angles. Single-mode: a laser and one angle." maxWidth={480} />

Both beat copper on distance. SMF wins when the run is a campus or a WAN span. MMF is the usual **in-building** fiber: risers, a long floor, a short courtyard.

## Which one do you pull?

| Question | Lean toward |
| --- | --- |
| Desk to the floor switch, tens of meters, RJ45 on the PC | Copper UTP |
| Same building, a few hundred meters | MMF |
| Separate buildings, kilometers | SMF |
| Motors, radios, or other EMI along the path | Fiber (light does not pick up that noise) |
| Cheap ports, and 100 m is enough | Copper |
| The PC has no SFP | Copper — the client cannot take fiber |

Fiber also **leaks less** usable signal than copper. A UTP pair can radiate a faint copy of the bits; that is a niche security worry, not the everyday reason you pick glass.

## How to choose the run

1. Look at the **ports** you actually have (8P8C versus SFP).
2. If the length is **under 100 m** and the room is electrically calm, prefer **UTP**.
3. If the length is **hundreds of meters**, prefer **MMF**.
4. If the length is **kilometers**, prefer **SMF**.
5. On old **10/100** gear without Auto MDI-X, match **straight-through** or **crossover** to the Tx pins.

A printer on the same aisle never needed glass. A router in the next town never needed Cat 5e.

## Summary

| Idea | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Standard | Shared rules and a shared medium so mixed vendors can talk |
| IEEE 802.3 | The Ethernet family for wired links |
| Bit / byte | Binary digit / eight bits |
| Link rate | Bits per second, in decimal thousands (kb, Mb, Gb, Tb) |
| UTP | Eight copper wires, four twisted pairs, no shield |
| 8P8C / RJ45 | The eight-pin plug and the jack it enters |
| 10/100BASE-T | Two pairs, 100 m, pins 1–2 and 3–6 |
| 1000/10GBASE-T | All four pairs, still 100 m on copper |
| Straight-through | Pin *n* to pin *n* — unlike devices on 10/100 |
| Crossover | 1–2 swap with 3–6 (and 4–5 with 7–8 at gigabit) |
| Auto MDI-X | A port remaps Tx/Rx so either cable works |
| MMF | LED, wide core, hundreds of meters |
| SMF | Laser, narrow core, tens of kilometers |
| SFP | Pluggable optic; two strands, Tx to the far Rx |
