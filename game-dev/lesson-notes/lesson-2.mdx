## Notes from the video — game parts, mechanics, systems, parameters

These notes summarise the lecture. Revisit them when you work on the assignment, especially 2.3.1 and 2.3.2.

### What counts as a game part?

If an object is **only** there to block the player or to decorate the scene, do not count it as a game part.

Some objects block **and** do something else. Trees in RTS games often stop armies **and** act as a resource you harvest by “attacking” them with specialised units. Those extra functions make them game parts.

### Mechanics are verbs

Mechanics are the most critical part of a game — the cogs that hold it together. They are **verbs**: active and dynamic. In a platformer, typical mechanics include running and jumping.

A mechanic can be simple or complex. That usually comes from its **input** and its **output**.

| | Meaning | Example (jump) |
|---|---|---|
| **Input** | How easy or hard it is to **activate** the mechanic | Press a button → jump a fixed height. Or hold the button to **modulate** jump length. |
| **Output** | How many things the player can **achieve** with that mechanic | Jump only moves you (hit a block and bounce; land on an enemy and take damage). Or jump **destroys** blocks and **damages** enemies. |

Tiny changes to input or output change how players perceive and use the mechanic. Richer input and output give more decisions, and — if the mechanic talks to other mechanics — more **interesting** decisions.

Players have limited **mental and physical** resources:

- **Too much input** demands a lot of skill. Souls-likes, Guitar Hero, and most competitive shooters lean this way.
- **Too much output** burns attention. Players lose track of what they are doing, how to do it, or what the mechanic even did. Many RPGs and strategy games pile on output (loads of weapons and powers you forget exist).

**Minecraft** stays approachable and still rewards dedicated players: attack is not a combo, but you still choose when to run, block, or hit. Output detail stays modest. You can see a sword’s attack value without needing a separate formula for “sword vs tree.” Logic and trial-and-error teach you that chopping a tree with a sword is a bad idea.

**Super Mario Bros.** is the classic of **simple input, huge output**. Hold to change jump length; jumping also breaks blocks and hurts enemies. Walking, sprinting, and jumping do a lot of gameplay work, so players think before they jump and feel accomplishment when they land a perfect stomp.

**General tip:** more complex mechanics create more gameplay value through dynamism with other mechanics and systems. Keep some mechanics **simple** when you do not want deep engagement. In a complex game, not every mechanic should have the same value: some must **shine**, others **support** the stars.

### Complex vs complicated

The more **exceptions** a mechanic has, the more **complicated** it is. Those words are not the same:

- **Complex** — more gameplay value because the **output** connects to other mechanics. Players get more value from the same input.
- **Complicated** — locks, many steps, or different rules depending on what the mechanic touches. More mental load and input for **less** output, and failure is harsher.

As a rule, avoid complicated mechanics. Prefer **maximising output for a single input**, not the reverse.

### Systems (preview)

A **system** is a collection of mechanics. Jumping, running, and kicking can sit in a **combat** system. Jumping, running, and grabbing ledges can sit in a **parkour** system, which can sit inside a larger **movement** system. The same mechanic can appear in more than one system. Systems get their own section later in the course.

### Parameters

Parameters are the **smallest unit** of a game part. At a high level they are adjectives: character A runs **fast**, B runs **slowly**; mage attacks are **strong** vs warriors and **weak** vs druids.

Parameters attach to game parts and **modulate** the mechanics on those parts. At a low level they are numbers, booleans, strings, tags, labels.

On your assignment cards, **the numbers are parameters**: how hard an attack hits, how much life a character has, and so on. They set the strength, type, or behaviour of the mechanic on that game part.

## 2.3.2 Modify and/or add a new mechanic

:::caution Instructor question
Take **one** mechanic from 2.3.1 and modify it **in any way you want** (do not hunt for the “perfectly fun” change). Write the modified mechanic, then test how the game feels (that is why you change only one at a time).

After that, **add a new mechanic** too. If you do not know where to start, play a [reference game from Lesson 1](./lesson-1) and steal an idea that seems cool here.

When you finish, write a bulleted list of **all** mechanics with a few paragraphs describing each.
:::

:::tip Gabriel’s tip
Playtesting is one of the best ways to see if a new or modified mechanic works. You will go deeper in the next lesson; try a play now and notice how different it feels.
:::
